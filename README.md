### Major evidentiary upgrade: Harvard training is confirmed

Daniel J. Wilson’s biography, citing a **Harvard University Archives list of courses taken by Lovejoy**, the *Harvard University Catalogue* 1896–97 (pp.73, 75, 99), and Lovejoy’s October 1896 letters, states that Lovejoy:

- studied **Sanskrit and some sacred Buddhist texts under Charles R. Lanman**;
- took **comparative religion with Charles C. Everett**, covering Vedic religion, Hinduism, Buddhism, Mazdaism, and Chinese religions;
- studied **Hebrew religion under Crawford H. Toy**;
- also took Everett’s course on the psychological basis of religious faith.

Harvard Library exposes the **1896–1897 Faculty of Arts and Sciences Courses of Instruction** in a digitized serial-level ListView collection, providing a primary route to the exact course descriptions cited by Wilson.

### Paris 1898–99: now a central research site

Wilson’s biography, explicitly citing Lovejoy’s letters to Wallace W. Lovejoy of **17 October, 1 December, and 5 December 1898**, says that Lovejoy’s Paris year was chiefly devoted to a historical approach to comparative religion. He studied Psalms with Maurice Vernes, heard Jean Réville on the Fourth Gospel, **hoped to take a course in Pāli Buddhism from Sylvain Lévi**, and, when that course was not offered, **occasionally went to Lévi’s home to read sacred texts with him**. The exact texts and languages of those private readings remain to be established from the letters.

The EPHE evidence now adds a second direct Paris strand. Its official 1898–99 report states that Lovejoy took an active part in Léon Marillier’s conferences. Marillier’s first course argued that future-life beliefs among “non-civilized” peoples begin as continuation rather than moral recompense; the second analyzed human sacrifice and ritual anthropophagy through funerary, expiatory, magical and fecundative mechanisms, with Bantu, Guinea-coast, North American and non-Aryan Indian comparisons. These are precisely the organizing problems of notebook 005. The published 1898–99 EPHE program also shows Foucher teaching Nyāya/Vedānta and Manu, Vernes teaching Psalms, and no Pāli-Buddhist course, independently fitting Wilson’s account of Lovejoy’s Paris plans.

Johns Hopkins holds **MS-0873, Daniel J. Wilson transcriptions of Arthur O. Lovejoy letters**, a one-box collection that is now the highest-value route to the 1898 Paris letters and the 1896 Harvard letters cited by Wilson.

### New institutional/network lead: Vivekananda and the Graduate Philosophical Society

Wilson records Lovejoy as a member of Harvard’s **Graduate Philosophical Society** and its secretary during his second Harvard year. Swami Vivekananda delivered **“The Vedanta Philosophy”** before that same Society on 25 March 1896; later editions were introduced by Everett, and Lanman helped revise the stenographic text. This is a concrete institutional intersection among Lovejoy, his confirmed teachers, and live Vedānta discourse. **Lovejoy’s personal attendance at the Vivekananda lecture is not yet proved**; Society minutes and Lovejoy’s March 1896 correspondence remain priority targets.

The Cambridge Conferences provide a second public comparative-religion layer. Wilson says Lovejoy attended/enjoyed them; the 1896–97 Cambridge/Evolution of Ethics orbit included talks by Swami Saradananda on Hindu ethics, Anagarika Dharmapala on Buddhist ethics, a Parsi/Zoroastrian lecture, and Chinese ethics. Exact Lovejoy attendance at those individual talks remains to be established from Conference programs, Bull/Curtis papers, and correspondence.

### Mature China strand: a different kind of Orientalism

The 1933 **“The Chinese Origin of a Romanticism”** is being treated separately from the early Buddhist philology. Close reading of the expanded 1948 version shows that its source architecture is overwhelmingly European-language: Mendoza and early China reports, Jesuit writings and translations, *Confucius Sinarum philosophus*, Kircher, Leibniz, Fénelon, Wolff, Voltaire, Temple, Le Comte, Attiret, Chambers, and European garden/aesthetic literature. Lovejoy is reconstructing how a European **representation of China** travelled and became causal evidence in arguments for irregular landscape gardening and a Romantic aesthetic. At present this is best described as a history of cross-cultural transmission/representation, not direct Sinological philology. The 1933 JEGP original still needs exact comparison with the revised 1948 version.

### 1935: Orientalist expertise becomes explicitly distributed

The Preface to *Primitivism and Related Ideas in Antiquity* provides direct primary evidence for the mature collaboration model. Lovejoy and Boas say they are especially indebted to **W. F. Albright and P.-E. Dumont** for supplementary essays extending the history of primitivism into two regions not previously given special study. Albright writes on ancient Western Asia (Mesopotamia and Israel); Dumont writes on Indian literature through Vedic, epic, and Purāṇic sources. The division of linguistic/area competence is thus made visible in the book itself. Johns Hopkins ArchivesSpace also confirms Albright as a related person in the Lovejoy papers; exact project correspondence remains to be located.

### Current provisional career shape

1. **1895–1901: direct Orientalist/comparative-religion formation and practice** — Everett/Toy/Lanman coursework; Sanskrit and Buddhist texts; notebook 004; 1898 JAOS Buddhist philology; Paris private reading with Sylvain Lévi; active participation in Marillier’s EPHE comparative-religion conferences and notebook 005; 1901 “Philosophy of Buddhism” lectures.
2. **1898–1906: transformation of comparative materials into theory** — 005’s Paris empirical/classificatory archive increasingly appears to precede by years the 1906 problem of quasi-mechanical efficacy / “primitive philosophy,” rather than being a near-draft of it.
3. **1930s: mediated/distributed global history of ideas** — Chinese transmission reconstructed chiefly through European representations, while Indian and ancient Western-Asian materials enter the primitivism project through area specialists such as P.-E. Dumont and William F. Albright.

The working thesis is that Orientalist and comparative-religion training was not merely an abandoned youthful curiosity. It was an early laboratory for operations later redistributed into history of ideas: decomposition of systems, semantic discrimination, historical layering, classification by causal mechanism, borrowing without identity, and reconstruction of intelligible arrangements. The Marillier identification now gives this methodological genealogy an institutional setting and a much firmer chronology.
