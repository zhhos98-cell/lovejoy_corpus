### Harvard and Paris: direct Orientalist/comparative-religion formation

Daniel J. Wilson, using a **Harvard University Archives list of courses taken by Lovejoy**, the *Harvard University Catalogue* 1896–97, and Lovejoy’s 1896 letters, records that Lovejoy studied **Sanskrit and sacred Buddhist texts with Charles R. Lanman**, comparative religion with **Charles C. Everett** (Vedic religion, Hinduism, Buddhism, Mazdaism, Chinese religions), and Hebrew religion with **Crawford H. Toy**. Harvard Library has digitized the relevant 1896–97 course-announcement series. The remaining linguistic question is narrower: whether Lovejoy had separately designated formal Pāli instruction and how independently he read Pāli texts.

Wilson’s account of Paris, based on Lovejoy’s letters of 17 October, 1 December, and 5 December 1898, says Lovejoy studied Psalms with Maurice Vernes, heard Jean Réville, **hoped to take Pāli Buddhism with Sylvain Lévi**, and when that course was not offered **occasionally went to Lévi’s home to read sacred texts with him**. The EPHE evidence independently adds Lovejoy’s active participation in Marillier’s comparative-religion conferences. Johns Hopkins holds **MS-0873, Daniel J. Wilson transcriptions of Arthur O. Lovejoy letters**, the highest-value next archive target for these claims.

### Indian philosophy did not disappear after the 1898 paper

The strict Indic/Buddhist publication record is small but the teaching/review record is longer. Wilson records a 1901 four-lecture syllabus, **“The Philosophy of Buddhism.”** A newly surfaced 1907 review shows Lovejoy still evaluating specialist Indian-philosophy work: he reviewed Paul Deussen’s *Outline of the Vedānta System of Philosophy, According to Shankara* in *Journal of Philosophy, Psychology and Scientific Methods* 4.1 (1907), 23–24. Wilson says Lovejoy called the translation a useful convenience for teachers of Hindu philosophy and urged study of **Rāmānuja’s Vedānta** rather than still more concentration on Śaṅkara. A primary scan of this short review is still required for exact quotation.

### The history-of-religion strand continues through at least 1908

The early religion programme now includes, among other items:

- 1902, **“Religion and the Time-Process”**;
- 1906, **“The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy”**;
- 1907, **“The Entangling Alliance of Religion and History”**;
- 1907, **“The Origins of Ethical Inwardness in Jewish Thought”**;
- 1908, **“Pragmatism and Theology”**;
- 1908, **“Religious Transition and Ethical Awakening in America”**;
- 1908, **“The Theory of a Pre-Christian Cult of Jesus.”**

Wilson explicitly characterizes Lovejoy’s early history-of-religion/history-of-philosophy writings as early essays in the historical method he later refined as the history of ideas. The current project is testing that claim from the archival side rather than assuming continuity.

### Mature Asian/global strands

The 1933 **“The Chinese Origin of a Romanticism”** is best treated, on current source analysis, as a history of cross-cultural transmission and European representations of China rather than direct Sinological philology: its source architecture is overwhelmingly European-language (Jesuit reports/translations, Mendoza, Kircher, Leibniz, Voltaire, Temple, Le Comte, Attiret, Chambers, garden/aesthetic literature). The 1935 *Primitivism and Related Ideas in Antiquity* shows a different mature mode: the Preface explicitly thanks **W. F. Albright** and **P.-E. Dumont** for supplementary essays on Ancient Western Asia and Indian literature, making the division of area/language expertise visible in the book’s architecture.

### Current provisional career shape

1. **1895–1901: direct Orientalist/comparative-religion formation and practice** — Lanman/Everett/Toy; Sanskrit and Buddhist texts; notebook 004; 1898 JAOS Buddhist philology; Lévi private reading; Marillier EPHE work; 1901 Buddhism teaching.
2. **1898–1908: sustained comparative/historical religion programme** — notebook 005 begins in the Paris/Marillier setting but is demonstrably reused by 1905; the 1906 `primitive philosophy` paper and 1907–08 Jewish/Christian/religion essays show continuing transformation rather than abrupt abandonment. The 1907 Deussen review shows Indian philosophy itself still remained in Lovejoy’s scholarly field.
3. **1930s: mediated/distributed global history of ideas** — Chinese transmission through European representations; Indian and ancient Western-Asian work increasingly organized through specialist collaboration.

The working thesis is that Orientalist and comparative-religion training was an early laboratory for operations later redistributed into history of ideas: decomposition of systems, semantic discrimination, historical layering, classification by causal mechanism, borrowing without identity, and reconstruction of intelligible arrangements. The chronology and degree of direct continuity are being tested source by source.
