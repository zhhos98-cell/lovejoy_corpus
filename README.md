### Major evidentiary upgrade: Harvard training is confirmed

Daniel J. Wilson’s biography, citing a **Harvard University Archives list of courses taken by Lovejoy**, the *Harvard University Catalogue* 1896–97 (pp.73, 75, 99), and Lovejoy’s October 1896 letters, states that Lovejoy:

- studied **Sanskrit and some sacred Buddhist texts under Charles R. Lanman**;
- took **comparative religion with Charles C. Everett**, covering Vedic religion, Hinduism, Buddhism, Mazdaism, and Chinese religions;
- studied **Hebrew religion under Crawford H. Toy**;
- also took Everett’s course on the psychological basis of religious faith.

Harvard Library exposes the **1896–1897 Faculty of Arts and Sciences Courses of Instruction** in a digitized serial-level ListView collection, providing a primary route to the exact course descriptions cited by Wilson.

### Major evidentiary upgrade: Paris continued the Orientalist line

Wilson’s biography, explicitly citing Lovejoy’s letters to Wallace W. Lovejoy of **17 October, 1 December, and 5 December 1898**, says that Lovejoy’s Paris year was chiefly devoted to a historical approach to comparative religion. He studied Psalms with Maurice Vernes, heard Jean Réville on the Fourth Gospel, **hoped to take a course in Pāli Buddhism from Sylvain Lévi**, and, when that course was not offered, **occasionally went to Lévi’s home to read sacred texts with him**. This means the direct Buddhist/Indic training line continued beyond Harvard. The exact texts and languages of those private readings remain to be established from the letters.

Independent Paris-side evidence fits the chronology: Lévi was returning from his 1897–98 India–Nepal–Japan mission just as Lovejoy arrived, and contemporary/institutional records place him at the center of Sanskrit and Indian-religion scholarship. The causal claim that Lévi’s mission explains the missing Pāli course remains an inference, not a fact.

Johns Hopkins also holds **MS-0873, Daniel J. Wilson transcriptions of Arthur O. Lovejoy letters**, a one-box collection that is now the highest-value route to the 1898 Paris letters and the 1896 Harvard letters cited by Wilson.

### New institutional/network lead: Vivekananda and the Graduate Philosophical Society

Wilson records Lovejoy as a member of Harvard’s **Graduate Philosophical Society** and its secretary during his second Harvard year. Swami Vivekananda delivered **“The Vedanta Philosophy”** before that same Society on 25 March 1896; later editions were introduced by Everett, and Lanman helped revise the stenographic text. This is a concrete institutional intersection among Lovejoy, his confirmed teachers, and live Vedānta discourse. **Lovejoy’s personal attendance at the Vivekananda lecture is not yet proved**; Society minutes and Lovejoy’s March 1896 correspondence remain priority targets.

The Cambridge Conferences provide a second public comparative-religion layer. Wilson says Lovejoy attended/enjoyed them; the 1896–97 Cambridge/Evolution of Ethics orbit included talks by Swami Saradananda on Hindu ethics, Anagarika Dharmapala on Buddhist ethics, a Parsi/Zoroastrian lecture, and Chinese ethics. Exact Lovejoy attendance at those individual talks remains to be established from Conference programs, Bull/Curtis papers, and correspondence.

### Current provisional career shape

1. **1895–1901: direct Orientalist/comparative-religion formation and practice** — Everett/Toy/Lanman coursework; Sanskrit and Buddhist texts; notebook 004; 1898 JAOS Buddhist philology; Paris private reading with Sylvain Lévi; 1901 “Philosophy of Buddhism” lectures; overlapping Harvard/Cambridge comparative-religion networks.
2. **c.1901–1906: comparative-religion transformation** — notebook 005 and the emerging problem of causal efficacy / “primitive philosophy.”
3. **1930s: distributed/global history of ideas** — Chinese transmission as a historical problem and collaboration with area specialists such as William F. Albright and Paul-Émile Dumont in the primitivism project.

The working thesis is that Orientalist philology was not merely an abandoned youthful curiosity. It appears to have been an early laboratory for operations later redistributed into comparative religion and history of ideas: decomposition of systems, semantic discrimination, historical layering, borrowing without identity, and reconstruction of intelligible arrangements.
