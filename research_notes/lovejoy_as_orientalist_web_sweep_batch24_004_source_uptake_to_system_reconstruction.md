## Core finding

The strongest methodological evidence in 004 does **not** lie in treating every first-person-sounding sentence in the notebook as Lovejoy’s own argument. Much of the opening historical discussion is demonstrably copied or closely abstracted from Garbe, Rhys Davids, Warren and other authorities. The more defensible historical object is the movement between layers:

> **source uptake → doctrinal discrimination → system reconstruction.**

Three points now make that movement directly observable.

1. At PDF p.17 Lovejoy explicitly breaks from source digest with the phrase **“To R.D.’s remark it should be added …”** and proposes a more discriminating test for a genuinely Sāṁkhyan parallel: the central issue is not simply eternity of matter/self, but the **separateness of Self and World**, the thorough-going dualism on which the Sāṁkhya doctrine of release depends.
2. At PDF p.18 the notebook rejects an initial reading of Buddhist silence as merely the **“philosophical agnosticism of a purely practical teacher.”** The refusals to answer, it argues, arise from an objection to the **form of the questions put**, while Buddhism remains a doctrine of salvation based on insight. No matching wording has yet been located in Rhys Davids or the other identified immediate sources; this should presently be classified as an **unattributed, likely Lovejoy analytical synthesis**, not as securely original prose.
3. At PDF p.20 Lovejoy states the aim of the **“present effort”**: to understand Buddhism’s general philosophical position, correlate it with the Three Characteristics, and understand its **technical working out** in `paticca-samuppāda` in relation to current Indic ideas, by carefully analysing the twelve notions and their complicated interrelation. This is an unmistakably programmatic description of the notebook’s own task.

The 1898 JAOS paper then converts this working method into a publishable claim: terms and perhaps ideas may be largely borrowed, while their **arrangement and application** are nonetheless original and characteristic; compositeness does not make the causation formula a meaningless heap.

The result is a tighter genealogy of practice than either “Lovejoy learned Buddhism” or “004 already contains unit-ideas.” What can actually be documented is a developing set of **rules for inference from texts**.

## 1. Layer 1 — source uptake: the notebook is a working digest before it is an argument

The opening of 004 contains large blocks of identifiable source-derived prose.

### Garbe 1894, direct

PDF pp.4–5 / manuscript pp.8–9 are headed `The Method of the Sankhya / Garbe p.150`. They closely reproduce Richard Garbe, _Die Sâmkhya-Philosophie_ (1894), p.150ff., on `pramāṇa`, the three sources of knowledge, and the alien/adopted logical material associated with Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika.

### Rhys Davids 1896, direct

PDF pp.12–14 contain a close digest of T. W. Rhys Davids, _Buddhism: Its History and Literature_ (1896), pp.24ff., including:

- the question whether Buddhism was based on Sāṁkhya;
- Garbe’s chronological claim and Rhys Davids’s objection that a large gap remains between extant Sāṁkhya texts and Gotama;
- the conclusion that one may infer pre-Buddhist thinkers resembling later Sāṁkhya without proving that the mature system existed as a whole.

PDF pp.14–16 then continue through the _Brahmajāla_ sixty-two heresies. Rhys Davids already supplies an important classificatory operation: these opinions need not represent mutually exclusive schools; different propositions could coexist and recombine.

This matters for historiography. **Decomposition and recombination are already present in Lovejoy’s source environment.** The claim cannot be that Lovejoy invented these operations ex nihilo in the notebook.

### Warren / Oldenberg / Childers as textual infrastructure

The notebook repeatedly works through Henry Clarke Warren’s _Buddhism in Translations_, Oldenberg, Childers and translations of the Piṭakas. The research apparatus is therefore highly mediated. Lovejoy’s practice is better described as the active manipulation of a translated/edited Orientalist archive than as autonomous Pāli philology throughout.

This does not diminish the notebook’s interest. It specifies what kind of intellectual labour we can observe: **selection, juxtaposition, supplementation, classification and reconstruction across a mediated source field.**

## 2. Layer 2 — explicit supplementation: “To R.D.’s remark it should be added”

The clean transcription of PDF p.17 provides one of the strongest authorial hinges in the notebook:

> `To R.D.’s remark it should be added ...`

Lovejoy then argues that, although eternity of Matter and Self belongs to Sāṁkhya dualism, this is not the doctrine’s most distinctive point. The central point on which its doctrine of release depends is the **separateness of Self and World — “in a word its thorough-going dualism.”** He proposes a passage in _Majjhima-Nikāya_ 63, “the soul is one thing & the body another,” as a more likely reference to something genuinely Sāṁkhyan.

This is methodologically valuable because the operation is visible at sentence level:

1. receive Rhys Davids’s historical discussion;
2. identify what Lovejoy thinks is an insufficient diagnostic feature;
3. redefine the relevant doctrinal core;
4. search the Buddhist source field for a comparison at that more specific structural level.

The question has shifted from **“are these doctrines similar?”** to **“which feature is sufficiently central and diagnostic to support a historical comparison?”**

This is a form of doctrinal discrimination. It should not yet be called a `unit-idea` method. But it is a precise antecedent practice: Lovejoy distinguishes a system’s central operative relation from less diagnostic propositions that happen to accompany it.

## 3. Layer 3 — critique of the question-form, not mere agnosticism

On PDF p.18 Lovejoy summarizes four issues on which Buddhism refuses straightforward assertion:

- infinity of the world;
- eternity of the world;
- relation of soul to body;
- future life.

He then records a striking interpretive move. At first sight this looks like the **“philosophical agnosticism of a purely practical teacher”** who treats speculative questions with contempt. But, the note continues, closer scrutiny shows that the refusal is based on an objection to the **form of the questions put**, rather than simply the impatience of a common-sense moralist; Buddhism is itself a doctrine of salvation based on insight and must identify the origin of suffering/existence before prescribing its removal.

An exact phrase search has not located this wording in Rhys Davids’s 1896 book, and the surrounding sequence marks a transition away from the Rhys-Davids digest into the notebook’s synthetic problem. Still, absence of a match is not proof of sole authorship. The evidentiary label should therefore be:

**likely Lovejoy synthesis; immediate printed source not yet identified.**

The analytical significance is clear even under that caution. Lovejoy treats a philosophical question as carrying presuppositions that may themselves be rejected. Silence or refusal is therefore not automatically evidence of the absence of theory. The historian must reconstruct **what makes the question malformed from within the system being studied.**

This is already a rule of interpretation rather than a doctrine about Buddhism.

## 4. Layer 4 — the notebook states its own analytical programme

PDF p.20 / manuscript p.71 is the cleanest methodological statement in 004. The heading changes to `Nidānas`, and the note says, in substance:

- to understand Buddhism’s **general philosophical position**, correlated with the Three Characteristics;
- then to understand the **technical working out** of that general conception in `paticca-samuppāda`;
- to read that working-out in connection with **current Indic ideas**;
- to analyse the twelve notions and their **complicated interrelation**;
- the “present effort” can only approach such understanding through careful analysis of the texts.

This passage is crucial because it tells us what the notebook itself is doing. The historical object is no longer merely a pile of quotations about Buddhism. Lovejoy explicitly organizes the task at two analytical levels:

> **general philosophical position ↔ technical articulation in an interrelated conceptual formula.**

The relation is neither purely genealogical nor purely semantic. A system has a general position, and inherited/current terms become intelligible by asking how they function in the technical realization of that position.

This helps explain why later pages can both acknowledge non-Buddhist origins of individual components and insist on the intelligibility of their Buddhist configuration.

## 5. Layer 5 — Senart creates the stress-test for system reconstruction

By PDF pp.48–52 the notebook directly engages Émile Senart’s 1896 interpretation of the twelve nidānas.

Senart’s treatment gives Lovejoy a difficult case because it combines two claims that the notebook itself partly accepts:

- terms/components are historically heterogeneous and in part inherited;
- the sequence is not homogeneous as a strict temporal series.

The disagreement concerns the inference drawn from those facts. The clean transcription at PDF p.49 says that it is false to conclude that the formula is therefore unintelligible or merely accidentally ordered; there is a **discoverable technical system** behind the arrangement. Some exact wording remains medium-confidence, so the published paper is the stronger authority for the final form of this claim.

The key point is that Lovejoy does not rescue coherence by denying historical compositeness. He tries to reconstruct coherence **after granting compositeness**.

That is the crucial analytical move.

## 6. Layer 6 — 1898 publication secures the transformation in Lovejoy’s own voice

Lovejoy’s 1898 JAOS article opens by describing `upādāna` as a pivotal link in the causation formula and proposes to establish the technical terms more coherently through examination of Piṭaka usage. The article then makes several authorial moves that can be securely separated from the notebook’s copied materials.

### A. Local semantic discrimination

Against Senart, `upādāna` is not a collective abbreviation for the five skandhas. The skandhas are the conditions/objects subject to attachment; `upādāna` is the attachment or habitual volitional relation to them.

### B. Causal discrimination

Lovejoy distinguishes two kinds of causal work:

- `karma` explains the **particular condition** into which one is reborn;
- `upādāna` explains the **fact of rebirth as such**.

A technical semantic problem is therefore solved by asking what causal role each concept must perform in the system.

### C. Reconstruction after borrowing

Lovejoy explicitly says that the terms, **perhaps even the ideas**, are doubtless largely borrowed; nevertheless their **“arrangement and application”** are original and characteristic. The causation formula is not “shreds and patches” simply because it is historically composite.

### D. General position and technical equipment

The article treats impermanence / the Three Characteristics as complementary to dependent origination and says the two formulas together constitute nearly all of Buddhism’s indispensable theoretical equipment.

This is the published version of the p.20 notebook programme: a general philosophical position is reconstructed through the functional relation among technical components.

## 7. The strongest 004-method thesis after source control

The responsible thesis is no longer:

> Lovejoy was already practicing the mature history of ideas in the 1890s.

Nor is it:

> Lovejoy independently invented decomposition while studying Buddhism.

The source-controlled claim is stronger precisely because it is narrower:

**004 records Lovejoy appropriating a contemporary comparative-philological apparatus and converting it into a practice of system reconstruction. He receives decomposed doctrines, translated texts and competing genealogies from Garbe, Rhys Davids, Warren, Oldenberg and Senart; he then increasingly discriminates among doctrinal features by their diagnostic and causal functions, asks when the form of a question misrepresents the system being studied, and reconstructs a characteristic arrangement from historically composite materials.**

The important continuity into later history of ideas is therefore best formulated as continuity in **rules of inference**:

1. **Similarity is not enough:** ask which resemblance is structurally diagnostic before inferring genealogy.
2. **Questions are not neutral:** reconstruct the presuppositions that make a question answerable or malformed within a system.
3. **Components do not define the whole:** historical borrowing does not determine the function acquired in a new configuration.
4. **Compositeness is compatible with intelligibility:** reconstruct the arrangement and causal/logical work performed by components.
5. **Systematic meaning can be distributed:** the general position may be technically articulated across several formulas and categories rather than stated in one doctrinal proposition.

These operations can later be tested against the 1902 `Religion and the Time-Process`, the 1907 Jewish-history work, and mature history-of-ideas texts without assuming terminological identity.

## 8. Relation to notebook 005

This also sharpens the contrast between the two notebooks.

### 004

The dominant transformation is:

> **source digest → doctrinal discrimination → semantic/causal function → system reconstruction.**

Its main problems are genealogy, conceptual composition, question-form and intelligibility.

### 005

The dominant transformation reconstructed elsewhere is:

> **dossier circulation → source criticism → determinant/mechanism → reclassification.**

Its main problems are contamination, differential outcomes, ritual mechanism and causal efficacy.

The relation between the two should not be forced into one primitive `method`. Together they show two different workshops in which Lovejoy learned to move from heterogeneous evidence to analytical distinctions.
