## Published argument, pending direct primary ingestion

Daniel J. Wilson’s annotated bibliography quotes Lovejoy’s central contrast: “primitive” actors seek not primarily friendly relations with supernatural beings but well-adjusted **quasi-mechanical** relations with a source of supernatural energy, so as to control it for individual/group benefit.

A modern source-critical history of the `mana` category reports exact Lovejoy page-level formulations:

- “impersonal, quasi mechanical … and communicable” (pp.359, 362)
- “according to quite regular and intelligible laws” (pp.379, 381)
- `mana` as “the efficacious, the powerful, the productive” (p.380)
- Lovejoy’s proposed names “primitive energetics” / “manitouism” (pp.381–382)

These quotations must be checked against the primary scan before use in publication, but they make the p.105 notebook overlap especially significant.

## Reception discovered

### James H. Leuba, 1912

*A Psychological Study of Religion* discusses Lovejoy’s criticism of Marett, quotes the “regular and intelligible laws” formulation and calls attention to Lovejoy’s term “Primitive Energetics.” This establishes immediate reception in psychology/religion theory within six years of publication.

### C. G. Jung

Jung repeatedly cites Lovejoy’s 1906 paper in later discussions of `mana`/primitive energy. Digitized editions and bibliographies preserve references especially to pp.361, 363, 365, and 380ff. Jung uses Lovejoy as an authority for `wakonda/manitou/mulungu` as power/energy, while later criticizing the abstraction implied by calling such material a modern “concept.”

This creates a notable downstream genealogy:

**Lovejoy’s comparative-religion intervention → early psychology of religion → Jungian energetic vocabulary.**

It should be reconstructed carefully by editions/dates rather than asserted as simple direct intellectual influence.

## Buddhist reception: additional confirmation

The 1898 `upādāna/upādisesa` paper remains a live bibliographic item in modern Buddhist scholarship. Search results include:

- a comparative study of the *Majjhima-nikāya* quoting Lovejoy’s p.129 definition of `upādāna`;
- Korean Buddhist scholarship on early Buddhist metaphors / `upādi`, citing Lovejoy alongside Oldenberg, Masefield, Norman and later nibbāna scholarship;
- Indian-philosophy bibliographies placing Lovejoy’s article early in the Abhidharma secondary literature chronology.

Thus the strict Orientalist episode has a small but real specialist afterlife, while the 1906 comparative-religion paper seems to have had a broader theoretical afterlife.

## Negative findings preserved

## Current methodological model after batch 02

The 1898 and 1906 cases now suggest two related but distinct operations:

1. **004 → 1898:** technical philology decomposes a Buddhist causal formula into historically/functionally distinguishable terms and reconstructs an intelligible arrangement.
2. **005 → 1906 (working hypothesis):** comparative ritual/ethnographic notes decompose visible practices by causal mechanism, especially personal-spiritual agency versus intrinsic/quasi-mechanical efficacy, yielding a generalized theory of “primitive energetics.”

If the full 1906 source concordance confirms this, early Lovejoy can be reconstructed not merely as a temporary Buddhist specialist but as someone using Orientalist/comparative-religion source practices to build a more general theory of how conceptual systems are decomposed and recombined.
