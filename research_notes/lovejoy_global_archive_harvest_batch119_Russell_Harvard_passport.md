# Archive-side Batch 119 — Russell's Harvard appointment, passport refusal, and the split between message control and mobility control

Date: 2026-08-19  
Status: synced  
Scope: follow-up to Batch118's reconstruction of the intercepted 30 July 1916 Russell-to-Woods letter. This pass asks a different question: how far can the separate British decision preventing Russell from taking up his Harvard teaching be reconstructed, and what does that do to the surrounding Lovejoy/Johns Hopkins comparison?

## Core result

Batch119 adds **no new GLA component and no new GAL lead**. The next unassigned true component remains `GLA0057`; the next unassigned collection lead remains `GAL0047`.

The strongest result is a correction in object type and mechanism. The 1916 transatlantic Russell/Woods episode contains at least two distinct forms of state control:

1. **message-transmission control** — the 30 July Russell -> Woods letter, reconstructed in Batch118 through BRACERS `3598 / 57120` and interception records `122538-122539`;
2. **person-mobility control** — the refusal of permission/passport for Russell to travel to Harvard, reconstructed here through BRACERS `122554`, `122555`, `122592`, Harvard's published curriculum and cancellation notices, and the parliamentary dispute over the decision.

Those mechanisms belong to the same historical environment but they are not the same administrative act. Treating them separately makes the documentary network substantially cleaner.

A second result is equally important: Russell's Harvard appointment was not merely an informal invitation visible in private correspondence. By **24 February 1916**, Harvard's provisional 1916-17 course announcement publicly listed Russell for `Philosophy 9c: Social Psychology and Ethics` and `Philosophy 21: Advanced Logic`. By **12 April 1916**, the final announcement publicly assigned him `Philosophy 4a: Contemporary Ethical and Social Problems` for the second half-year; Advanced Logic remained in the philosophy curriculum. On **22 September 1916**, the *Harvard Crimson* reported that British restrictions prevented him from coming and that he therefore could not fulfil the engagement until after the war.

That sequence moves the Harvard episode from `private_offer_only` to `publicly_institutionalized_teaching_plan`, while still leaving the exact Corporation/faculty appointment instrument to be recovered.

## 1. Private offer and public curriculum now form one institutional sequence

BRACERS `3597` remains the private-correspondence anchor. Its Notes entry says Woods offered Russell a half-year position beginning in February 1917 and proposed teaching in:

- symbolic logic or theory of knowledge;
- ethics with reference to social and international applications.

The Harvard public record now sharpens that offer.

### 24 February 1916 — provisional course announcement

The *Harvard Crimson* reports the provisional Faculty of Arts and Sciences programme for 1916-17 and names Russell as instructor for:

- `Philosophy 9c hf. — Social Psychology and Ethics`;
- `Philosophy 21 hf. — Advanced Logic`.

This is significant because it is not retrospective publicity after the passport controversy. It predates the summer interception episode and the public September cancellation by months.

### 12 April 1916 — final course announcement

The final announcement changes the socially oriented course. Russell is now explicitly listed for:

- `Philosophy 4a hf. — Contemporary Ethical and Social Problems`, second half-year.

The article also says `Philosophy 21 hf., Advanced Logic, with the Elements of Logical Symbolism` remains in the final curriculum and will be open only to graduates. The excerpt does not re-state Russell as its instructor, so this project does **not** silently carry the February attribution into the final April item. The official catalogue is the correct place to settle that detail.

The public curriculum and BRACERS `3597` align closely in structure — a technical logic/knowledge component plus a social/ethical component — but they should not be treated as verbatim manifestations of one fixed contract. Course titles and curricular organization changed between provisional and final publication.

Evidence rule added:

> **A published university curriculum can prove that a proposed appointment had entered institutional teaching plans without, by itself, identifying the formal appointment instrument, governance vote, or exact contractual terms.**

## 2. The passport refusal has its own administrative chain

BRACERS Notes expose a cluster of Home Office-derived records that must be separated from the censorship/interception cluster used in Batch118.

### `122554`

Secretary Sir Edward Grey's office communicates to Mr Secretary Samuel that information has been received that Bertrand Russell is planning to travel to the United States.

This is an interdepartmental notice of contemplated movement.

### `122555`

Samuel's office replies that Russell had been found guilty at the Mansion House Police Court and states that in Samuel's opinion **no passport should be issued**.

This is stronger than generic surveillance context but the wording still matters: the public Notes describe an official **opinion/recommendation**, not yet the final external notification.

### `122592`

A later BRACERS entry states:

`Mr Russell has been convicted under the Defence of the Realm Act for writing an undesirable pamphlet, and no passport will be issued to him to proceed to the United States. Please inform the President of Harvard.`

At the catalogue-description level, this is the cleanest bridge from British administrative decision to Harvard as institution. It records the refusal as operative and explicitly attaches a notification instruction to the President of Harvard.

Together the three records produce a provisional state-control chain:

`information about contemplated U.S. travel (122554)`  
`-> Home Office no-passport position (122555)`  
`-> operative refusal + Harvard notification instruction (122592)`.

Literal dates, exact senders/recipients, forms and modern TNA item numbers remain pending. The order above is a functional reconstruction from the catalogue descriptions, not yet a fully dated file sequence.

## 3. Harvard's September announcement independently confirms the outcome

The *Harvard Crimson* of **22 September 1916** reports that Russell, who was to lecture during the second half-year, had been refused permission by his government to come to the United States and therefore could not fulfil his Harvard engagement until the war was over.

This matters because it is an independent recipient/institution-side outcome report. The state-control chain is therefore attested from both sides:

- British administrative-copy catalogue: no passport / inform Harvard President;
- Harvard public record: government restriction means Russell cannot come.

The combination is stronger than relying on later biography alone.

It also supplies a terminus: by 22 September the non-arrival decision was public knowledge at Harvard.

---

## 4. Foakes-Jackson and the Cecil/Samuel exchange show the blocked appointment generating political feedback

BRACERS `122550` records F. J. Foakes-Jackson writing from New York on behalf of Russell's Harvard appointment. He says there is resentment that a major scholar is being kept out of the United States because of his political views and argues that allowing Russell to come would counter the impression that England is intolerant.

`122551` records Samuel's answer in the Cecil correspondence. Samuel rejects the argument and says, in substance, that someone convicted of gross misrepresentation and attempts to create disaffection should not be allowed to proceed to a neutral country to conduct similar propaganda.

This pair is useful because it documents feedback generated by the blocked appointment rather than simply the initial refusal. The Harvard case became a political/reputational object moving back into British elite correspondence.

Again, the project keeps levels separate:

- Foakes-Jackson's claim that the episode was producing American resentment is advocacy testimony;
- Samuel's claim that Russell would use America for propaganda is an official rationale;
- neither is treated as neutral description of Russell's actual intentions.

## 5. Hansard reveals a procedural contradiction that must remain unresolved

The parliamentary record is especially useful because it shows that even contemporaries disputed **how** the refusal had been produced.

On 28 November 1916, Trevelyan quotes Herbert Samuel's earlier 19 October statement: Samuel had said Russell was asked whether, if permitted to go to America, he would undertake to lecture at Harvard and refrain from anti-British propaganda, and that Russell refused.

Trevelyan then flatly disputes that account. He says Russell was never asked for such an undertaking, never himself asked for a passport, and that Harvard sought permission for him to travel but was refused by the Foreign Office.

Other speakers continue to dispute the government's treatment while agreeing on the underlying outcome: the Harvard engagement had been blocked by British state action.

The evidence model therefore has to split two claims:

### secure outcome

`Russell's Harvard journey/engagement was prevented by British passport/permission policy.`

This is supported by:

- BRACERS `122555`;
- BRACERS `122592`;
- Harvard public reporting of 22 Sep;
- Hansard debate.

### unresolved procedure

`Russell personally applied for a passport and/or was asked for an undertaking and refused it.`

This is **not settled**. Hansard contains a direct contemporary contradiction and the currently exposed BRACERS Notes do not resolve who formally made the passport request or whether the alleged undertaking ever existed.

Evidence rule added:

> **When an administrative outcome is independently documented but contemporary official accounts conflict over procedure, encode the outcome as resolved and the procedure as contested until the application, undertaking, minute, or instruction itself is recovered.**

This prevents a familiar biographical shorthand — `Russell applied for a passport and was refused` — from outrunning the surviving evidence currently in hand.

## 6. Harvard appointment evidence is now stronger than a single Woods letter

Before this pass, the Harvard appointment branch rested mainly on correspondence descriptions such as BRACERS `3597` and later accounts of the passport refusal.

Batch119 now produces a three-layer institutional stack:

1. **private proposal** — Woods -> Russell (`3597`);
2. **public curricular institutionalization** — Harvard provisional and final course announcements, Feb-Apr 1916;
3. **public cancellation/non-arrival** — Harvard report, 22 Sep 1916.

This is useful for archival retrieval because it generates new target record classes at Harvard beyond Woods personal papers:

- Faculty of Arts and Sciences course/curriculum records;
- President and Fellows / Corporation appointment minutes;
- President's incoming/outgoing correspondence around the British notification;
- Philosophy Department appointment and course-planning files;
- 1916-17 official catalogue and corrections/supplements.

These are retrieval targets, not yet new `GAL` entities. Harvard HUG 1880.2xx and Houghton MS Am 2693 are already registered through the Woods side; institutional governance records should receive separate collection entities only after a stable fonds/series is identified.

## 7. Relation to Lovejoy and GLA0030

The Lovejoy anchor remains `GLA0030 / BRACERS 121122` at Johns Hopkins.

That record says Lovejoy, acting through the Department of Philosophy, wanted Russell to lecture at Hopkins on technical philosophy but opposed a lecture on war because Russell's views were of an `ultra-pacifist character`.

Batch119 makes the comparison with Harvard sharper:

### Harvard

Russell's proposed role contained both:

- technical philosophy / advanced logic;
- ethics, social psychology, or contemporary ethical/social problems.

The British state then prevented the transatlantic journey itself.

### Johns Hopkins

The surviving Lovejoy document suggests an internal disciplinary/political sorting:

- technical philosophy acceptable;
- war lecture unacceptable to Lovejoy.

These are parallel institutional filters, not one coordinated system. There is still no evidence that:

- Lovejoy knew the details of Harvard's appointment;
- Lovejoy knew of the intercepted Woods letter;
- Lovejoy participated in the passport refusal;
- Hopkins and Harvard coordinated their Russell decisions.

The value of the comparison is structural: the same scholar's possible American academic mobility was being separated into `technical philosophy` and `war/social-political speech` in more than one institutional setting, while the British state could additionally control the physical possibility of reaching the American institution.

That is a documentary pattern worth preserving without turning it into a causal claim.

## 8. Message control and mobility control must remain separate

Batch118 and Batch119 together now justify a two-channel model:

### Channel A — communication object

`Russell -> Woods, 30 Jul 1916`  
`3598 ~ 57120`  
`interception records 122538-122539`  
`result: letter stopped / non-delivery`.

### Channel B — embodied academic travel

`Harvard appointment/curriculum`  
`122554 -> 122555 -> 122592`  
`result: Russell cannot travel to take up the engagement`.

The channels interact historically because both concern Russell, Woods/Harvard and wartime state control, but they are analytically different:

- one governs a **document in transit**;
- one governs a **person in transit**.

This distinction should now propagate into future archive graphs.

Evidence rule added:

> **Do not collapse censorship of correspondence, passport refusal, prohibited-area orders, prosecution, and institutional appointment decisions into a generic category of 'suppression'. They are different administrative technologies with different documents, custodial paths, decision-makers, and evidentiary burdens.**
