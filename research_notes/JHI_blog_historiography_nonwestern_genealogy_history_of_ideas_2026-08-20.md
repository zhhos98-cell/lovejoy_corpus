# JHI Blog historiography map — non-Western genealogy of the history of ideas

Date: 2026-08-20
Status: pre-pitch historiographical positioning

## Core result

The strongest historiographical gap is **not** simply `Lovejoy needs reassessment`, `global intellectual history needs non-Western cases`, or `Skinnerian contextualism should be provincialized`. All three already have substantial literatures.

The sharper opening is geographical and disciplinary:

> **The standard genealogy of Anglophone intellectual history remains largely intra-European/American: histories of philosophy and philology are followed by Lovejoy's institutionalization of the history of ideas, then by Skinner/Pocock/Dunn and the contextualist/new-intellectual-history critique. Global intellectual history has greatly widened the field's objects, actors, routes, and conceptual worlds, but has less often rewritten the disciplinary genealogy itself. Notebook 004 offers an unusually concrete case in which one canonical founder's own pre-disciplinary working practice is embedded in Indian philosophical materials and in a transnational Indological/Buddhist controversy.**

This should not be inflated into `Buddhism caused the history of ideas`, `India invented Lovejoy's method`, or even `Lovejoy learned contextualism from Indology`. The secure intervention is that **the working prehistory of Lovejoy's historical practice cannot be reconstructed solely inside the Western philosophical canon**.

A concise formulation:

> **Indian philosophy was not only a later object to which intellectual history expanded. It was already one of the sites in which the future founder of the history of ideas practiced historical reconstruction before that discipline acquired its name.**

## 1. The established genealogy of the history of ideas is still predominantly Western

### Classic disciplinary genealogy

Standard summaries derive the history of ideas from European history of philosophy / rhetoric / Enlightenment historiography and then narrate a twentieth-century line through Lovejoy and the later contextualist critique. Fania Oz-Salzberger's overview, for example, moves from Brucker and Vico through European history of philosophy; Leo Catana explicitly describes the history of ideas as growing out of eighteenth/nineteenth-century history of philosophy; Elías Palti's 2024 book again opens with `From the History of Ideas to the New Intellectual History` and narrates the move from Lovejoy to Pocock/Skinner.

The canonical sequence remains roughly:

`European history of philosophy → Lovejoy/history of ideas → Skinner/Pocock/Dunn/new intellectual history`.

### Lovejoy revisionism has not substantially changed this geography

- Anthony Grafton (2006), `The History of Ideas: Precept and Practice, 1950–2000 and Beyond`, reconstructs the institutional and methodological fortunes of the JHI/history-of-ideas project.
- Darrin McMahon (2014), `The Return of the History of Ideas?`, argues that Skinner and Darnton did not do justice to Lovejoy and that parts of the older history of ideas deserve recovery.
- Daniel Wickberg (2014) rereads Lovejoy as cultural history rather than simply ahistorical abstraction.
- Leo Catana (2010) argues that Lovejoy's grand 1936 methodological declarations were partly disciplinary rhetoric and that his actual practice remained continuous with nineteenth-century general histories of philosophy.
- Nico Mouton (2023) defends Lovejoy's long-range approach against common caricatures.

These are important controls for the Lovejoy/Skinner frame, but their main question is `what was Lovejoy's actual method and how fairly was he criticized?` Their genealogy remains overwhelmingly internal to European/American histories of philosophy and intellectual history.

**Our project should not compete with this literature by claiming merely to rehabilitate Lovejoy.**

## 2. Global intellectual history has changed the geography of objects and circulation

The 2013 Moyn/Sartori volume and subsequent work established global intellectual history as a major field. Its recurrent concerns include transnational circulation, translation, appropriation, global concepts, and the conditions under which intellectual exchange takes place.

Andrew Sartori's synthetic account (2015) describes globally oriented intellectual history as insisting on:

1. diverse intellectual worlds and genealogies;
2. transregional connections;
3. sophisticated comparison;
4. the irreducibility of global ideas to Western provenance.

Shruti Kapila (2014) goes further by treating India as **methodologically instructive** for major political concepts, not simply as a site where Western concepts are received. Her Indian case challenges circulation/dialogue as the default metaphors and foregrounds conflict and rupture.

Janaki Bakhle's `Putting Global Intellectual History in Its Place` is especially relevant. She objects to global histories in which ideas originate in Europe and their globalization is equated with local indigenization. She writes that global intellectual history must confront hidden assumptions about where authentically global ideas originate and the direction in which they travel; the aim is not simply to reverse the flow but to move beyond unidirectional influence models.

Sanjay Subrahmanyam similarly criticized `Global Intellectual History` for remaining too tied to Hegel/Marx and modern Western theoretical agendas.

### Limit for our purposes

Much of this literature asks how to globalize **the object of intellectual history** once the discipline and its methodological debates are already in view.

Our case asks a different chronological question:

> **Was the prehistory of the discipline's own canonical practice already geographically wider than its later self-genealogy suggests?**

That is the distinctive opening.

## 3. The closest current move: non-Western traditions as epistemologies, not exemplars

Omnia El Shakry (2021), `Rethinking Arab Intellectual History`, states the problem exceptionally clearly. Despite global intellectual history, she argues, the field has not fully confronted its geohistorical/geopolitical location; Western European traditions remain central to the canon, while the non-West often appears as an afterthought, producing **exemplars but rarely epistemologies**. She asks how Arab intellectual history might alter the contours of intellectual-history debates themselves.

This is probably the single best historiographical sentence against which to position the Lovejoy case.

### Iqtidar 2026

Humeira Iqtidar's chapter `Meaning, Context, Interpretations in the Islamic Tradition: Provincialising Skinnerian Contextualism` is now the most immediate current comparator. She argues that context and intention have played central roles in Islamic political/juridical interpretation for centuries and that practices only expected of Anglophone historians of ideas from the 1970s had long been standard among Islamic scholars.

This directly provincializes the chronology of contextualism.

**Our move must be different.** We should not claim that Buddhist/Indian scholarship had `Skinnerian contextualism` first. Instead:

> **Iqtidar provincializes a methodological chronology by recovering analogous interpretive practices outside the West. Our archive provincializes a disciplinary genealogy by showing that Lovejoy himself, before the history of ideas existed as a named program, was working inside a technical field devoted to Indian philosophy.**

### Ganeri

Jonardon Ganeri's `Contextualism in the Study of Indian Intellectual Cultures` asks what happens when Skinnerian methods are applied to Sanskrit philosophical culture; points of breakdown clarify the distinctive contours of Indian intellectual history and require methodological innovation.

Again, his direction is roughly:

`Skinnerian method → test in Indian intellectual culture → methodological modification`.

Our chronology reverses the temporal arrangement:

`pre-history-of-ideas Lovejoy → Indian philosophical controversy → later disciplinary codification`.

No causal claim is required.

### Jenco

Leigh Jenco's work on Chinese thought makes a still more ambitious epistemic argument: marginalized non-Western thought should not merely be included as an object but should become something that **disciplines our own thought**. Her work on the `legitimacy of Chinese philosophy` also shows how disciplinary categories themselves structure and constrain what can count as knowledge.

This supports a larger question for our ending: not simply `why did intellectual history omit Buddhism?`, but `what happens to the discipline's genealogy when a canonical founder's own non-Western scholarly practice is restored to it?`

## 4. A second historiography makes the Lovejoy case much more plausible: philology and the making of the modern humanities

James Turner's `Philology: The Forgotten Origins of the Modern Humanities` and the wider history-of-humanities literature have shown that modern humanities disciplines emerged from a porous nineteenth-century philological matrix rather than from sealed disciplinary ancestors.

Devin Griffiths's 2017 `The Comparative Method and the History of the Modern Humanities` is especially useful. He argues that comparative philology was one of the principal fields that raised the comparative method to prominence and provided models for anthropology, sociology, mythology, folklore, history, and comparative literature. Across these fields, comparativism repeatedly confronted a tension between **genetic/historical** and **functionalist/contextual** explanations and developed ways of discriminating different axes of similarity and difference.

This means the methodological problems we have found around Sāṃkhya/Buddhism are not isolated oddities. They belong to a much larger nineteenth-century comparative-humanities ecology.

### India/Sanskrit are not incidental to that story

Thomas Trautmann's `Aryans and British India` stresses the role of India and Sanskrit in the development of comparative philology and ethnology, and the interactions of British Sanskritists with Indian teachers. Ian Stewart's 2023 work on Sir William Jones revises the European intellectual-history story of Indo-European scholarship by recovering a British/Scottish tradition self-consciously built on Jones's Calcutta work.

Current comparative histories of philology explicitly reject the assumption that philology is exclusively a European phenomenon; Oxford/Princeton/Columbia's `Comparative History of Philology in Early Modern Asia` project treats Sanskrit and Chinese knowledge systems as global intellectual history without making Europe the standard of comparison.

### The missing connection

The history-of-humanities literature says:

`comparative philology / Oriental scholarship / Sanskrit → formative for modern humanities`.

The history-of-ideas literature says:

`history of philosophy → Lovejoy → history of ideas → Skinner/new intellectual history`.

**The two genealogies are rarely connected through Lovejoy himself.**

Notebook 004 gives us an archival bridge: an early Lovejoy operating inside precisely the kind of Sanskrit/Pāli comparative-philological ecology that the history-of-humanities literature identifies as formative for modern humanistic reasoning.

This is likely the most original historiographical slot.

## 5. JHI Blog itself is already primed for this intervention

The Blog's 2021 essay `Decolonize Intellectual History!` stated that intellectual history in its `general form` had largely remained white intellectual history, with non-white intellectual history often ghettoized in area studies, even as the last decade brought major expansion into India, China, Japan, Islamicate, African and other intellectual histories.

The Blog's earlier discussions of global intellectual history likewise worried about one-way reception histories in which ideas emanate from Europe and the non-West becomes a receptacle.

The 2026 British Academy Skinner volume now makes the methodological genealogy itself current again, especially Iqtidar's `Provincialising Skinnerian Contextualism`.

Thus our proposed essay can sit at the intersection of two current JHI conversations:

1. the continuing reassessment of the Lovejoy/Skinner genealogy;
2. the decolonizing/global expansion of intellectual history beyond Western objects and methods.

But it should contribute a **documentary third move**:

> **globalize/provincialize the disciplinary prehistory itself.**

## 6. What we can legitimately claim

### Strong and documentable

- One of Lovejoy's earliest sustained historical-philosophical projects concerned Buddhism and Sāṃkhya.
- Notebook 004 places him in actor-recognized controversies over the twelve nidānas and the historical relation of Buddhism to Sāṃkhya.
- Parts of this notebook pass directly into his 1898 JAOS article.
- This work took place before `history of ideas` became his named disciplinary program.
- The later canonical image of Lovejoy is disproportionately built from mature work on Western philosophical/literary traditions and from the methodological controversy with Skinner.
- The conventional disciplinary genealogy of intellectual history/history of ideas remains largely intra-European/American.

### Strong historiographical inference

> **The working prehistory of Lovejoy's historical practice is geographically wider than the later disciplinary genealogy through which intellectual historians usually encounter him.**

> **Indian philosophy was not merely a later object to which intellectual history expanded; it was already one of the concrete intellectual fields in which its future canonical founder practiced historical reconstruction.**

### Do not claim

- Buddhism or India `originated` the history of ideas.
- Lovejoy's mature method was caused by Buddhist studies.
- Indian scholars directly transmitted a method to Lovejoy without evidence.
- Lovejoy learned `contextualism` from Indology.
- The history of ideas has a `non-Western origin`.

The important distinction is between **genealogy of causal origin** and **geography of documented working prehistory**.

## 7. Best first-order thesis + historiographical payoff

The substantive thesis should remain close to Lovejoy's actual intervention:

> **In his 1898 Buddhist article, Lovejoy argued that a philosophical tradition could be historically composite without being merely derivative: its terms, `perhaps even the ideas`, might be borrowed while their `arrangement and application` remained `original and characteristic`.**

The larger intellectual-history intervention is then:

> **That argument emerged from Indian philosophical materials and a transnational Indological controversy. Recovering the notebook therefore does more than add Buddhism to Lovejoy's bibliography: it relocates one documented early workshop of the future history of ideas outside the Western canon through which the discipline usually narrates its own ancestry.**

This is stronger than `reassessing Lovejoy` and safer than `non-Western origin of intellectual history`.

---

## 8. Pitch-ready historiographical formulation

Possible compact version:

> `Histories of intellectual history usually move from European histories of philosophy to Lovejoy and then to the contextualist challenge associated with Skinner. Global intellectual history has transformed the geography of the field's objects, but the genealogy of its own methods and canonical figures remains more stubbornly Western. Lovejoy's notebook labelled “Sankhya + Buddhism” offers a small but concrete complication. Before the history of ideas acquired its name, one of its future founders was already reconstructing philosophical inheritance through a technical controversy over Indian thought.`

Then immediately give the first-order thesis:

> `Against readings of the Buddhist nidāna formula as derivative or accidentally composite, Lovejoy argued that terms—“perhaps even the ideas”—could be borrowed while their “arrangement and application” remained “original and characteristic.”`

Then payoff:

> `The piece uses this episode to ask not whether Buddhism was an origin of the history of ideas, but what happens to the discipline's own genealogy when one restores the non-Western scholarly fields that were already present in the working archive of a canonical founder.`

This is likely the strongest current pitch frame.

## Final judgment

The historiography is favorable. There is a crowded literature on Lovejoy rehabilitation and a crowded literature on global/decolonial intellectual history, but a much thinner overlap between:

`Lovejoy's disciplinary genealogy` × `non-Western intellectual fields` × `comparative philology/Indology as working practice`.

Notebook 004 can occupy that overlap without making an origin claim.
