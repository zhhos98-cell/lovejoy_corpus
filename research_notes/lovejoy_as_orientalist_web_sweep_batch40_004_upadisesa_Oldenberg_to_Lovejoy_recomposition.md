## Core finding

The second half of Lovejoy 1898 is even less a priority story than the first half. Oldenberg 1882 already supplies most of the **semantic direction**, much of the **canonical evidence**, the **Anāgāmin/Arahat contrast**, the **synonymy of `upādāna` and `saṁyojana`**, and even a strong **scribal/orthographic hypothesis** for `upādi/upadhi`.

Lovejoy’s increment lies elsewhere. He does three things to Oldenberg’s argument:

1. **decouples semantic judgment from etymological certainty** — Oldenberg ultimately declares `upādi` to be a Pāli spelling of `upadhi`; Lovejoy says the competing derivations are both possible and that meaning must be settled by usage;
2. **extends/repackages the textual case** — Lovejoy adds a third Sutta-Nipāta passage (`anupādisesa` as the ethical goal), and an additional “hither/further shore” comparison, while explicitly reusing Oldenberg’s deceased-monk evidence and the `saṁyojana ≈ upādāna` relation;
3. **lowers the final confidence level** — despite saying the oldest/probably most numerous texts favor Oldenberg, Lovejoy ends that the matter “cannot be regarded as finally settled” because contrary passages remain.

The best description is therefore **controlled recomposition of an inherited semantic thesis**, not independent discovery.

## 1. Oldenberg 1882 is the actual positive source Lovejoy names

Primary recovered online:

**Hermann Oldenberg, _Buddha: His Life, His Doctrine, His Order_, trans. William Hoey (London/Edinburgh: Williams and Norgate, 1882).**

Internet Archive item: `buddhalife00oldeuoft`.

The English translation’s third excursus treats:

- `Upadhi`, p.427;
- `Upadana`, p.429;
- `Upadisesa`, p.433.

Lovejoy 1898 explicitly cites this as `Oldenberg, Buddha (English tr., p. 433)` and calls the discussion admirable. This gives a direct source relation without having to infer access to the 1897 German edition.

## 2. Oldenberg already acknowledges the strongest contrary witness

Oldenberg does **not** simply impose the ethical-residue interpretation and ignore textual opposition.

He prints the Itivuttaka passage and says clearly that it supports Childers’s usual interpretation:

- `saupadisesa nibbānadhātu` = the saint while earthly life/body/sense-perceptions remain;
- `anupadisesa nibbānadhātu` = the condition after those vanish in physical death.

Oldenberg then marks the conceptual difficulty. `saupādāna/anupādāna` and `upadhi/nirupadhi` are ethical oppositions between inward bondage and freedom; Childers makes `saupadisesa/anupadisesa` a physical opposition between the inwardly free saint alive and the inwardly free saint dead. Oldenberg says that this divergence is highly surprising.

Crucially, he also says he would **not** reject the Itivuttaka merely from this structural parallelism. He turns to canonical counter-evidence. Thus the method already combines:

- acknowledgement of a contrary witness;
- system-level semantic pressure;
- primary-text control.

Lovejoy does not invent this evidentiary architecture.

## 3. Oldenberg’s positive evidence already contains most of Lovejoy’s core case

### A. Anāgāmin as `saupadisesa`

Oldenberg cites the passage in which a practitioner either attains perfect knowledge in the present life or, `sati va upādisese`, becomes an Anāgāmin. Since the Anāgāmin still carries a residual sinful condition, Oldenberg concludes that `upādisesa` here means not bodily residue but **ethical residue / residuum of impurity**.

Lovejoy 1898 uses the same basic contrast and makes it central to his first positive example: `upādisesa` distinguishes the Anāgāmin from the Arahat, which makes “skandhas remaining” a poor differentia because both have skandhas while alive.

### B. The already-deceased monk

Oldenberg cites the Vangīsa/Nigrodhakappa Sutta. A monk’s death is already known, yet the question is whether he has attained Nirvāṇa or is `saupadisesa`. Oldenberg therefore argues that `saupadisesa` cannot simply mean “the saint while physically alive”; it concerns incomplete freedom from sinful nature / continued liability to rebirth.

Lovejoy explicitly says this second example was **also cited by Oldenberg** and then reinterprets its point in path-language: was the dead monk fully free from attachment, i.e. Arahat, or only almost free, i.e. Anāgāmin?

### C. `upādāna` and `saṁyojana`

Oldenberg’s p.430 note explicitly says the two words are synonymous and notes close correspondences between the four `upādānas` and the ten `saṁyojanas`.

Lovejoy’s p.136 “hither/further shore” comparison relies on exactly this relation: the Anāgāmin has some fetters remaining; the Arahat has removed all; because `saṁyojana` is roughly synonymous with `upādāna` (he cites Oldenberg p.430), the contrast parallels `saupadisesa/anupadisesa`.

Thus even this semantic bridge is not independently generated by Lovejoy, though the specific “shore” comparison appears to be his added presentation.

## 4. The most important divergence: Oldenberg says `upādi = upadhi`; Lovejoy refuses to settle meaning by etymology

Oldenberg’s argument eventually becomes quite strong and philologically specific.

From the Sunakkhatta Suttanta poisoned-wound analogy he observes that `saupadisesa/anupadisesa` marks the presence/absence of a final remnant of danger, while the exposition substitutes `nirupadhi` in the spiritual application. He therefore treats this as evidence of identity between the `upādi` of `upādisesa` and `upadhi`.

He then makes an orthographic claim with high confidence: the problematic Pāli `upādi` is only a spelling of `upadhi`, probably peculiar to modern Pāli manuscripts. He proposes scribal assimilation to familiar forms such as `saṁghādisesa` and influence from `anupādāya`. On that reconstruction, `saupadisesa/anupadisesa` means presence/absence of a remnant of earthly sinful nature.

Lovejoy does **not** simply reproduce this philological certainty.

His published sequence is different:

- Sanskrit `upadhi` has technical meanings in Nyāya/Sāṁkhya;
- Müller/Dahlmann identify it with Pāli `upādi`, while Böhtlingk/Childers/Rhys Davids derive `upādi` from `upāda` and distinguish it from `upadhi`;
- **both derivations seem etymologically possible**;
- therefore **meaning should be settled by usage rather than by etymology**;
- Lovejoy then says he can contribute points, “relevant but not necessarily conclusive,” favoring the view that `upādi` means the same thing as `upādāna`.

This is a real change in inferential posture. Lovejoy takes Oldenberg’s semantic result (ethical residue) while declining Oldenberg’s strongest lexical-identity claim (`upādi` simply = `upadhi`). He makes usage, not derivation, do the principal work.

## 5. Lovejoy adds at least one independent textual extension

Oldenberg’s recovered 1882 excursus contains the Anāgāmin passage, deceased-monk passage, poison-residue analogy, `upādi/upadhi` orthographic argument, and `upādāna/saṁyojana` synonymy.

A full-text search of the 1882 English translation does **not** locate Lovejoy’s third Sutta-Nipāta evidence, the verse translated in 1898 as the claim that the truly wise regard `anupādisesa` as the chief thing/goal. Nor does it locate Lovejoy’s “hither/further shore” (`orapāra/parapāra`) comparison.

These should therefore be treated as **Lovejoy’s extension of the evidentiary package**, subject to the ordinary caveat that absence from OCR/full-text search is not proof that Oldenberg never discussed the passages elsewhere.

The third passage matters because Lovejoy gives it an explicitly doctrinal control: physical death cannot itself be the Buddhist summum bonum; `anupādisesa` must denote the moral condition of freedom from attachment. This argument is already present in 004 p.66/mp173 in substantially the same form, so here we can again watch notebook evidence become print.

## 6. 004 records the extension and synthesis before publication

Corrected notebook pp55–71 show the second-half argument in working form.

Among the secure signals:

- Lovejoy collects `upādāna`, `upādhi`, `āsava`, `saṁyojana` as expressions with different derivations/connotations but overlapping systematic reference to attachment;
- he works through `upādānakkhandha` and khandha passages rather than assuming a physical aggregate meaning;
- on p.66/mp173 he states that no one familiar with Buddhist modes of thought could take `anupādisesa` in the cited passage to mean physical death, because death as such cannot be Buddhism’s summum bonum;
- he concludes from the three Sutta-Nipāta occurrences that `upādisesa` refers to an ethical state, positive for the Anāgāmin with some sinful residue, negative for the Arahat whether before or after death;
- the notebook continues into Buddhaghosa/commentarial and textual-critical material rather than stopping at the confirming cases.

This means the manuscript’s value is not that it predates Oldenberg’s semantic thesis. Oldenberg is earlier. Its value is that it shows how Lovejoy **broadens, tests, and reweights** an inherited thesis before publishing it.

## 7. Lovejoy’s final sentence is more cautious than Oldenberg’s orthographic conclusion

This is the most defensible epistemic difference.

Oldenberg, after considering the contradictory Itivuttaka, eventually says he has “no scruple” in declaring `upādi` merely a Pāli spelling of `upadhi`, then explains the Itivuttaka’s physical reading as a later error/conjectural development.

Lovejoy ends differently. He says:

- oldest and probably most numerous texts point toward Oldenberg’s interpretation;
- **other passages can be cited for the usual/physical view**;
- therefore the matter **cannot be regarded as finally settled**;
- scribal errors caused by homophony among `upādi`, `upadhi`, and Sāṁkhyan `upadhi` **may** explain the discrepancy.

This is not “Lovejoy is cautious whereas Oldenberg ignores contradiction”; that would be false. Oldenberg explicitly faces the contradiction. The narrower distinction is:

> **Oldenberg ultimately resolves the contradiction through a confident orthographic/text-history hypothesis; Lovejoy retains the ethical reading as the prevailing interpretation but keeps the textual contradiction epistemically open.**

That is a meaningful difference in how much reconstruction each scholar allows the evidence to bear.

## 8. The two halves of the 1898 article now have a parallel source-transformation structure

The Hardy recovery and the Oldenberg comparison produce a very clean architecture for the whole paper.

### `upādāna` half

Upstream:
- Hardy: reproduction versus manner controlled by karma;
- Senart: `upādāna = upādānakkhandha`, composite/duplicative chain;
- Oldenberg/Jacobi: live semantic/genealogical dispute.

Lovejoy’s operation:
- convert inherited causal observations into fact/manner allocation;
- test semantic identity against Buddhist usage;
- historicize why inherited karma and specifically Buddhist `upādāna` have distinct functions.

### `upādisesa` half

Upstream:
- Oldenberg: ethical residue thesis + principal canonical evidence + `upādi=upadhi` orthographic solution;
- Childers/Itivuttaka: physical residue/saint-alive versus saint-dead reading;
- other philological authorities: competing derivations.

Lovejoy’s operation:
- retain Oldenberg’s ethical semantic direction;
- detach the conclusion from a settled etymology;
- extend the usage evidence;
- retain contradictory witness more openly;
- end with a provisional rather than fully resolved text-history explanation.

The common Lovejoyan move is therefore not “find a hidden coherent system” in the abstract. It is:

> **take an inherited scholarly proposition, separate the kinds of evidence supporting it, test its function across textual contexts, and publish a reconstruction whose confidence is calibrated to the surviving contradictions.**

## 9. Implication for the `004 → 1898 → 1902` route

Batch 39 showed one form of **historical-functional recomposition**: inherited Hardy causal material becomes a historical explanation of the different jobs performed by karma and `upādāna`.

Batch 40 shows a complementary operation: **semantic reconstruction under unresolved textual genealogy**. Lovejoy can accept the dominant functional meaning of a term while refusing to equate that meaning with a fully solved etymological/textual history.

Together these make the bridge to 1902 more precise. By 1902 Lovejoy likewise separates:

- structural/conceptual affinity from demonstrated historical borrowing;
- recurring logical motive from the named system containing it;
- current function/configuration from origin/transmission.

The continuity is a discipline of **keeping explanatory levels apart and assigning different evidence to each**, not a proto-`unit-idea` vocabulary.

## 10. Evidence status

### Secure

- Lovejoy 1898 explicitly cites Oldenberg’s 1882 English translation p.433 for the ethical-residue interpretation.
- Oldenberg 1882 explicitly acknowledges the Itivuttaka support for Childers before arguing against it from additional canonical evidence.
- Oldenberg 1882 gives the Anāgāmin and deceased-monk evidence used by Lovejoy and explicitly identifies `upādāna` and `saṁyojana` as synonymous.
- Oldenberg 1882 ultimately identifies `upādi` with `upadhi` through an orthographic/manuscript hypothesis.
- Lovejoy 1898 explicitly leaves the competing etymologies open, privileges usage over etymology, and says the matter is not finally settled.
- 004 p.66 contains the ethical-state / death-not-summum-bonum argument later printed in 1898.

### Strong but qualified

- Lovejoy’s Sutta-Nipāta v.86 and hither/further-shore comparisons appear to extend Oldenberg 1882; they were not found in full-text searches of the English translation. Treat as additions to the recovered source package, not a claim that Oldenberg never knew them elsewhere.
- The main Lovejoy-specific delta in the second half is evidentiary recomposition and calibrated uncertainty, not the semantic conclusion itself.

### Still unresolved
