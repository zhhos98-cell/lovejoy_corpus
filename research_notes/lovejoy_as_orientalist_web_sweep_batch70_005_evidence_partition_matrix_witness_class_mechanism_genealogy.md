# Batch 70 — 005 evidence partition matrix: witness → class → mechanism → genealogy across future life, sacrifice, blood, agrarian rites, cannibalism and initiation

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced
Scope: 005 only; test Batch 69’s `question-relative evidentiary license` model against the notebook as a whole rather than a handful of source-critical examples.

## Core result

The four-level model survives a notebook-wide test. Across the major 005 problem fields Lovejoy repeatedly separates four questions that synthetic comparative theories often collapse:

1. **Witness admissibility** — is the report sufficiently usable to enter the comparison?
2. **Classificatory admissibility** — can the observed act/belief be grouped with formally similar cases?
3. **Mechanism inference** — what relation actually makes the practice work: provision, propitiation, communion, sympathetic efficacy, incorporation, purification, status continuity, etc.?
4. **Genealogical/developmental inference** — does the similarity establish common origin, borrowing, survival, substitution, primitive stage or historical sequence?

The recurrent structure is asymmetric. Lovejoy often accepts a datum at levels 1–2 while withholding levels 3–4. In compressed form:

> **comparison is cheap; mechanism costs more evidence; genealogy costs most.**

This is now visible across future-life, sacrifice, blood/fetish, agricultural sacrifice, cannibalism and initiation. It is not a local trick of the Steinmetz pages.

## 1. Future life, PDF pp.16–19: a usable report does not establish moral eschatology

### Witness

Keating’s Dakota account is usable, but missionary contact and internal Christian intrusion must be considered. The report itself already contains a contact warning; Lovejoy continues to treat provenance as an evidentiary variable.

### Classification

The reported material can certainly be classified under differentiated post-mortem destinations: good/bad regions, special treatment of suicide, chastity/infanticide, manner of death, villages of the dead.

### Mechanism

Lovejoy refuses to let the presence of reward/punishment vocabulary settle the mechanism. He asks whether the distinctions arise from:

- moral desert;
- taboo or ritual interdict;
- clan/social obligation;
- manner of death;
- imported Christian moralization.

### Genealogy/development

A seemingly moral afterlife cannot automatically prove an indigenous stage of moral eschatology. Borrowing/contact remains a rival historical explanation.

**Partition:** `usable witness → valid eschatological comparison → mechanism disputed → indigenous moral genealogy HOLD`.

---

## 2. Future-life typology, pp.20–28: formal heaven/hell differentiation does not prove ethical judgment

Lovejoy repeatedly separates **destination** from **criterion of destination**. A heaven, hell, trial, bridge, separate village or special post-mortem office can exist while the determining principle remains bodily, ritual, social or naturalistic rather than ethical.

Thus:

- classificatory presence of differentiated afterlife space = YES;
- moral mechanism = requires proof of desert/reward/punishment as such;
- evolutionary placement as `moral eschatology` = cannot be inferred from spatial differentiation alone.

This is an early, clean case of the same rule later applied to sacrifice: **outward architecture does not settle inward causal principle**.

---

## 3. Sacrifice, pp.61–65: a ritual can enter the sacrifice field while containing several histories

At the opening of the sacrifice sequence Lovejoy asks that a form’s natural/social character be determined before deciding whether it is primitive or how it became sacrifice. On p.65 he says several motives are often mingled and that syncretism can combine sacrifices once distinct in character.

### Level structure

- **L1:** ethnographic rite admitted;
- **L2:** can be placed inside a sacrifice comparison;
- **L3:** food/provision, magic, constraint, propitiation, communion, etc. must be separated;
- **L4:** present composite form cannot establish a single original sacrificial type.

This is the notebook’s clearest statement that **synchronic classification is compatible with diachronic plurality**.

---

## 4. Funerary killing, pp.66, 72, 76, 118: one material act supports several mechanisms

Killing a wife, slave, attendant or messenger at death/funeral belongs descriptively to human sacrifice or mortuary killing. But Lovejoy repeatedly redistributes the mechanism:

- provision of attendants in the next life;
- communication/message-bearing;
- status continuity;
- appeasement/fear of the dead;
- communal/family obligation;
- only in some cases mystical or sacramental union.

The same visible datum therefore remains stable at L2 while moving among several L3 mechanisms. Consequently it is poor evidence for a universal L4 origin in communion.

This is perhaps the cleanest empirical demonstration of `same act ≠ same mechanism ≠ same history`.

---

## 5. Ritual killing and animal sanctity, pp.69–71: cultural rank and sacrificial meaning are separate inferences

Lovejoy rejects two shortcuts:

1. animal sacrifice does not automatically mark advancement over human sacrifice;
2. ritualized killing of a rain-maker/diviner does not automatically make the killing a sacrifice.

A rain specialist can be killed as punishment for failure rather than offered to obtain rain. Human flesh can be eaten medicinally or for spirit-related efficacy without proving sacrifice proper.

Here the partition is especially sharp:

- observed ritual killing = datum;
- classificatory placement as `sacrifice` = itself contested;
- developmental ranking from victim species = rejected;
- religious mechanism must be established independently.

So even L2 is sometimes withheld. Lovejoy does not merely weaken genealogy after accepting a category; he can reject the category when formal morphology carries too little diagnostic information.

## 6. Blood, flesh, fetish and charm, pp.82–90: identical material substances cross ontological mechanisms

The blood/body-part sequence is analytically important because the same substances recur under different relations:

- blood in circumcision → incorporation / fraternity / group membership;
- flesh/bones in foundations → protective efficacy;
- blood used for rejuvenation/curing → life-giving magical efficacy;
- eye/head/body part → transfer of intelligence/strength/power;
- fetish → efficacy through indwelling spirit;
- charm/amulet/plant/drug → efficacy can be credited to the object itself;
- blood used during epidemic/protection → ritual potency without sacrifice.

Thus `blood present` or `human body part present` has extremely low diagnostic value by itself. The material object is classificatorily cross-cutting; mechanism has to be reconstructed from the relation in which it is used.

This strongly supports a paper-level concept of **low diagnosticity of outward form/material**.

---

## 7. First fruits and agrarian rites, pp.91–99: offering-like form does not settle sacrifice class

First-fruit ceremonies can resemble sacrifice while functioning as purification, taboo, communal renewal or magical/fecundative action. Ancestor offerings, natural divinities and local cults can be layered and later identified with one another.

On pp.94–97 Lovejoy explicitly reconstructs pantheons and ritual complexes as possible superimpositions of different cult histories, and asks whether apparent similarity reflects development or borrowing.

Therefore:

- L2 class membership (`sacrifice`, `ancestor cult`, `nature cult`) is revisable;
- L3 mechanism can change through identification/syncretism;
- L4 genealogy must allow layering, borrowing and reorganization rather than a one-origin model.

---

## 8. Agriculture/domestication, pp.103–105: chronology can falsify a developmental sequence

Lovejoy tests a theory connecting agricultural sacrifice, wild animals and later domestic animal victims. He notices that the proposed sequence risks circularity if domestication is assumed both to precede agriculture and to explain a later sacrificial change.

African evidence that agriculture can precede domestication supplies a counterexample to the supposed necessary order. The point is not merely that another case exists; the **chronology attacks the warrant connecting the stages**.

This is a strong L4 operation:

> a developmental chain must be independently chronological, not reconstructed from the forms whose history it is supposed to explain.

The subsequent plant-soul discussion likewise separates a quasi-mechanical/magical fecundative mechanism from a later sacramental sacrifice of a divine representative. Formal sequence is decomposed into different explanatory mechanisms before genealogy is accepted.

---

## 9. Cannibalism, pp.106–111: ceremoniality has low diagnostic value

This block is the most explicit negative-case laboratory in 005.

Lovejoy accepts that cannibalism can be:

- customary;
- public;
- solemn;
- rule-bound;
- attached to selected body parts.

But none of those features, singly or together, proves sacramental or religious motive. Rival mechanisms include famine, revenge, affection, acquisition of qualities, sympathetic magic and propitiation.

On p.109 he also warns that uncertain testimony can be too easily converted into an evolutionary theory. On p.110 the animal-for-human substitution theory is downgraded because only a limited number of cases securely demonstrate the transition.

This yields a clear threshold rule:

> **frequency of analogy is not equivalent to frequency of demonstrated historical transition.**

---

## 10. Circumcision and initiation, pp.112–117: analogy can survive while genealogy fails

Lovejoy allows an analogy between circumcision/bodily injury and sacrifice at a very general level, but explicitly rejects the stronger proposition that circumcision is a partial human sacrifice substituted for complete sacrifice.

Whipping, cutting, scarification, seclusion and menstruation rites are redistributed among:

- incorporation;
- purification;
- fortification;
- ordeal;
- group discipline;
- puberty/status transition;
- secrecy/membership regulation.

The adult-men counterexample on p.117 breaks a proposed sacrificial explanation of ritual beating. The logic is:

`formal resemblance retained` → `general comparison retained` → `specific mechanism rejected` → `genealogical survival rejected`.

This is exactly the distinction Batch 66 found in N. W. Thomas: a comparative category may remain useful even when common origin is not licensed.

---

## 11. Sacrifice summary, p.117: distribution is used against a master theory

Lovejoy’s summary says alimentary sacrifice is widespread, expiatory/propitiatory sacrifice forms another large class, while sacramental union is comparatively exceptional.

This is not simply a new taxonomy. It is an argument about explanatory scope. A communion theory may correctly describe some cases while failing as a general theory of sacrifice.

The comparative corpus is therefore used to assign a **domain of validity** to a mechanism rather than to choose between `true` and `false` theories wholesale.

This is a particularly important bridge to later Lovejoy: ideas/explanations can be decomposed and given bounded ranges instead of treated as total systems.

---

## 12. Final selected-body-part note, p.119: explicit epistemic remainder

The closing comparative-religion note treats head/brain, heart and sexual organs through transferable efficacy, but Lovejoy also allows that a selected-part practice may be a reduced form of whole-body consumption and that the exact historical motive can be unrecoverable.

This matters because the notebook does not always resolve ambiguity by inventing another origin. Sometimes the final state is:

> **mechanism plausible; genealogy underdetermined.**

That is the strongest possible evidence that the notebook’s classificatory work is not simply a machine for producing origins.

## 14. Consequence for the paper

Batch 69’s four levels should now be treated as an empirical reconstruction of 005 rather than a heuristic imposed from outside.

A tighter article-level formulation is:

> **Lovejoy’s comparative practice in 005 did not chiefly consist in multiplying counterexamples. It partitioned evidentiary work. Reports first had to qualify as witnesses; similarities could then support a comparative class; mechanisms required a further reconstruction of relations and motives; claims of origin, borrowing, survival or developmental sequence faced the highest burden. The notebook repeatedly preserves lower-level similarities after rejecting higher-level genealogies.**

That final clause is the key. Lovejoy does not solve overgeneralization by abandoning comparison. He **keeps the comparison while withdrawing the inference**.

This is also why N. W. Thomas is such a good Marillier-era control: Thomas’s distinction between classificatory inclusion and unequal evidentiary value for a historical proposition has exactly the same logical shape without requiring a direct influence claim.
