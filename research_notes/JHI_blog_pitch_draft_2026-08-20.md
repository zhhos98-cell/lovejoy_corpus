# JHI Blog pitch draft: Arthur O. Lovejoy’s Buddhism Problem

Status: working pitch draft
Date: 2026-08-20

Dear JHI Blog Editors,

I hope this message finds you well. I’d like to propose a short piece for the *JHI Blog* and wondered if it might be a good fit.

While working through Arthur O. Lovejoy’s papers at Johns Hopkins, I came across a notebook labeled in his hand “Sankhya + Buddhism.” It records Lovejoy’s work in progress: passages copied from other scholars, summaries, questions, corrections, and his own attempts to test different contemporary interpretations.

The notebook seems closely connected to his 1898 article on *upādāna* and *upādisesa*. That article later appeared as the first item in the bibliography of his published writings appended to *Essays in the History of Ideas*. My work on this material builds on source criticism, tracing the texts Lovejoy was reading and the places where he developed his own arguments.

The 1898 article provides a helpful point of comparison. Lovejoy argues that terms, “perhaps even the ideas,” may have come from elsewhere while their “arrangement and application” could still be “original and characteristic.” The notebook allows me to trace how he worked towards this distinction and how he evaluated the evidence behind it.

I would like to write a short piece, tentatively titled **“Arthur O. Lovejoy’s Buddhism Problem.”** I am not planning to turn the piece into a technical discussion of Buddhist doctrine. I am more interested in what this episode reveals about Lovejoy before *The Great Chain of Being* became the usual starting point for discussions of his intellectual history.

The case also opens a wider question about where Lovejoy’s early intellectual formation took place. His work developed partly through encounters with Indian philosophy and comparative scholarship. This setting is less visible in later accounts of the discipline. I would like to explore this context carefully, without making a larger claim about continuity or influence than the surviving sources can support.

I am still thinking about the final emphasis. The piece could connect this episode more closely with later discussions of the genealogy of intellectual history as a discipline. It could also stay nearer to the late nineteenth-century world of Oriental philology, classical scholarship, and Harvard’s intellectual environment. I would be very interested in your thoughts on which direction would work best for this topic.

I am imagining something around 1,600–1,800 words, centered on the notebook and the 1898 article.

Thank you very much for your consideration.

Best,
Haohao Zhang
