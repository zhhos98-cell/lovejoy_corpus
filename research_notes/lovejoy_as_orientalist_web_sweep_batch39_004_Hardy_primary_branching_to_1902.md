## Core advance

The Hardy source chain is now closed at primary-text level.

R. Spence Hardy, _A Manual of Budhism, in its Modern Development_ (London: Partridge and Oakey, 1853), printed pp.394–396, directly supplies the causal architecture that Lovejoy records on notebook 004 PDF p.33 / manuscript p.97 and later stabilizes in the 1898 JAOS article.

The sequence is demonstrable:

> **Hardy 1853:** `upādāna` produces renewed existence; karma controls the manner/state/grade of that renewed existence.
>
> **004 p.33:** under `Upādāna, Relation to Karma`, Lovejoy reformulates the distinction as **fact of continued existence** versus **manner of continued existence**, explicitly citing `Hardy, p.394`.
>
> **Lovejoy 1898:** karma is the cause of the **particular condition** in which one is reborn, while `upādāna` is the cause of the **fact of rebirth in itself**.

The strongest Lovejoy-specific increment is therefore not discovery of two causal factors. It is the transformation of an inherited distinction into a **historical-functional explanation**: an older category (`karma`) retains one inherited causal job inside Buddhism, while a different category (`upādāna`) explains the specifically Buddhist problem of why rebirth continues at all and therefore how it can cease.

## 1. Hardy p.394 is stronger and more precise than the mediated quotation suggested

Direct public-domain source:

- R. Spence Hardy, _A Manual of Budhism, in its Modern Development; Translated from Singhalese MSS._ (London: Partridge and Oakey, 1853).
- Internet Archive: `https://archive.org/details/manualofbudhism00hard`
- searchable OCR: `https://archive.org/stream/manualofbudhism00hard/manualofbudhism00hard_djvu.txt`

Hardy begins the relevant paragraph by defining `upādāna` as **“the cleaving to existing objects.”** He then says that, except in arahats, two properties are involved in the post-mortem continuity problem: `upādāna` and karma. When the five khandhas dissolve, Hardy nevertheless lets `upādāna` and karma continue as explanatory factors in reproduction.

The crucial causal sequence is explicit on printed p.394:

- `upādāna` cannot but exert its power; renewed existence necessarily follows;
- destruction of `upādāna` alone secures cessation/nirvāṇa;
- **“By upadana a new existence is produced, but the manner of its operation is controlled by the karma with which it is connected.”**

Hardy then concedes a problem: some passages make `upādāna` look like the efficient cause of reproduction, others karma. But his practical division of labour remains clear. Karma appoints whether the renewed being is an insect, worm, fish, bird, beast, human, demon, deva, or brahma; on p.395, if the new existence is human, karma determines male/female, monarch/outcaste, beautiful/ugly, happy/miserable. On p.396, Hardy restates the relation by saying karma naturally produces new existence **in conjunction with `upādāna`**; neither factor possesses a mind.

This is much more than a stray sentence. Hardy already has a two-factor causal architecture, a termination condition (`destroy upādāna → cessation of being`), and a specification function for karma.

## 2. 004 does analytical work on Hardy rather than merely copying him

The corrected transcription at 004 PDF p.33 / manuscript p.97 reads, under the explicit heading **`Upādāna, Relation to Karma`**, approximately:

> may `upādāna` be called the cause of the **fact of continued existence**, and karma the cause of the **manner of continued existence?** — `Hardy, p.394`.

The grammar remains medium-confidence, but the opposition and source citation are secure.

This is an important transformation. Hardy's wording still oscillates over efficient causation: he says renewed existence is produced by `upādāna`, but later that karma produces new existence in conjunction with it. Lovejoy's notebook converts that unstable two-factor description into a clean analytical question:

> **fact** versus **manner**.

That opposition is not a quotation from Hardy. It is the notebook's way of making Hardy's descriptive material usable as a problem of explanatory allocation.

004 also confirms that Hardy is not an isolated late citation. Earlier, in the `saṅkhāra = karma` discussion, the notebook cites Hardy p.386. The Manual belongs to the working apparatus through which Lovejoy is trying to map different terms onto different positions in the causal sequence.

## 3. The best microhistory is now a branching history from a shared source

Jacobi 1898 independently quotes Hardy p.394 almost verbatim. But he uses it in a direction opposite to Lovejoy's eventual published reconstruction.

### Hardy → Jacobi: assimilation

Jacobi's problem is the relation of Buddhism to Sāṁkhya-Yoga. He takes Hardy's two-factor description as support for bringing `upādāna` toward `adṛṣṭa` / karma. He proposes that `upādāna` includes passions or sinful dispositions which allow karmic effects to operate and generate further karma. Whatever the exact semantic definition, he insists that its role parallels `adṛṣṭa` in orthodox philosophy.

Thus the shared Hardy passage becomes evidence for **systematic/genealogical assimilation**.

### Hardy → 004 → Lovejoy 1898: differentiation

Lovejoy instead turns the same passage into `fact` versus `manner`, then tests `upādāna` separately through the khandha Saṁyutta and related textual apparatus. In print, `upādāna` is attachment / the habitual identification of will and interests with the skandhas. It is therefore semantically different from karma even though both contribute to rebirth.

Thus the shared Hardy passage becomes evidence for **division of explanatory labour**.

### Oldenberg 1898: lexical-functional control against Jacobi

Oldenberg rejects Jacobi's `adṛṣṭa` construction as insufficiently grounded in Buddhist usage and rebuilds `upādāna` through Pāli lexical and contextual evidence. He explicitly distinguishes `phassa → vedanā → taṇhā → upādāna` as successive functional stages rather than synonyms.

This is a third use of the same problem field: **semantic/contextual control before genealogy**.

The strongest historical object is therefore not a priority contest but a branching source transformation:

`Hardy p.394`

→ **Jacobi:** assimilate `upādāna` to a Sāṁkhya/orthodox causal analogue

→ **Lovejoy:** differentiate `upādāna` from karma and assign separate causal jobs

with **Oldenberg** supplying an independently strong lexical-functional critique of the assimilative route.

## 4. What Lovejoy actually adds in 1898

The published paper does not merely polish the fact/manner distinction. It changes the kind of explanation being offered.

### 4.1 Semantic reconstruction

Lovejoy defines `upādāna` through its relation to the skandhas: the skandhas are objects/conditions subject to attachment; `upādāna` is the attachment, ultimately described as a chronic condition of will growing out of desire.

### 4.2 Causal allocation

The notebook's `fact/manner` opposition is stabilized as:

- karma → **particular condition** of rebirth;
- `upādāna` → **fact of rebirth in itself**.

### 4.3 Historical explanation of the allocation

This is the strongest increment beyond Hardy. Lovejoy says karma came to Buddhism with a long prehistory and an existing moral economy: good/bad acts explain better/worse future states. That older doctrine simply assumed rebirth in some form. Buddhism, however, made cessation of rebirth a summum bonum. Once continued rebirth itself became contingent, **rebirth-as-such** needed an explanatory cause distinct from the category that merely distributes future conditions. Lovejoy assigns that role to `upādāna`.

Thus he converts Hardy's practical two-factor model into a historical-systematic thesis:

> **an inherited term can preserve one old explanatory function while a new configuration requires another term to perform a different function.**

This is the most defensible Lovejoyian operation currently visible in the 004→1898 causal argument.

## 5. This revises batch 31's “functional non-equivalence” formulation

Batch 31 was right to identify functional discrimination as central to the published paper, but later primary recovery shows that it cannot be treated as a Lovejoy-specific invention.

Upstream/shared:

- Hardy already divides renewed existence from its manner/condition;
- Oldenberg explicitly differentiates adjacent nidāna links by semantic and causal role;
- Warren and Senart already made compositeness/repetition a live historical problem.

What 004 lets us see Lovejoy doing is narrower and more historical:

1. receive an inherited proposition;
2. recast it as an explicit analytic distinction;
3. test the terms separately against textual usage;
4. stabilize the distinction in print;
5. explain **historically why** the inherited and new terms perform different work in the resulting Buddhist configuration.

The operation should therefore be renamed, for this line, **historical-functional recomposition** rather than simply “functional non-equivalence.”

## 6. Connection to 1902: from local causal allocation to category history

The 1902 article, “Religion and the Time-Process,” does not repeat the Hardy problem. Its continuity with 004/1898 is at the level of explanatory architecture.

Three features matter.

### A. Lovejoy explicitly carries the 1898 Buddhist analysis forward

At the dependent-origination passage he refers readers back to his own 1898 paper. The Buddhist technical work is therefore an acknowledged foundation for the larger comparative argument, not merely a later historian's reconstructed precursor.

### B. Historical transmission is separated from conceptual configuration

When Lovejoy notes a resemblance between Aristotle and Vedānta, he refuses to infer Oriental borrowing merely from affinity; an internal Greek genealogy can explain the Greek configuration. Resemblance and transmission are therefore different historical propositions requiring different evidence.

### C. A recurrent category/motive is tracked without treating its containers as identical

The article follows a “perfectionist” presupposition/theory of worth through historically different systems and can decompose a later system, such as Royce's, into conflicting “warring categories.” A category's provenance, recurrence, logical role, and current combination are distinct analytical problems.

The continuity with Hardy→004→1898 can therefore be stated without teleology:

> **1898:** an inherited causal category is not explained merely by where it came from; one must ask what function it performs after recombination.
>
> **1902:** a recurrent philosophical category or resemblance is not explained merely by similarity or presumed transmission; one must reconstruct its genealogy and its logical role in each configuration separately.

This is a division of explanatory labour between **genealogy/transmission** and **function/configuration**. It is a much safer bridge to later history-of-ideas practice than calling 004 a proto-`unit-idea` notebook.

## 7. A source-critical correction to the access investigation

A previous batch 34 interpretation treated Wilson's shorthand `A. O. Lovejoy H[ome] L[ibrary] Group 32` as potentially a personal book/library inventory. Wilson's surrounding narrative shows this was incorrect: Lovejoy was volunteering in the Boston Children's Aid Society **Home Library program**, working with a children's reading group. `Group 32` is therefore a program/group identifier, not a catalogue of Lovejoy's books.

Batch 34 and the manual queue have been corrected. This route must not be used to infer ownership of Oldenberg, Warren, Senart, or other Indological books.

The question whether Lovejoy actually saw Oldenberg's 1897 third edition remains open and should be pursued through course records, correspondence, Lanman/HOS teaching materials, or direct borrowing/citation evidence.

## 8. Current evidentiary hierarchy

### Secure primary/source-derived

- Hardy 1853 p.394–396 directly gives the reproduction/manner distinction and karma's specification of renewed state.
- 004 p.33 explicitly cites Hardy p.394 under `Upādāna, Relation to Karma` and reformulates the issue as fact versus manner of continued existence.
- Jacobi 1898 directly quotes Hardy p.394 and uses the passage toward `adṛṣṭa`/karma assimilation.
- Oldenberg 1898 directly rejects Jacobi's assimilation and distinguishes adjacent nidāna terms through Buddhist lexical/contextual use.
- Lovejoy 1898 directly publishes the particular-condition/fact-of-rebirth distinction and supplies an explicit genealogy of karma's inherited role.
- Lovejoy 1902 explicitly cites his 1898 Buddhist paper and separates resemblance from demonstrated borrowing in comparative category history.

### Strong inference

- The published Lovejoy fact/condition distinction develops directly from the notebook's Hardy fact/manner question. The conceptual and verbal sequence is unusually tight, although exact notebook dating against every 1898 fascicle remains unresolved.
- The most useful continuity to 1902 is not a shared doctrine but a repeated division between provenance/transmission and present functional/logical role.

### Not established

- Lovejoy's direct use of Oldenberg's 1897 third-edition p.273 note;
- whether Lovejoy had read Jacobi 1898 before drafting/publishing his own article;
- exact within-1898 publication order sufficient to infer short-range response;
- that “historical-functional recomposition” was conscious method terminology for Lovejoy at this date. It is our analytic label for an observable operation.
