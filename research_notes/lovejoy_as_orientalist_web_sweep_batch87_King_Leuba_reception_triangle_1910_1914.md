# Batch 87 — King–Leuba reception triangle, 1910–1914

## Research question

After Batches 81–86, the remaining high-value problem is no longer whether Lovejoy 1906 was cited, but how the argument moved through an interacting review-and-reply ecology. This batch tests the sequence

`Lovejoy 1906 -> King 1910 -> Leuba 1910 review -> King 1911 reply -> Leuba 1912 synthesis -> Lovejoy 1914 review of Leuba`

without converting bibliographic adjacency into an unsupported transmission claim.

## Result in one sentence

By 1912 Lovejoy's 1906 article was circulating inside a **triangular reception environment** rather than a simple Lovejoy→Leuba dyad: King had made the impersonal-power problem part of a synthetic developmental account of religion; Leuba had reviewed King by 1910; King publicly replied in 1911 to reviews that he said had misunderstood his book and explicitly identified Leuba's *Psychological Bulletin* review at vol. vii, p. 301; and in 1912 Leuba placed King ch. VI and Lovejoy 1906 side by side as alternative summaries of the evidence while separately auditing Lovejoy's stronger causal thesis. The missing primary text of Leuba 1910 prevents us from saying that Lovejoy itself was an issue in that review.

## 1. King 1910: the object is now retrieval-controlled, but p. 163 remains a PRIMARY HOLD

The 1910 Macmillan volume is securely identified as Irving King, *The Development of Religion: A Study in Anthropology and Social Psychology*, 371 pp., bibliography pp. 355–361. Public-domain/full-view routes are now independently controlled through:

- Open Library, edition OL7016632M, Internet Archive identifier `developmentreli00kinggoog`;
- Wikimedia Commons / Internet Archive scan `developmentofrel00kingrich`, 402 scanned pages, public domain, with direct IA PDF route recorded in the Commons metadata;
- Google Play Books full/free edition, Google Books id `kO0XAAAAIAAJ`.

Nicolas Meylan remains a precise secondary locator for the key passage: King p. 163 is reported as concluding that impersonal power is not "in itself a religious concept" and as treating mana/manitou/orenda as a theory or point of view that may or may not be used by religion. Meylan also states that King directly referred to Lovejoy. This is strong **SECONDARY-CONTROLLED** evidence, but it is still not a substitute for a facsimile/transcription of King's p. 163 and its footnotes. The status therefore remains `SECONDARY-CONTROLLED / PRIMARY HOLD`.

## 2. Leuba 1910 p. 301 is bibliographically direct, content-level open

Irving King's own 1911 article, "Some Problems in the Science of Religion," *Harvard Theological Review* 4.1 (Jan. 1911), pp. 104–118, is decisive for the existence and reception-status of the Leuba review. King opens by saying that reviews of his recent *Development of Religion* had revealed misunderstandings of both its general point of view and specific passages, and that the article will restate the issues. His references identify two reviews: George A. Coe in *Harvard Theological Review* (July 1910) and `J. H. Leuba, Psychological Bulletin, vol. vii, p. 301.`

This gives us a secure primary chain:

`King 1910 book -> Leuba review in Psychological Bulletin VII, p.301 -> King 1911 public restatement/reply`.

What it **does not** yet give us is the content of Leuba p. 301. In particular, we cannot infer from King's reference alone that the review discussed Lovejoy, "primitive energetics," or the impersonal-power chapter. The review may have targeted King's definition of religion, his valuational theory, his treatment of magic, or some other issue. Any content-level coding of Leuba 1910 therefore remains `PRIMARY HOLD`.

## 3. Leuba 1912 makes King and Lovejoy functionally adjacent

The direct 1912 text of Leuba's *A Psychological Study of Religion* is much more informative than a citation count. In the chapter on the origin of the idea of impersonal powers, Leuba first quotes Lovejoy's objection to Marett: preanimistic belief concerns a force working according to "quite regular and intelligible laws," hence Lovejoy's proposed name "Primitive Energetics." Leuba immediately re-audits this stronger proposition, arguing that the ritual means used to bring Wakonda/Manitou/Mana into play do not show a definite and stable quantitative relation between means and effects; the power is therefore magical rather than mechanical.

Leuba then shifts from adjudicating the thesis to managing the dossier. He introduces Irving King's chapter "The Mysterious Power" as bringing together the philological and other data bearing on the subject, and the footnote states that a reader interested in the point will find a good summary of the evidence either in King ch. VI or in Lovejoy's shorter article. Later Leuba prefers the term "dynamism" to Lovejoy's "manitouism" because it signals power more transparently.

This is the strongest reception-mechanics datum of the batch. **King and Lovejoy are not assigned the same function.** In Leuba 1912:

- Lovejoy is simultaneously a **theoretical adversary** on mechanical regularity and a **usable evidence summary**;
- King is primarily an **evidence aggregator / synthetic restatement** of the impersonal-power dossier;
- Leuba's own contribution is an **adjudication plus warrant substitution**, preserving the category while changing the strength and kind of causal warrant.

The 1912 juxtaposition therefore supports the disaggregation model from Batch81: theory, category, evidence dossier, and proof procedure can travel separately.

## 4. The new mechanism: controversy-mediated recirculation

The chronology now suggests a more precise model than linear "influence":

1. **Lovejoy 1906** isolates a universal impersonal/quasi-mechanical causal category and argues for regularity/intelligibility.
2. **King 1910** incorporates the impersonal-power problem into a developmental psychology of religion. Primary p. 163 still needs capture; secondary control indicates that he separates the category from religion as such.
3. **Leuba 1910** reviews King. Existence and page locator are primary-secure; substantive content is not yet recovered.
4. **King 1911** publicly replies to reviews, explicitly identifying Leuba and saying the reviews disclosed misunderstandings of his position.
5. **Leuba 1912** puts King ch. VI and Lovejoy 1906 beside one another as evidence summaries, while directly rejecting Lovejoy's stronger mechanical-regularity claim and rebuilding the genetic argument with developmental psychology.
6. **Lovejoy 1914** reviews Leuba's 1912 book in *International Journal of Ethics* 24.2, pp. 216–220. Bibliographic primary control is secure; Daniel J. Wilson's annotated bibliography says Lovejoy attempted to correct Leuba's misconception of Lovejoy's own 1906 position, but the exact review text still needs direct capture before this is coded as a substantive primary result.

The important shift is that the relevant unit of reception is no longer the individual citation. It is an **argument packet moving through books, reviews, replies, and re-syntheses**. A review can alter what becomes salient in a source; a reply can expose which parts were contested; a later synthesis can keep the dossier while replacing its warrant. This is why the chain should be treated as a microhistory of recirculation, not a citation census.

## 6. Claims to retain / claims to withhold

### Retain

- **Reception is triangular by 1912:** Lovejoy, King, and Leuba are demonstrably in the same argumentative field, with King and Lovejoy explicitly juxtaposed by Leuba.
- **Reception is controversy-mediated:** Leuba reviewed King; King replied; Leuba's later book then treats King and Lovejoy together in the impersonal-power dossier.
- **Functional disaggregation is visible inside a single Leuba passage:** Lovejoy's theory can be criticized while his evidence summary remains useful.
- **The proof/warrant problem is becoming the durable object:** the category survives while claims about mechanical law, religious status, and historical genesis are repeatedly re-proved or weakened.

### Withhold

- Do not say Leuba learned of Lovejoy from King.
- Do not say Leuba's 1910 *Psychological Bulletin* review discusses Lovejoy until p.301 is captured.
- Do not say King simply adopted Lovejoy's "primitive energetics"; Meylan's account already shows King retains Marettian/emotional elements and gives the category a different developmental placement.
- Do not upgrade the Lovejoy 1914 self-correction episode from secondary-controlled to direct primary until pp.216–220 are transcribed.

## 8. Working formulation

A stronger formulation than "Lovejoy influenced later theories of mana" is now available:

> **Lovejoy's 1906 article entered an early psychology-of-religion controversy as a divisible argumentative resource. By 1912 its evidence dossier could be recommended after its mechanical causal thesis had been rejected, while King supplied a parallel synthesis and the review/reply circuit around King's book created an intermediate arena in which the problem itself was repeatedly reformulated. Reception proceeded by controversy-mediated recirculation and warrant substitution, not by whole-theory adoption.**

This preserves the key Batch81–83 correction while adding a social/textual mechanism for how the disaggregation happened.
