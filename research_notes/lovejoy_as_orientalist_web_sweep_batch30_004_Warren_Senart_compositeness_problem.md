## Core finding

Henry Clarke Warren’s _Buddhism in Translations_ (1896) already calls the full twelve-link formula of dependent origination **“a piece of patchwork”** made from two or more current formulas. Warren’s argument is specific: the human being appears to be brought into existence twice, first through `viññāṇa + nāmarūpa` by ignorance/karma, and again through birth by desire/attachment/karma (`bhava`). He therefore suspects historical combination and even suggests that the Buddha’s distinctive addition may have been the first two links, ignorance and karma.

Émile Senart’s 1896 paper goes further in the direction Lovejoy attacks. Through Lovejoy’s direct quotations and contemporary/later citations, Senart can be securely seen to identify `upādāna` with the collective `upādānaskandhas`, use the resulting repetition of the skandha complex as evidence against the speculative value/coherence of the formula, and speak later of a **“tyrannie des cadres et des mots.”**

This sharply revises the novelty question. Lovejoy’s contribution in 1898 is **not** the discovery that the formula is composite, repetitive, historically layered or non-strictly temporal. Warren and Senart are already there. Lovejoy’s published intervention is to challenge the move from those observations to a particular semantic identification and then to **systemic unintelligibility**.

The strongest formulation is:

> **Lovejoy accepts the historical problem of composition but changes the burden of interpretation: before repetition can prove incoherence, the apparently repeated terms must be shown to perform the same conceptual/causal function.**

His distinction of `karma` from `upādāna`, and of the skandhas from attachment to the skandhas, is therefore an argument about **functional non-equivalence inside a composite structure**.

## 1. Warren 1896: patchwork is already local Harvard orthodoxy/hypothesis

Warren’s Chapter II introductory discourse moves from the Five Groups to dependent origination. At pp.115–116 he observes that the full formula seems to bring the human being into existence twice:

- `avijjā → saṅkhāra → viññāṇa → nāmarūpa ...`;
- then again `taṇhā → upādāna → bhava → jāti`.

He therefore suspects that the formula in its present form is **a patchwork of two or more formulas current in the Buddha’s time**, later made into one. Warren then hypothesizes that the Buddha’s own addition may have been the first two propositions, because ignorance/wisdom is especially connected to the Buddha’s distinctive discipline of insight.

This is a major source-control result for 004. The notebook’s concern with duplication and composite structure does not begin with Senart alone and does not originate with Lovejoy. One of the newest Harvard Buddhist tools available to him already frames dependent origination as a historical-compositional problem.

Warren also structures the chapter in essentially the order through which 004 later works: unanswered questions → ego/non-ego → human composition → Middle Doctrine → ignorance → karma → consciousness → name-and-form → sense organs → contact → sensation → desire → attachment → existence → birth → discussion of dependent origination.

Thus Warren supplies both **textual infrastructure and an analytical scaffold**.

## 2. Senart 1896 radicalizes the repetition argument around `upādāna`

Lovejoy’s 1898 paper quotes Senart directly from the 1896 _Mélanges Charles de Harlez_ article. The crucial Senart claim is that `upādāna` is merely a reduced expression for `upādānaskandhas`, i.e. a collective designation for the five skandhas.

Lovejoy then reconstructs the consequence: if the five skandhas already occur, explicitly or implicitly, across earlier links of dependent origination, identifying `upādāna` with them means the same skandha complex appears again. Lovejoy says Senart uses this supposed repetition as a principal argument for the formula’s **derivative, composite, and practically meaningless character**.

Independent later citations confirm the same Senart passage and give the relevant page as **p.284**. They also preserve Senart’s later phrase **“la tyrannie des cadres et des mots”** at pp.294ff., a characterization of Buddhist scholastic structure that Oldenberg would subsequently discuss.

The full Senart scan still needs direct extraction before a complete point-by-point reconstruction can be canonical. But for the `upādāna` argument, Lovejoy’s article is a primary witness quoting the exact French sentence and page.

## 3. The decisive disagreement is semantic before it is historical

Lovejoy does not begin his rebuttal by denying that the formula is composite. He attacks Senart’s **semantic premise**.

Senart:

> `upādāna` = collective shorthand for the five `upādānakkhandhas`.

Lovejoy:

> the Sutta material distinguishes **the skandhas** from **the skandhas insofar as they are subject to attachment**, and distinguishes both from attachment itself.

Lovejoy’s article uses Saṁyutta-Nikāya passages to state the relation precisely: the skandhas are things subject to attachment, while passion/desire connected with them is the attachment. `Upādāna` becomes a habitual/chronic volitional identification with the skandhas, not the skandhas themselves.

Once that semantic distinction is made, the supposed repetition has changed character. The same source-field may be referenced more than once without the same **function** being repeated.

This is a key operation for the paper:

> **formal recurrence of vocabulary/category ≠ functional identity.**

The historian must discriminate role before using repetition as evidence of accidental compilation.

## 4. `karma` versus `upādāna`: causal role becomes the test of conceptual identity

Lovejoy then makes the functional distinction even more explicit by comparing `karma` and `upādāna`.

Both concern rebirth, but they do different work:

- **karma** explains the **particular condition** into which one is reborn;
- **upādāna** explains the **fact of rebirth as such**, the persistence of attachment that produces another combination of skandhas.

This is more than lexical clarification. Lovejoy uses **causal function within the whole formula** to determine the significance of a term.

The method can be stated compactly:

1. identify apparent duplication;
2. test whether the duplicated terms/categories perform the same causal role;
3. if their roles differ, repetition does not by itself demonstrate incoherence;
4. reconstruct the larger formula through these differentiated functions.

This is exactly the kind of operation that notebook p.20 describes programmatically as moving from Buddhism’s general philosophical position to the **technical working-out** of the twelve interrelated notions.

## 5. Lovejoy still grants historical layering

The 1898 article’s strongest statement comes later. Lovejoy says the terms, and **perhaps even the ideas**, are largely borrowed, but their **arrangement and application** are original and characteristic.

This is important because it keeps two questions separate:

- **genealogy/composition:** where did the terms and component ideas come from?
- **systemic function:** what explanatory work do they perform once arranged inside this Buddhist formula?

The formula can therefore be historically composite without being analytically reducible to its sources or dismissed as an accidental heap.

This does not mean Lovejoy proves the formula historically originated as a perfectly designed whole. It means he changes what counts as an adequate interpretation of a composite text: **historical derivation is insufficient until functional arrangement has also been reconstructed.**

## 6. Oldenberg provides an important contemporary control

Oldenberg’s 1897/1898 revisions belong to the same controversy. Later versions of his _Buddha_ explicitly state that he wants to show the obscurities in the causation formula are **less severe than he had previously supposed and than Senart supposed**. He also cites Senart’s language about the `tyrannie des cadres et des mots`.

This matters because Lovejoy was not alone in pushing back against a maximally disintegrative reading of the formula. The historiographical claim must therefore remain local:

- Lovejoy’s **specific semantic/causal reconstruction of `upādāna`** is his intervention;
- the wider move toward finding more coherence in dependent origination was shared in the contemporary scholarly field.

## 8. Historiographical payoff

This comparison lets the 004 argument avoid two inflated claims.

Lovejoy did **not** invent historical decomposition of Buddhist doctrine; Warren and Senart already decomposed the formula aggressively. Nor did he uniquely discover that formal compositeness can coexist with some coherence; Oldenberg was also revising his own earlier skepticism.

What the notebook/article pair lets us recover much more precisely is a **change in the unit of proof**:

> from counting repeated/historically borrowed components to distinguishing the functions those components perform in an arrangement.

That is the strongest documentary bridge toward later Lovejoyan practice. It is not yet `unit-ideas`; it is an inferential discipline for deciding when two apparently similar components should or should not be treated as the same historical/conceptual unit.
