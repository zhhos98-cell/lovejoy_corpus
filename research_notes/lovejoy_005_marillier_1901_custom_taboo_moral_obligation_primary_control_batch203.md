# Batch 203 — Lovejoy 005 ↔ Marillier 1901 `Notes sur la coutume, le tabou et l’obligation morale`: primary-text control and originality correction

Date: 2026-08-26
Status: synced / PRIMARY-TEXT UPGRADE / ATTRIBUTION CORRECTION
Scope lock: **MS38_004_001_061_005 and the immediate Marillier/EPHE context only.** Ordinary forward tracing remains bounded at 1910 unless Lovejoy explicitly returns to Buddhism / India / Sanskrit-related material.

## Core result

Earlier repository passes had bibliographically identified Léon Marillier’s 1901 *Notes sur la coutume, le tabou et l’obligation morale* but had not located a directly searchable primary text. Batch31 therefore treated its substantive argument only through later relays and explicitly left primary collation as an immediate next action.

That gap is now closed at the proposition level.

A directly indexed public-domain scan reproduces Marillier’s text at least across printed pp. 412–415. The relevant passages show that Marillier himself explicitly distinguished:

1. **automatic / quasi-causal consequences of taboo violation** that require neither intervention by society nor punishment by gods;
2. **group-danger / clan-solidarity consequences** for which a violator may be struck or excluded without the act thereby becoming morally odious;
3. **moral culpability**, marked by a different affective and normative structure, especially remorse / consciousness of fault;
4. the historical transformation of a once-intelligible taboo into **custom**, after its rationale is forgotten and the rule is obeyed categorically and without motive.

This is extremely close to the classificatory grammar visible in Lovejoy 005 pp.16–20, especially the treatment of suicide, ritual interdiction, clan solidarity, social/religious exclusion, and the distinction between these mechanisms and strictly moral desert.

The originality allocation therefore requires correction.

> **The move from ritual or quasi-mechanical consequence → clan/social consequence → moral obligation cannot safely be treated as a specifically Lovejoyan innovation in notebook 005. Marillier himself formulates substantially the same distinctions in print in 1901, while Mauss’s 1896 use of Marillier’s taboo dossier shows that the underlying problem-structure was already public before Lovejoy’s documented 1898–1899 participation in Marillier’s EPHE conference.**

Lovejoy’s strongest distinctive operation in this early sequence now lies elsewhere: in repeatedly applying the mechanism-sensitive distinction across eschatology, suicide, sacrifice, blood, cannibalism, agriculture and ritual efficacy, and then abstracting one recurrent mechanism into the general theory of impersonal, transferable efficacy published as `Primitive Energetics` in 1906.

## 2. Marillier p.412: taboo can punish without society or gods

Marillier first describes taboo as an especially visible type of rule demanding immediate and complete obedience. He then distinguishes the **source of the consequence** from any external act of judgment.

For serious violations, he writes that the consequences often require intervention from **neither the social body nor the gods**. They are not ordinarily penalties decreed by an authority external to the tabooed being/object/act. Instead they are effects said to result immediately from the imprudent person’s contact with the dangerous supernatural force.

Operationally:

`taboo violation`

→ `contact with force`

→ `immediate consequence`

rather than necessarily:

`transgression`

→ `judge / deity / community evaluates guilt`

→ `punishment`.

This directly supports the repository’s use of `mechanism before morality` for 005. It also aligns with Marillier’s 1900 `Religion`, where magical sacrifice works through mechanical constraint rather than psychological/moral exchange.

**Relation code:** `P-METHOD / CAUSAL-MECHANISM ≠ MORAL-SANCTION`.

---

## 3. Marillier p.413: clan solidarity is explicitly non-identical with moral blame

This is the strongest new control.

Marillier says that while the reasons making a taboo dangerous remain consciously understood, the violator may be regarded as clumsy, rash, or extraordinarily powerful. His act is blamed **only insofar as, through the solidarity of the clan, it may bring harmful consequences upon the group to which he belongs**.

Crucially, Marillier immediately denies the inference from group reaction to moral odiousness:

- the act itself does not thereby acquire an odious character;
- the violator may be struck in order to ward off danger;
- the action is precautionary rather than vengeance motivated by hatred;
- the deliberate taboo-breaker does not experience the anguish of remorse;
- he is anxious about the consequences of the act.

This is not merely a generic statement that primitive societies are collective. It is an explicit analytical separation:

`collective danger / clan solidarity`

≠

`moral culpability / odious act / remorse`.

**Relation code:** `P-METHOD / CLAN-SOLIDARITY ≠ MORAL-GUILT`.

## 4. Direct comparison with Lovejoy 005 p.18: suicide and clan solidarity

The corrected 005 transcription gives the following proposition for PDF p.18 / manuscript p.35:

> `Reason for placing suicide among deaths leading to punishment is not necessarily moral. It may indicate social hypotheses or obligations. Criminality of suicide may arise from solidarity of clan: whether one kills another or oneself, one strikes at the clan through one of its members. Punishment is exclusion from social and religious rights/connections; the suicide is already outside the clan and cannot bring blood into immediate contact.`

The correspondence with Marillier p.413 is unusually close at the level of analytic structure:

| Marillier 1901 | Lovejoy 005 p.18 |
|---|---|
| taboo violator may endanger group through `solidarité de clan` | suicide may be criminal because of `solidarity of clan` |
| reaction can be protective rather than vengeance | punishment need not express moral desert |
| group consequence does not make the act intrinsically odious | suicide is not necessarily classed as moral guilt |
| distinction depends on mechanism producing danger | Lovejoy tests social obligation, exclusion, blood/contact and afterlife placement separately |

This requires a correction to the earlier provisional claim in `MS38_004_001_061_005_Marillier_method_to_Lovejoy.md` that suicide as a clan-solidarity/social-injury mechanism had no located direct Marillier parallel.

A direct parallel is now available.

What remains unproved is **textual borrowing**. The 1901 printed work postdates Lovejoy’s documented 1898–1899 seminar participation and Lovejoy’s later statement that the conclusions behind the 1906 paper were reached in 1900. The proper inference is therefore methodological/pedagogical proximity, not `Lovejoy copied Marillier 1901`.

## 5. Lovejoy 005 pp.16–20 now reads less like a private invention and more like active seminar reworking

The clean transcription records the following sequence:

### PDF p.16 / ms p.31

- the supernatural can be an intermediary to the moral;
- destiny may be determined by manner of death, ritual/manual acts, and social rather than moral elements;
- missionary influence must be separated from earlier evidence.

### PDF p.17 / ms p.33

- apparent moral change may arise naturally or through missionary influence;
- violation of custom/taboo/ritual interdict is **not itself equivalent to violation of a moral order**.

### PDF p.18 / ms p.35

- suicide punishment is not necessarily moral;
- criminality may arise from `solidarity of clan`;
- punishment may be exclusion from social/religious rights and connections;
- blood/contact enters as a separate mechanism.

### PDF p.19 / ms p.37

- later moral conceptions may infiltrate from a higher cultural stage or through contact;
- missionary chronology is a source-critical control.

### PDF p.20 / ms p.39

- Lovejoy explicitly calls some forms `half-moral`;
- the afterlife becomes moral only when fate/punishment/reward depends on **desert**.

The notebook is still analytically active. But the relevant originality should now be located in **reorganization, application and cross-domain use**, not in the first invention of the ritual/social/moral distinctions themselves.

## 6. Marillier pp.413–414: taboo becomes moral obligation only by becoming custom

Marillier’s next step is even more important for the historical model.

He states that so long as a taboo has not transformed into custom, it does not possess the character of a moral obligation. When that character appears, the taboo has consolidated into an instinctive and irrational rule: its original reason is no longer understood and the rule is obeyed without motive.

At p.414 he makes the conclusion explicit: taboo is a categorical imperative **insofar as it is custom**, and only in that respect; it is categorical for the same reasons as other customs. Marillier therefore rejects attempts to derive the whole origin of obligatory morality solely from ritual prohibitions or from one specific element such as the sacred.

This adds a historical-layering operation:

`originally intelligible taboo / causal rationale`

→ `rationale fades`

→ `rule survives`

→ `custom / categorical obedience`

→ `appearance or experience of moral obligation`.

That structure strongly recalls Marillier’s earlier EPHE rule that institutions, rites and legends may survive the beliefs that produced them. It also helps explain why 005 repeatedly refuses to read a present explanatory label directly back into the origin of a practice.

**Relation code:** `P-HISTORICAL-LAYERING / RATIONALE LOSS → CUSTOM → OBLIGATION`.

## 7. Important limit: 1901 is an authorial control, not a demonstrated source Lovejoy read

Chronology matters.

Marillier’s *Notes...* appeared in 1901. Lovejoy’s 1906 article says that his conclusions were reached in **1900** and were strongly influenced by his studies under Marillier. Notebook 005 also contains the internal slip `Marillier — Survivance — 12 June` and a second Marillier memorandum.

Therefore Batch203 does **not** establish:

`Marillier 1901 publication` → `Lovejoy reads it` → `Lovejoy copies it into 005`.

The stronger and safer relation is:

`Marillier/EPHE research and teaching before/during Lovejoy’s Paris period`

→ `Lovejoy 005 works inside the same classificatory problem field`

→ `Marillier 1901 supplies Marillier’s own near-contemporary printed formulation of distinctions closely matching the notebook`.

The 1901 text is thus a **primary authorial control on the Marillier side**.

## 8. The pre-1901 chronology is independently controlled by Mauss 1896

The problem-structure did not suddenly appear in Marillier’s 1901 publication.

Marcel Mauss’s 1896 critique of Steinmetz explicitly directs readers to Marillier’s 1896 `Tabou mélanésien`, pp.53–56, as a gathered Melanesian source dossier. In the same argument Mauss distinguishes several sanction mechanisms:

1. ritual prohibitions may have no sanction because obedience is effectively instinctive;
2. transgression may bring a **magical punishment** through the supernatural efficacy of the interdict itself;
3. taboo violation may acquire a **penal/social** character when public danger calls forth diffuse group reaction.

Mauss also states that a taboo violator can endanger the existence of the clan and that the supernatural/ritual mechanism must be analytically separated from public reaction.

This matters for priority. By 1896 — before Lovejoy’s documented Paris participation — the Marillier/Steinmetz/Mauss controversy was already publicly decomposing sanction into magical, social-penal, ritual and moral-juridical levels.

Batch203 therefore strengthens, rather than creates, the chain established in Batch32:

> **Marillier 1893–94 survivance/justice problem**
> → **Marillier 1896 taboo dossier**
> → **Mauss 1896 public mechanism/sanction decomposition**
> → **Marillier 1898–99 EPHE seminar resumes Steinmetz; Lovejoy is an active auditor**
> → **Lovejoy 005 pp.16–20 applies the distinctions to eschatology, suicide and contact/source problems**
> → **Marillier 1900 `Religion` sharpens mechanical vs psychological/moral efficacy**
> → **Marillier 1901 `Notes...` gives Marillier’s own explicit custom/taboo/clan-solidarity/moral-obligation formulation**
> → **Lovejoy 1906 abstracts impersonal transferable efficacy as `Primitive Energetics`.**

## 9. Revised originality allocation

### No longer safe as specifically Lovejoyan

The following cannot now bear an originality claim merely because they are visible in 005:

- ritual/taboo consequence ≠ moral guilt;
- group or clan danger ≠ individual moral desert;
- clan solidarity as a mechanism of sanction;
- automatic/supernatural efficacy independent of gods or social judgment;
- historical survival of a rule after its original rationale has faded;
- movement from ritual/customary mechanism toward experienced moral obligation.

All have strong Marillier/Marillier-field controls.

### Still plausibly distinctive in Lovejoy’s notebook practice

1. **Cross-domain reapplication.** Lovejoy runs essentially the same burden-of-proof test through future life, suicide, sacrifice, cannibalism, blood, charms, initiation and agriculture.
2. **Diagnostic use of borderline cases.** Suicide is not merely classified; it is used to distinguish social injury, taboo, contact, exclusion and later moralization.
3. **Coupling mechanism classification to source criticism.** Missionary/Hindu/Buddhist contamination and contact chronology are used to explain why a later report may display a different normative structure.
4. **Integration with eschatological morphology.** 005 attempts to map how differentiated destinations, judgment and trials can arise without immediately assuming moral desert.
5. **1906 abstraction.** The most secure Lovejoyan transformation remains the move from many local mechanism distinctions to a general comparative category of impersonal, diffused, transferable, controllable efficacy.

The current strong formulation is therefore:

> **Lovejoy did not need to invent mechanism-sensitive decomposition in isolation. Notebook 005 shows him working inside a Marillier/EPHE analytical environment where ritual danger, clan solidarity, custom and moral obligation were already distinguished. His distinctive early move is better located in the repeated cross-domain application of that grammar and in the later abstraction of transferable efficacy into a general theory.**

## 10. Consequence for the larger 004/005 argument

This correction is useful because it prevents a teleological history in which every subtle distinction in Lovejoy’s notebooks is retrospectively claimed as a private anticipation of `unit-ideas`.

The more historically precise model is asymmetric:

### 004

Lovejoy’s Buddhist work can be followed closely into the 1898 JAOS article. The high-value operations concern source comparison, borrowed terms/ideas versus their arrangement/application, semantic usage rather than etymology, causal versus temporal relations, and explicit evidentiary limits.

### 005

The notebook records a comparative-religion workshop whose decomposition of mechanisms is substantially embedded in Marillier’s EPHE method and controversy environment. Lovejoy’s own transformation is visible in what he does **with** that method across diverse cases and in the later conceptual abstraction.

The two notebooks therefore should not be forced into a single-origin story. They preserve different early research problems and different kinds of methodological learning.
