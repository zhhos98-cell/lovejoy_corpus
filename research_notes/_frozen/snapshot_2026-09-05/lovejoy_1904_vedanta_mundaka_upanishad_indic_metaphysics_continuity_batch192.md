This materially strengthens the pre-1905 continuity chain. The Indic/Buddhist work does not disappear after the 1898 JAOS article or become merely biographical background. By 1904 Lovejoy is still capable of mobilizing Indian philosophical materials inside a technical argument about the logical structure of monistic ontology.

## 1. Printed p.147: Vedānta is not ornamental; it does analytical work

Lovejoy has just distinguished two questions that are often collapsed in talk of divine `immanence` and `transcendence`:

1. whether the **being / esse / “that”** of finite things is included in the Absolute;
2. whether the **determinate nature / content / “what”** of finite things is included in the Absolute.

He then needs an example of a system that answers these two questions differently.

His chosen example is **Vedānta**.

Lovejoy writes that in `the monism of the Vedānta`, when the sage of the **Mundaka Upanishad** says of the universe, `All this is Brahman`, this does not mean that the determinations making the universe what it is are properties of Brahman, because Brahman is `that which has no properties` (`avarnam` in the OCR witness).

The example therefore performs a precise logical function:

`all finite being is included in Brahman`

while

`finite determinations are excluded from Brahman's nature`.

Vedānta is being used to make Lovejoy's `that / what` distinction concrete before he turns to Bradley and then to the historical Neo-Platonic sequence.

Primary control: Lovejoy, `The Dialectic of Bruno and Spinoza`, printed p.147.

---

## 2. Schopenhauer is explicitly classified as `modern Vedāntism`

Immediately after the Muṇḍaka example, Lovejoy says the same structure is found in **the modern Vedāntism of Schopenhauer**:

- everything that is, is Will;
- the undivided Will is nevertheless none of the determinate things that appear.

This is stronger than a casual analogy. Lovejoy places ancient/Indian Vedānta and a modern European metaphysician into the same structural class for the purposes of his argument.

The operation is:

`historically different systems`

→ abstract a common logical relation between Absolute and appearance

→ use that relation as a comparative type.

This is exactly the kind of controlled cross-system comparison that matters for the project's larger question about how Lovejoy handled ideas across traditions before his mature `unit-idea` vocabulary.

Primary control: printed p.147.

---

## 3. Printed p.144: Deussen + Schopenhauer + Māyā already prepare the Indic comparison

Three pages earlier, while discussing Erdmann's reading of Spinoza, Lovejoy says that if the phenomenal multiplicity of Spinoza's attributes and modes were only a refracted appearance generated by finite intelligence, Spinoza would belong with a familiar school of ontologists whose modern representatives include **Schopenhauer and Deussen**.

He then formulates the familiar problem in Indic language: what spreads the **veil of Māyā**, producing the many-coloured phenomenal world?

This passage is not yet his positive account of Spinoza. It is part of a classificatory counterfactual: *if Erdmann's interpretation were accepted, this is the philosophical class in which Spinoza would fall*.

That distinction matters. The passage proves Lovejoy's active command of an Indic/Schopenhauer/Deussen conceptual vocabulary, but it should not be cited as Lovejoy asserting that Spinoza himself is Vedāntic.

Primary control: printed p.144.

## 4. The Wilson bibliography now provides a clean 1901–1902 bridge

Daniel J. Wilson's annotated bibliography records a Lovejoy item that is especially important when read next to the 1904 primary article:

### 1901 — `Syllabus: The Philosophy of Buddhism`

Wilson records a Washington University Association syllabus for **four lectures on Buddhism**, with the titles:

1. `The Place of Buddhism among the Philosophies of India`;
2. `Two Essential Formulas of Buddhism`;
3. continuation of the same topic;
4. `Buddhist Ethics and its Goal`.

This is direct bibliographic evidence that Lovejoy was publicly teaching Buddhism at Washington University in 1901 and was explicitly situating it among the **philosophies of India**, rather than treating it solely as a religion detached from an Indian philosophical field.

Wilson bibliography control: primary-source item 243, OCR JSON p.127.

### 1902 — `Religion and the Time-Process`

Wilson's annotation says Lovejoy traced otherworldliness back to ancient thought in **both Western and Indian civilizations**, before following its Western development through Platonism, Christianity, deism, the decline of the chain of being, and evolutionary thought.

Wilson bibliography control: primary-source item 21, OCR JSON pp.45–46.

Taken together with the direct 1904 Vedānta/Muṇḍaka passage, the sequence is no longer dependent on a single 1898 Orientalist publication.

## 5. Why the 1904 Muṇḍaka passage is especially important

The 1904 article is about Bruno and Spinoza, not Buddhism or India. That is precisely why the Indic passage is significant.

It shows that Indian philosophical material had become available to Lovejoy as part of his **working comparative vocabulary**. He can deploy Vedānta when he needs a clean example of a logical configuration, then move to Schopenhauer, Bradley, Neo-Platonism, Bruno, and Spinoza.

The best formulation is therefore not `Lovejoy remained an Orientalist` in an institutional sense. It is more exact:

> Indian philosophical materials remained cognitively and analytically available to Lovejoy after the 1898 Buddhist article and after Paris; by 1904 he could reuse them inside a technical history-of-metaphysics argument whose explicit subject was European philosophy.

That is a stronger person-specific finding than a generic claim about the circulation of Orientalist knowledge.

---

## 6. This also sharpens the project's `layering` hypothesis

The 1904 passage itself performs a layered analysis.

Lovejoy refuses to treat `immanent` / `transcendent` as a single binary. He splits the relation into at least two dimensions:

- ontological inclusion of **being**;
- inclusion of **determinate content**.

Vedānta is then used as an example where the first relation is affirmed and the second denied.

This is structurally close to the analytical habit already visible in the 1898 Buddhist work: a term or doctrine must be decomposed into the distinct relations it is doing, rather than treated as a flat label.

The connection should remain methodological, not genealogical. The 1904 text does not say that Buddhist philology generated this distinction.
