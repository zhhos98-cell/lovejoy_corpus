# Wiener–Nakamura–Wright: Buddhism and a transpacific history-of-ideas infrastructure, 1945–1973

Date: 2026-08-31  
Status: **DIRECT PRIMARY FRONTMATTER + INSTITUTIONAL PROVENANCE / VERY HIGH VALUE / MAJOR MASTER-MAP REVISION**

## Core result

A major second-generation history-of-ideas infrastructure can now be reconstructed around Hajime Nakamura's *Ways of Thinking of Eastern Peoples: India, China, Tibet, Japan* (1964), edited by JHI executive editor Philip P. Wiener with a foreword by Arthur F. Wright.

This is not merely a book on `Eastern philosophy` adjacent to JHI. Its publication history directly joins:

`JAPANESE COMPARATIVE-THOUGHT PROJECT`
→ `ROCKEFELLER TRANSLATION SUPPORT`
→ `STANFORD CROSS-DISCIPLINARY SEMINAR`
→ `COMMITTEE ON CHINESE THOUGHT`
→ `JAPANESE UNESCO TRANSLATION`
→ `EAST-WEST CENTER CONFERENCES`
→ `WIENER / JHI EDITORIAL LABOR`
→ `WRIGHT / CHINESE-CIVILIZATION NETWORK`
→ `JHI EDITORIAL GOVERNANCE`.

Buddhism is not incidental to this route. It functions as the principal cross-cultural historical medium through which the study compares selection, adaptation, and modification of ideas across India, China, Tibet, and Japan.

This is therefore a major historical bridge for the proposed Lovejoy/JHI article, but it must **not** be written as a direct transmission from Lovejoy's 1898 Buddhist studies to Nakamura/Wiener. The stronger finding is a long-term **role conversion of Buddhist material inside history-of-ideas infrastructures**.

# II. Wright's foreword: the object is explicitly anti-monolithic East/West comparison

Arthur F. Wright says the first major problem of the book is how to identify meaningfully `East` and `West` and make systematic comparisons without reproducing false antitheses.

He explicitly rejects the inherited idea of an undifferentiated:

- `Oriental mind`;
- `Oriental logic`;
- monolithic `East`;
- monolithic `West`.

Instead Nakamura distinguishes:

- Indians;
- Chinese;
- Tibetans;
- Japanese;

through their distinctive cultures and histories, while recognizing multiple traditions inside Western civilization.

The relevant historical move is therefore:

`EAST/WEST CIVILIZATIONAL MONOLITH`
→ `MULTIPLE HISTORICIZED CULTURAL UNITS`.

This should be compared cautiously with the earlier JHI movement from broad labels to discriminated components, but no methodological identity should be assumed.

---

# III. Buddhism is the historical test of selection, adaptation, and modification

Wright identifies the second problem as the general process through which peoples receive world-wide movements of thought and belief.

His model is explicitly selective rather than passive:

`INFLUENCE ARRIVES`
→ `PEOPLE SELECT AMONG INFLUENCES`
→ `ADAPT / MODIFY SELECTED ELEMENTS`
→ `CULTURAL AMALGAM`.

Buddhism supplies the long historical experiment.

It begins/evolves in India and moves successively through Central/East/Southeast Asian societies. Indians, Chinese, Tibetans, and Japanese select and adapt different elements of the evolving Buddhist tradition.

Thus Buddhism is used to reconstruct:

- borrowing;
- resistance;
- selection;
- modification;
- adaptation;
- long-continuing differences in cultural/intellectual modes.

This makes 1964 a later and much wider development of the `imported ideas / reaction` problem already visible in JHI's 1951 Chinese symposium.

# IV. The project deliberately moves beyond traditional Indology/Buddhology

Wright describes Nakamura as trained in Indian and Buddhist philosophy but moving beyond the traditional confines of Indology and Buddhology.

He says Nakamura asks not primarily whether a doctrine agrees with an absolute philosophical system but:

- why some people/societies come to hold given views;
- how behavior is affected by those views;
- under what changed conditions they change them.

Wright explicitly places the inquiry beyond traditional philosophy and describes its evidence/objects as:

- social-historical;
- psychological;
- linguistic;
- philosophical;
- literary;
- behavioral.

His summary of the method moves:

`LANGUAGE / LOGIC / JUDGMENT / INFERENCE`
→ `FORMAL PHILOSOPHICAL WRITING`
→ `LITERATURE`
→ `INDIVIDUAL + GROUP BEHAVIOR`.

This is a direct actor-level example of the wider `cultural-process` enlargement already visible in Pearce, but here applied trans-civilizationally.

---

# V. Wright's striking metaphor: Buddhism as a comparative precipitant

Wright says that in each section Buddhism is used as a kind of **chemical precipitant** to isolate indigenous habits of thinking that are most enduring/resistant to change.

This metaphor is methodologically revealing.

Buddhism functions not only as an object with an internal history, but as a **controlled cross-cultural input** whose differentiated reception is used to reveal differences among receiving societies.

Analytical structure:

`SHARED / MOBILE BUDDHIST INPUT`
+
`DIFFERENT RECEIVING HISTORIES`
→ `DIFFERENT SELECTION / ADAPTATION PATTERNS`
→ `INFER CULTURAL MODES`.

The resulting claim can be criticized for typologizing peoples/cultures, but it is not a simple stage hierarchy or passive-diffusion model.

---

# VI. Wiener 1964: the revision explicitly moves away from innate/national/racial explanation

Philip P. Wiener's Editor's Preface is crucial for the JHI history.

He records that the revised edition incorporated newer work on India, China, Tibet, and Japan and underwent major structural/bibliographic revision.

Most important, Wiener says the new edition shows a greater emphasis on:

`SOCIO-CULTURAL TRADITIONS`
+
`ENVIRONMENTAL INFLUENCES`

and less emphasis on:

`INNATE / NATIONAL / RACIAL TRAITS`.

This is a direct second-generation history-of-ideas editor participating in the revision of a comparative typological project away from inherited racial/national essentialism.

It should not be over-read as complete de-essentialization. The book still speaks of characteristic and enduring modes of thought among peoples. But the explanatory jurisdiction is explicitly being relocated.

---

# VII. Wiener explicitly says Nakamura changed his own history-of-ideas scope

At the end of his preface, Wiener writes that Nakamura's work **broadened the scope of his own interests in the history of ideas**, and calls their collaboration an intellectually formative experience.

This is one of the strongest actor-level statements now available for the internationalization of the JHI second generation.

It shows:

`JHI EDITOR WORKS ON NON-WESTERN COMPARATIVE PROJECT`
→ `EDITOR SELF-REPORTS BROADENED HISTORY-OF-IDEAS INTEREST`.

This is stronger than inferring expansion from JHI tables of contents.

# VIII. Nakamura's own methodological programme

Nakamura says the project began in 1945–46 through a Japanese Ministry of Education committee promoting comparative study of different peoples' ways of thinking.

He stresses the need for methodological clarity/scientific accuracy.

His procedure is:

1. isolate characteristic features by analyzing forms of judgment and inference;
2. avoid treating a people `in toto`;
3. use isolated features to investigate concrete cultural phenomena;
4. focus especially on how Buddhism and Indian logic moved from India into China, Tibet, and Japan;
5. ask how a universal religion/logic was adapted to native characteristics;
6. integrate comparison with Occidental thought throughout rather than as an appendix.

The central historical relation is therefore explicitly:

`INTRODUCTION`
→ `ADAPTATION`
→ `MODIFICATION`
under comparative control.

# IX. Full research/translation provenance

Nakamura's acknowledgments reconstruct an extraordinary institutional chain.

### 1945–46 — Japan

- Professor Kichinosuke Ito, University of Tokyo;
- commissioned by a Ministry of Education committee to promote comparative study of different peoples' ways of thinking;
- Nakamura submits research reports.

### 1950 — Rockefeller Foundation

- Rockefeller director Charles B. Fahs visits Tokyo;
- encourages tentative English translation on Shunsuke Tsurumi's advice;
- Rockefeller grant given to Institute for the Science of Thought, Tokyo;
- translation board formed.

### 1951–52 — Stanford University cross-disciplinary seminar

Nakamura visiting professor; faculty seminar meets every other week to discuss the translated work.

Participants listed by Nakamura include:

- John David Goheen — philosophy;
- Arthur F. Wright — history;
- Nobutaka Ike — political science;
- Bernard Joseph Siegel — anthropology;
- Bert Alfred Gerow — anthropology;
- David Shepherd Nivison — Chinese philosophy;
- Donald Herbert Davidson — philosophy;
- Thomas Carlyle Smith — history;
- Raymond K. Waters — Japanese.

This is direct evidence of an interdisciplinary collaborative editing environment.

### 1952 — Committee for Chinese Thought

After Nakamura leaves the US:

- Nivison and Waters continue editing the English manuscript;
- portions of `Chinese Ways of Thinking` translated in London;
- Arthur F. Wright edits them;
- they are **distributed by the Committee for Chinese Thought at Aspen, Colorado**.

This directly joins the Nakamura project to the same Chinese-thought research infrastructure which intersects with JHI's 1951 imported-ideas symposium and later AAS/ACLS networks.

### 1958–60 — Japanese National Commission for UNESCO

- project taken up as an early translation initiative for philosophical works by Japanese scholars;
- complete English version published in 1960.

### 1962–63 — East-West Center

- Nakamura's work becomes basis of conferences/seminars at East-West Center;
- Senior Scholars + Research Translations / Institute of Advanced Projects support revision;
- Wiener undertakes editorial revision;
- Arthur F. Wright supplies new foreword;
- Kenneth Inada/Charles A. Moore involved.

### 1964 — East-West Center Press

Revised English edition published.

This provenance is itself a history of intellectual-history infrastructure.

# XI. Important anti-teleological control

Do not narrate this as a simple triumph of contextual/global history.

The project retains potentially problematic explanatory assumptions:

- stable `ways of thinking` attached to peoples;
- claims about enduring indigenous habits;
- selection of Buddhism as a precipitant of national/cultural difference;
- broad comparisons across four civilizations.

But the project itself contains an internal revision toward:

- historical adaptation;
- socio-cultural traditions;
- environmental influences;
- multiple Asian histories;
- rejection of a single Oriental mind;
- criticism of Western claims to universality.

The historically interesting object is the **revision process**, not a clean break from Orientalism.

# XII. Relation to Lovejoy and the proposed article

The strongest relationship to Lovejoy's early Buddhist work is a change in Buddhism's historiographical role:

### Lovejoy 1898

`BUDDHIST TEXTUAL / CONCEPTUAL SYSTEM`
= object reconstructed internally through philological, semantic, classificatory, chronological and causal disputes.

### Lovejoy 1906

Asian/ethnographic materials contribute to comparative `primitive` aggregation.

### JHI 1951

`BUDDHISM AS IMPORTED IDEA`
→ Chinese rejection/syncretism/reaction.

### Nakamura/Wiener/Wright 1964

`BUDDHISM AS TRANS-CIVILIZATIONAL MOBILE INPUT`
→ comparative selection / adaptation / modification across India-China-Tibet-Japan
→ infer differentiated cultural/intellectual modes.

Thus one can write a history of **role conversion** without claiming one continuous method or direct transmission.

Potential larger claim:

> **The second generation of the JHI/history-of-ideas network did not simply add Asian examples to a Western method. Through translation seminars, area-studies committees, East-West institutions, and collaborative editing, it helped construct new analytical roles for Asian ideas as mobile, selectively received, culturally transforming historical objects.**

This requires comparison with Latin American and other regional networks before generalizing globally.
