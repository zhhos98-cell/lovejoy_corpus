# Batch 171 — Paris, Baltimore, primitivism, paper techniques, and the politics of historical inference

Date: 2026-08-23
Scope: evidence and argument development; no alteration to the locked JHI Blog article or its title.
Companion dataset: `lovejoy_history_of_ideas_club_complete_event_register_1923_1952_batch171.csv`.
Companion retrieval index: `../archive_index/lovejoy_paris_hopkins_priority_archival_targets_batch171.csv`.

## 1. The question has changed

The defensible question is not whether Lovejoy included or excluded Indian material, women, ethnography, or political history in some undifferentiated sense. It is how the same people and materials changed epistemic position when they crossed distinct institutional and material settings: the Harvard classroom, the Paris seminar, the ruled notebook, the Baltimore discussion group, the specialist essay, the editorial foreword, the university archive, and the mature disciplinary self-description.

Those settings did not agree. A Sanskritist could address a Greek class. An Indianist could present to the club before appearing as a supplementary contributor in print. A woman could develop primitivism as an independent doctoral and journal project, receive a Guggenheim Fellowship, publish a sole-authored monograph, and nevertheless acquire a male institutional foreword. An accused China specialist could address revolutionary Chinese ideas after a public anti-Communist attack. None of these counterexamples abolishes hierarchy; none permits hierarchy to be inferred merely from absence.

The methodological issue therefore concerns **what a particular kind of evidence warrants**. Institutional juxtaposition is not personal transmission; a documented talk is not its surviving text; a supplementary position is a fact about publication design, not proof of exclusion from conversation; a public accusation establishes political context, not the speaker's argument, the audience's intentions, or Lovejoy's personal defense.

## 2. A complete primary-source register, not isolated positive hits

The source is the published appendix `Speakers and Their Topics, 1923–1952`, in *Studies in Intellectual History* (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins Press, 1953), pp. 200–217, now openly available through Project MUSE: <https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/1/oa_edited_volume/chapter/2450983>.

The accompanying CSV transcribes all **178 printed date/event slots** across **30 academic years**: **175 documented speaker/topic entries**, **three explicitly unrecorded entries**, and **128 distinct printed speaker strings**. Joint presentations remain joint entries. The table preserves the academic year, date as printed, normalized date where warranted, speaker, explicit external affiliation or location, source-footnote membership inference, printed title, minutes-survival status, editorial flags, topical tags, source line, and source URL.

The source explicitly states that speakers without another printed affiliation were Johns Hopkins members when they presented. The CSV records this as institutional membership only. It does not silently convert membership into a faculty appointment, professorial rank, or departmental affiliation.

Crucial preservation controls:

- `December? 1925`, `March? 1926`, `April? 1927`, and `November or January?` remain uncertain.
- The two separately printed `February 14, 1936` entries remain two entries and are flagged rather than reconciled by conjecture.
- `January 11, 1944`, printed under academic year `1944–45`, remains unchanged and is flagged as an internal chronological inconsistency.
- The appendix states that **all 1933–34 minutes are missing** and that its entries derive from letters, diaries, and other records. The 1934–35 minutes are missing except for April. These conditions are carried at event level.
- Manual thematic coding identifies 14 women-scholar presentations, 14 Goucher-affiliated presentations, nine Lovejoy presentations, eight primitivism-related entries, and 27 politics-related entries. These are interpretive tags, not categories supplied by the source or exhaustive identity claims.

The History of Ideas Club records are separately described as `RG-15-120`: <https://aspace.library.jhu.edu/repositories/3/resources/1029>. The published appendix and the archival record group are different witnesses; neither should be substituted for the other.

## 3. The first named primitivism speaker was Lois Whitney

The first club entry explicitly naming a primitivist subject is:

> November 13, 1924 — Lois Whitney, Goucher College, *An Early Scotch Primitivist*.

Gilbert Chinard's `Nationalistic Primitivism in Eighteenth Century England` follows on November 23, 1926; Lovejoy's `Notes on Primitivism in Antiquity` follows on November 22, 1928. Priority here means the earliest such **documented club presentation**, not the invention of the scholarly concept.

Whitney was already an established investigator of the topic. Her `English Primitivistic Theories of Epic Origins` appeared in *Modern Philology* 21, no. 4 (May 1924): 337–378, DOI `10.1086/387511`. The available indexed text identifies an earlier unpublished University of Chicago doctoral dissertation, *Studies in Eighteenth-Century Primitivistic Theories of Epic Origins* (1921). The dissertation title/date are therefore supported by the article's displayed footnote as transmitted by an indexing service, but the original journal page and the university thesis record should still be inspected directly: <https://doi.org/10.1086/387511>.

Her article's opening reconstructs an opposition between the rule-governed epic associated with Homer or Virgil and a later image of the allegedly primitive bard singing before supposedly savage audiences. This directly complicates any division between **classical scholarship** and **primitivism**: the debate already concerns the historical production, literary status, and political imagination of antiquity. It also makes Scottish conjectural history, not merely ethnographic field material, part of the concept's institutional history.

The Guggenheim Foundation independently lists both Whitney and Dorothy Stimson as **1929 Fellows**: <https://www.gf.org/fellows?page=2&swcfpc=1>.

The Folger Shakespeare Library's catalogue identifies Whitney's *Primitivism and the Idea of Progress in English Popular Literature of the Eighteenth Century* as a **sole-authored 1934 Johns Hopkins Press monograph**, `xxi, 343 p.`, in the series `Contributions to the history of primitivism`: <https://catalog.folger.edu/record/29721>.

Lovejoy contributed a foreword, pp. xi–xx. Daniel J. Wilson's *Arthur O. Lovejoy: An Annotated Bibliography* (1982), entry 299, describes it as discussing chronological primitivism and praising Whitney's historical method; later scholarly editions report that the foreword distinguishes chronological and cultural primitivism. Until those ten pages are inspected directly, that fuller distinction remains **bibliographically supported but not primary-text verified**. The Folger item should be requested precisely for the title page, series page, table of contents, and pp. xi–xx.

The corrected chronology is consequently:

`Whitney dissertation, 1921 → Whitney journal article, May 1924 → Whitney club paper, November 1924 → Guggenheim Fellowship, 1929 → Whitney's own book + Lovejoy's foreword, 1934 → Lovejoy/Boas volume, 1935`.

This sequence does not establish that Lovejoy borrowed his categories from Whitney, that Whitney borrowed them from Lovejoy, or that their two publication series were identical. It does establish a woman scholar's early and sustained intellectual authorship within the same institutional problem-space.

The existing repository correctly distinguishes Lois Whitney from the Sanskritist William Dwight Whitney when auditing surname matches. That disambiguation should no longer be mistaken for intellectual irrelevance: the formerly discarded `Whitney` result identifies a central collaborator in the mature primitivism project.

## 4. Dumont: present in the room, supplementary on the page

The same appendix records:

> March 8, 1934 — Paul E. Dumont, *Primitivism in Ancient India*.

In 1935 Lovejoy and George Boas published P.-E. Dumont's `Primitivism in Indian Literature` as a `Supplementary Essay`, printed pp. 433–446, after the classical chapters. The repository's 1973 Octagon reprint places Dumont's final paragraph on printed p. 446 / PDF p. 464; printed p. 447 / PDF p. 465 already begins the separate appendix `Some Meanings of 'Nature'`. The frequently repeated `433–447` therefore extends one page beyond Dumont's essay in the inspected witness. On p. 446 Dumont explicitly states that his survey omits the entire Buddhist and Jain literatures. The oral and printed titles are not identical. The surviving record demonstrates temporal precedence, thematic proximity, and a precisely documented archival omission; it does **not** establish that the oral paper and published essay were verbally identical or identify who made the editorial decision.

The strongest reconstruction is therefore not `India excluded from the club`. It is:

`Indian material debated within the collective seminar → Indian expertise typographically repositioned as supplementary within a classical-centered publication`.

The missing 1933–34 minutes matter. The published retrospective reconstructs Dumont's talk from letters, diaries, or other records but does not identify which particular document supported this entry. The priority is to locate that reconstructive witness in `RG-15-120`, Lovejoy's `MS-0038`, the George Boas papers `MS-0010`, or Dumont-related departmental and personal files.

Christophe Vielle's 2014 university-hosted conference abstract states that, after Maurice Bloomfield's death, Dumont reached the Johns Hopkins Sanskrit/Indology position with support from Louis de La Vallée Poussin and **additional support from Sylvain Lévi**: <https://dial.uclouvain.be/pr/boreal/en/object/boreal%3A158687>.

That statement is later scholarly biographical synthesis, not the appointment letter itself. Nevertheless it identifies a sharply testable transatlantic institutional chain:

`Sylvain Lévi / Paris → support for Dumont's Baltimore appointment → Dumont's club paper on ancient India → supplementary Indian essay`.

It does not prove that Lévi recommended Lovejoy, that Dumont's paper transmitted a specifically Lévi-derived interpretation, or that Lovejoy determined Dumont's page placement personally. Appointment correspondence, the precise date and rank of Dumont's Hopkins appointment, and editorial correspondence remain the necessary controls.

## 5. Albright was not merely a provider of supplementary material

William F. Albright's contribution on ancient Western Asia was also marked `Supplementary Essay` in 1935. Yet the club register records him repeatedly addressing general historical method:

- April 8, 1937: `Art, Architecture and History`.
- March 11, 1943: `Historical Knowledge versus Scientific Knowledge`.
- November 9, 1949: `Biblical Criticism in the Light of the New Scrolls: A Test of the Literary-Historical Method`.

These entries do not erase the print hierarchy. They show that its intellectual meaning cannot be read as a simple division between editors who possessed method and Orientalists who supplied objects. Albright also appeared as a public theorist of evidentiary warrant, historical knowledge, and textual criticism.

The more precise contrast is **editorial arrangement versus recurring institutional authority**. To resolve how decisions were actually made requires the correspondence and production records, not an inference from the table of contents alone.

## 6. The constitution says “occidental”; the program does not stay inside it

Johns Hopkins' own institutional history quotes the club's remit as the historical study of ideas `in occidental literature`, while explaining that meetings admitted people beyond the university or professoriate: <https://pages.jh.edu/jhumag/0400web/28.html>.

The event register documents at least four directly relevant exceptions or complications:

1. Dumont, `Primitivism in Ancient India`, March 8, 1934.
2. Owen Lattimore, `Central Asian Migrations and Conquests`, January 12, 1939.
3. Arthur O. Lovejoy **and** Don C. Allen, `two papers on China in the Eyes of Europe, 1580–1780`, November 13, 1947.
4. Lattimore, `The Origin and Recent Development of Revolutionary Ideas in China`, November 9, 1950.

This requires four distinctions:

`written remit ≠ actual programming`;

`Asia as a specialist's archive ≠ Asia as a founder's historical subject`;

`China as seen by Europe ≠ Chinese revolutionary ideas in their recent historical setting`;

`presence in discussion ≠ equal authority in publication`.

The 1947 entry explicitly presents two papers, not necessarily a jointly authored paper. Its title makes European reception the organizing perspective. The 1950 entry, by contrast, names Chinese revolutionary ideas and their recent development; its actual argument cannot be reconstructed from the title alone. Whether either talk survives in meeting minutes, correspondence, draft, or publication requires separate verification.

## 7. Lattimore: the club's China question crossed the McCarthy crisis

The Johns Hopkins `Academic Council of Homewood Faculties` finding aid states that Senator Joseph McCarthy accused Lattimore in **March 1950**, that the Senate Foreign Relations Committee cleared him in **July 1950**, and that further hearings ran from July 1951 through April 1952: <https://aspace.library.jhu.edu/repositories/3/resources/848>.

Against that documented institutional chronology, the club's November 9, 1950 report on contemporary Chinese revolutionary ideas falls **after the initial accusation and first Senate clearance, but before the later 1951–52 hearings**.

This is an unusually strong political counterexample. The club did not simply confine Asia to a remote textual antiquity. It offered a named China specialist a platform for current revolutionary thought during the period in which his expertise had become nationally controversial.

The necessary limits are equally strict: the program does not establish who invited him, whether Lovejoy attended or spoke, whether the event was intended as solidarity, what Lattimore argued, or how the audience responded. Lovejoy had retired from regular Hopkins teaching by this period; the institution's later activity cannot automatically be equated with his personal direction.

A second Hopkins finding aid, for the `Walter Hines Page School of International Relations`, `RG-08-010`, narrates the accusation as occurring in **1951**: <https://aspace.library.jhu.edu/repositories/3/resources/931>. That conflicts with the Academic Council chronology and with the distinct 1950 initial accusation / 1951–52 later-hearings sequence. Preserve the conflict as a **finding-aid discrepancy** rather than silently selecting whichever account best serves the argument. Contemporary Senate records and university correspondence should adjudicate the precise chronology.

The Page School collection is especially promising because its own structure includes Lattimore's administrative files, subject files, 1943–52 correspondence, and a subgroup of **personal materials, 1950–53**. These are candidate record groups, not evidence that the November 1950 club talk survives there.

The existing Hopkins item-level index independently locates an `Owen Lattimore correspondence` component in Lovejoy's `MS-0038`, Box 77, Folder 11, attached item 10. Date and contents are not publicly exposed. Do not equate the file with the 1950 controversy or club meeting until the document itself is inspected.

## 7a. Victor Lowe makes the method–politics connection internal to Hopkins

The club register records Victor Lowe on March 10, 1949:

> `Source-Hunting in Philosophy and its Application to the Philosophy of Whitehead`.

The title alone does not recover Lowe's rules of source criticism. But two years later Lowe entered a documented public controversy with Lovejoy and Sidney Hook over Communism and academic freedom. The official Johns Hopkins description of the `Victor Lowe papers` states that Lowe argued **individual teaching and research**, rather than a presumed political category, should be used to predict academic conduct; it states that Lovejoy took an anti-Communist position and that the *Journal of Philosophy* published their positions and rejoinders in 1951 and 1952: <https://archivesspace.library.jhu.edu/repositories/3/resources/291>.

The same finding aid locates notes and correspondence in Series I and copies of two articles in **Box 2.10**. Victor Lowe's `In Defense of Individualistic Empiricism: A Reply to Messrs. Lovejoy and Hook`, *Journal of Philosophy* 49, no. 4 (1952): 100–111, has DOI `10.2307/2021579`; Wilson lists Lovejoy's `Rejoinder to Mr. Lowe`, pp. 111–112.

This is the strongest bridge yet between the early source-critical problem and its mature political stakes. In the 1898 Buddhist cases, Lovejoy asked whether an observed resemblance, borrowed term, or composite formula warranted a stronger relation of identity, derivation, or unintelligibility. In 1952 Lowe disputed whether party membership warranted a prediction about an individual's teaching and research. The objects differ radically; the form of dispute concerns the admissible inference from a documented classification to a stronger judgment.

The parallel must not be turned into moral symmetry. Membership in a political organization, the meaning of academic freedom during the Cold War, and the philological identity of Buddhist terms were not equivalent objects. Nor does the 1949 source-hunting paper prove that Lowe consciously transferred one method into the later controversy. What the archive establishes is an institutionally internal disagreement over the evidentiary rule by which a category could authorize judgment.

The November 1950 Lattimore paper falls between the first public attack and this 1951–52 Lovejoy–Lowe exchange. The club program, Lowe papers, Lattimore records, and Lovejoy's own published intervention together make anti-Communism part of the institutional history under study, not an external political background added by the historian.

The JHI Blog has already situated Lovejoy's academic-freedom arguments within the early Cold War and identifies his 1949 `Communism versus Academic Freedom`: <https://www.jhiblog.org/2022/11/21/3-1-the-jhi-in-the-early-cold-war/>. The new archival contribution is narrower and more concrete: it aligns the full club chronology, the exact Lowe paper locator, the Lattimore presentation, and the method-of-inference problem without treating any one of them as sufficient evidence for the others.

## 7b. Lovejoy's politics of Asia cannot be inferred from his disciplinary boundary

Wilson's annotated bibliography identifies two additional primary texts requiring direct retrieval. A 1939 `Address to Maryland Congressional Delegation`, printed in the *Congressional Record*, 76th Congress, first session, appendix, vol. 84, pt. 12, pp. 2090–2091, argued against aggressor states in Europe and Asia. A Baltimore newspaper piece of February 3, 1940, `Dr. Lovejoy Argues for an Embargo on Japan`, reportedly urged an American embargo on strategic materials rather than material support for Japanese aggression in East Asia.

These bibliographical descriptions do not establish the exact primary wording until the congressional pages and newspaper item are inspected. They do block an argument that mechanically derives Lovejoy's politics toward Asia from his `Occidental` disciplinary self-description. A scholar could organize intellectual history through an Occidental archive while taking explicit positions against an Asian empire. The boundary of a field, the hierarchy of comparative objects, and the direction of foreign-policy advocacy are related historical problems, not interchangeable political signs.

## 8. Lovejoy answered a class-based reading of the Great Chain in 1937

The publisher's official journal record identifies `Four Letters on Ernest Nagel's review of Lovejoy's “The Great Chain of Being”`, *Science & Society* 1, no. 3 (July 1937): 410–416, by Charles E. Trinkaus Jr., Ernest Nagel, Arthur O. Lovejoy, and V. J. McGill: <https://doi.org/10.1177/003682373700100309>.

Wilson's annotated bibliography, entry 301, summarizes Trinkaus as proposing a relationship between the Great Chain and social-class hierarchy, and Lovejoy as declining to infer such causal relations without sufficient evidence. Simon Schaffer also identifies the exchange and its relevance to Lovejoy's interpretive practice: <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/007327531004800309>.

The publisher metadata directly proves the collective publication, authorship, date, and pagination. Wilson and Schaffer provide **secondary descriptions** of its argumentative content. The journal text remains behind an access barrier; until pp. 410–416 are inspected, the reported wording cannot responsibly be presented as a directly checked Lovejoy quotation.

The historiographical significance is nevertheless clear: socioeconomic explanation was already raised by a contemporary interlocutor, and Lovejoy replied in public. The question is not whether later scholars retroactively injected politics into an innocent intellectual history. It is which kinds of historical connection Lovejoy considered adequately evidenced, and how that evidentiary preference limited explanations linking cosmological hierarchy with social hierarchy.

This makes the 1937 exchange a rigorous mature-period counterpart to the 1898 source-critical disputes. In both cases the central issue is the licensed inference, not merely the object under description. The political stakes differ; the methodological question remains whether a resemblance, genealogy, social analogy, or documentary sequence proves the stronger historical relation attributed to it.

## 10. Conard and Marillier supply the unequal counterexample

EPHE's official notice identifies Marillier as translator or collaborator in the French publication of Laetitia Moon Conard's work on Algonquian afterlife ideas: <https://prosopo.ephe.psl.eu/l%C3%A9on-marillier>. Southern Illinois University's journal archive separately identifies Conard as author of `Leon Marillier`, *The Open Court*, volume 1902, issue 1, article 6: <https://opensiuc.lib.siu.edu/ocj/vol1902/iss1/6/>.

The repository's automatically generated recommended citation labels the article `(2010)` although its volume is `1902`; 2010 is evidently associated with digital repository processing, not the original journal issue. The volume and original issue control the historical date. Its downloadable text was inaccessible during this sweep, so the obituary's exact wording has not been checked.

Grinnell's official departmental history reports that Conard held a doctorate in comparative religion, ran for Iowa governor on the Socialist ticket in 1932, helped establish sociology at Grinnell, was not appointed professor, and received **no regular college salary** over fifteen years as a lecturer: <https://www.grinnell.edu/academics/majors-concentrations/sociology/sociology-department-history>.

This is not the same pattern as Whitney's independent monograph or Stimson's acting presidency. It documents a different configuration: advanced scholarly qualification, politically explicit activism, published comparative-religion work, translation across French and American scholarly environments, and insecure institutional recognition.

No inspected source establishes that Conard attended Marillier's EPHE class, that Lovejoy met her, that her evidence entered notebook 005, or that she and Whitney met at Grinnell. Shared institutional or geographic coordinates are leads, not encounters.

## 11. Harvard: the boundary was administered, crossed, and generational

Existing project evidence establishes Greenough's Sanskrit instruction within Latin electives in 1872 and Lanman's separate Indo-Iranian appointment in 1880. An official Harvard archival item adds a precise counterexample: Charles Rockwell Lanman, `Lecture before Greek D`, January 23, 1893, `HUC 8892.235`: <https://hollisarchives.lib.harvard.edu/catalog/hua82020>.

The title proves that a Sanskritist gave a lecture before a Greek course or class. It does **not** identify the lecture's subject. The item must be inspected before describing its contents as Sanskrit, comparative philology, or classical–Oriental controversy.

Harvard's Lanman finding aid records his earlier Johns Hopkins teaching, **1876–1880**, before his move to Harvard. The institutional circuit is therefore:

`Lanman at Johns Hopkins → Lanman at Harvard → Lovejoy's Harvard Sanskrit/Buddhist study → Lovejoy's later Johns Hopkins career`.

A second and distinct circuit runs through Lévi's reported support for Dumont. Their coexistence does not prove that the two men operated a coordinated placement network.

One Harvard descriptive account credits Lanman with introducing Sanskrit at Harvard, whereas the earlier Greenough teaching is independently documented by Harvard-related historical evidence. The apparent inconsistency can be resolved only by distinguishing the **first instruction** from the **first dedicated institutional appointment**; archival description is not automatically identical to chronological fact.

Harvard's History of Religions Club, `HUD 3454.xx`, offers a comparative institutional precedent. Its existence or earlier date does not by itself prove that the later Baltimore club descended organizationally from it.

## 12. Paris: one city, multiple classifications and explanatory rules

The contemporary EPHE annual report distributes `Religions des peuples non civilisés`, `Religions de l'Inde`, and `Religions de la Grèce et de Rome` across distinct institutional positions: <https://www.persee.fr/issue/ephe_0000-0002_1898_num_12_8>.

Lovejoy is explicitly named among active participants in Marillier's seminar. His direct attendance at particular Lévi or Foucher public classes remains unproved. Foucher's report lists Joseph Trumbull Stickney among the named regular students and records Stickney's presentation of Sāṃkhya after Garbe; this is a documented Parisian instructional adjacency, not evidence of a personal Lovejoy–Stickney meeting.

Marillier combined an evolutionary language distinguishing allegedly civilized and uncivilized populations with Dreyfusard, Armenian-rights, Indigenous-rights, peace, and educational commitments. The EPHE notice documents both sides. Neither humanitarian commitments cancel the classificatory hierarchy nor the hierarchy exhausts his politics.

Mauss inherited practices of documentary criticism and collective bibliography from the same institutional environment while contesting Marillier's psychological explanatory model. The method was neither simply continuous nor simply superseded: documentary work and evidentiary criticism persisted while the account of what constituted a sufficient explanation changed.

## 13. Stickney, Senart, Foucher, Lévi, and Indian print make the workshop collective

The Bibliothèque nationale de France identifies a layered publication history for the *Bhagavadgītā*: <https://heritage.bnf.fr/france-southasia/reception-bhagava>.

First, the Bengali scholar-printer **Bābūrāma** produced the first printed Sanskrit text at Calcutta in 1808, using the indigenous manuscript-like *pothī* format. August Wilhelm Schlegel's 1823 Bonn edition drew on four Paris manuscripts, now identifiable as `BnF Sanscrit 336–339`. This is a substantive material and intellectual counterexample to narratives in which Indians supplied inert objects while Europeans originated textual knowledge.

Second, the same BnF account identifies Émile Senart's 1922 translation and Alfred Foucher's later revision; it separately identifies Stickney's translation, Sylvain Lévi's revision and enrichment, **Désirée Lévi's** enabling of publication in 1938, and **Silvia D'Intino's** return to the original manuscript for the 2016 edition.

These are not one undifferentiated transmission. They separate authorship, teaching, editorial revision, posthumous mediation, publication, and modern archival recovery. The presence of women at publication and restoration stages matters, but it does not license the stronger claim that they authored the philological text.

No inspected evidence identifies the exact custody location of the Stickney–Lévi working manuscript, links Lovejoy to the four Schlegel manuscripts, or proves that Bābūrāma's edition was used in Lovejoy's class.

## 14. “Primitive” does different historical work

The semantic field must be divided before political conclusions are drawn:

1. **Originary and dignifying**: the BnF quotes the 1787 French description of India's `dogmes primitifs` within an expressly admiring account of antiquity and philosophical significance.
2. **Literary and classical**: Whitney reconstructs changing assumptions about Homer, Virgil, the epic, and an imagined original bard; classical authority and primitivist reconstruction are mutually implicated.
3. **Chronological**: a privileged earlier historical condition, subsequently formalized within Lovejoy's published account.
4. **Cultural or evaluative**: criticism of contemporary civilization through an idealized alternative mode of life.
5. **Ethnographic and developmental**: living people are made evidence of another population's supposedly earlier condition, a move visible in the 1906 article and in the EPHE classification.
6. **Indian philological**: Dumont reconstructs formulations in ancient textual traditions; the surviving title does not itself tell us which evaluative or historical meaning he defended.
7. **Indigenous-American reception history**: Roy Pearce's April 12, 1945 paper specifies `The Indian as Noble Savage: the American Concept, 1775–1810`; the title identifies an American concept, not an Indigenous self-description.
8. **Contemporary political non-equivalence**: Lattimore's 1950 Chinese revolution paper addresses recent ideas without naming them primitive. This is an important negative case, not a missing data point.

The political problem is therefore not located in a single pejorative adjective. It arises where textual antiquity, living testimony, literary ideals, civilizational chronology, editorial placement, disciplinary authorization, and contemporary geopolitics are made commensurable—or deliberately kept distinct.

## 15. Page-level affordances: relations before propositions

The six supplied manuscript openings remain the strongest direct evidence for Lovejoy's own material practice. Existing page identifications and transcription limits are documented in `JHI_blog_notebook_diagrams_paper_techniques_affordances_research_log_2026-08-23.md`.

Notebook 004, manuscript p. 89, turns a facing page sideways and draws multiple causal/sequential links between numbered components. Page 121 uses nested braces and alternative term-equivalences. Page 123 combines a branching classification with an explicit distinction between a relation that is temporal and one that is logical. Page 129 sets out a comparative table and labels divisions `not mutually exclusive`. Page 149 aligns the twelve links with multiple cross-references. Notebook 005, manuscript p. 193, places a small branching sketch beside an argument concerning plants, domestic animals, sacrifice, and proposed development.

The evidentiary claim is not that diagrams decorate conclusions already reached. Branches, braces, columns, overwritten labels, and sideways arrangements allow different relation-types to remain simultaneously visible: temporal sequence, logical inclusion, synonymy, repetition, equivalence, causal dependence, and competing taxonomies.

Ayelet Even-Ezra's *Lines of Thought* provides an analytical comparison for branching diagrams as a material-cognitive practice: <https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/L/bo63098990.html>. Her medieval corpus does not establish a direct historical transmission to Lovejoy. `Affordance` here describes what an observed layout permits in its local use, not a transhistorical automatic effect.

Two later club entries supply useful medium-specific controls:

- Lovejoy, January 20, 1932, `Grounds and Effects of the New Cosmography in the Seventeenth and Eighteenth Centuries`, anticipates a mature subject later associated with the 1933 William James Lectures and *The Great Chain of Being*. No surviving draft identity or graphic continuity has been demonstrated.
- Dorothy Miner, April 13, 1944, `The Transmittal of Artistic Ideas in Medieval Workshops`, places manuscript transmission and workshop practice inside the club itself. This does not prove that Miner influenced Lovejoy's notebook techniques.

The useful comparison is:

`working page and its graphic affordances → oral seminar presentation and debate → printed argument and editorial hierarchy → archive arrangement and retrospective category`.

Whether the mature Great Chain working papers contain comparable diagrams remains an open archival question. A scanned 1966 edition yielded no substantive OCR-detected body diagrams; that result does not establish the visual contents of the 1936 first edition or the missing working papers.

## 16. Counterexamples prevent a false developmental story

The archive now blocks at least eight tempting simplifications:

| Tempting claim | Counterevidence | Historically narrower claim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The club excluded Asian subjects. | Dumont 1934, Lattimore 1939/1950, Lovejoy and Allen 1947. | Its written Occidental remit and actual programs were not identical. |
| Orientalists contributed objects but not method. | Albright's 1943 and 1949 explicitly methodological talks. | Print position and seminar authority varied independently. |
| Lovejoy alone originated the mature primitivism project. | Whitney's 1921 thesis lead, 1924 article/talk, 1929 fellowship, and 1934 book. | The problem developed within overlapping, unequally framed scholarly careers. |
| Every woman in the network served only as invisible support. | Whitney's sole-authored book; Stimson's acting presidency and historical essay. | Different women held different and unequally recognized forms of authority. |
| Women's contribution implies institutional equality. | Conard's doctorate, lecturer status, and lack of a regular salary. | Intellectual participation and stable institutional recognition diverged. |
| Parisian evolutionary classification fixes Marillier's personal politics. | His documented Dreyfusard and Indigenous-rights commitments. | Liberal activism could coexist with hierarchical classificatory practice. |

## 17. Research-ready formulations without changing the locked article

> The problem was not whether Indian thought entered the conversation. Dumont had presented “Primitivism in Ancient India” to Lovejoy's club in March 1934; only in the following year's book did Indian materials reappear as a “Supplementary Essay.” Inclusion in the workshop and subordination in print were compatible institutional outcomes.

> Nor did the category arrive ready-made from its eventual editors. Lois Whitney had published on primitivistic theories of epic origins before presenting a Scottish primitivist to the club in 1924; ten years later Lovejoy supplied the foreword to her own book. The question is less who first owned the concept than who received the authority to frame its history.

> The club's Occidental remit did not prevent Owen Lattimore from discussing Chinese revolutionary ideas in November 1950, months after McCarthy's initial accusation. The contrast between a European reception history of China, an Indian supplement, and a contested contemporary Asia locates politics not simply in omission, but in the changing conditions under which comparative knowledge could be spoken.

> On the notebook page, a sequence could remain temporal while its components were classified through nested logical inclusion. Branches, columns, and cross-references did not merely illustrate comparison; they kept incompatible relations available for testing before argumentative prose forced them into order.

These are optional formulations for future discussion only. They have not been inserted into the agreed article or its README version.
