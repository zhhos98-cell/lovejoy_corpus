## New thesis: Martin Guerre as a recurring diagnostic ancestor of verification regimes

The first sidecar described Martin Guerre as a portable epistemic object. The stronger formulation after a second sweep is that twenty-first-century actors repeatedly use the affair as a **diagnostic ancestor of verification regimes**. The case appears at moments when writers, teachers, institutions, or technical communities need to distinguish between different operations that ordinary language compresses into the word `identity`.

The recurring sequence is:

`recognition` → `identification` → `authentication` → `biometric verification` → `proof of liveness` → `proof of humanity`.

The sequence is not a simple story of technical progress. At each transition, the object that has to be proved changes.

- In Artigat, the question is whether a socially known claimant is the same person who departed.
- In modern state identification, the question is whether a person can be attached to a durable administrative record.
- In digital authentication, the question is whether the current user is entitled to act as the already enrolled identity.
- In biometric enrolment, the question is whether body and credential correspond.
- In liveness detection, the question is whether the body presented to the system is presently alive rather than a replay, mask, image, or automated artefact.
- In current `proof of humanity` systems, the prior boundary between one human and another is joined by a second boundary between human and machine.

Martin Guerre survives these changes because the sixteenth-century affair provides a compact negative control: it is a famous world in which recognition, memory, intimacy, local reputation, bodily signs, and testimony nearly fail to anchor one body to one authorized identity. Later systems can describe themselves by saying, explicitly or implicitly, `this is what our verifier is meant to prevent`.

The most important twenty-first-century shift is therefore from the philosophical noun `identity` toward the procedural vocabulary of **verification**. The affair is repeatedly recruited to make that shift intelligible.

## 1. Lorraine Daston, 2019: Martin Guerre as a device for defamiliarizing biometric certainty

Lorraine Daston's `Classic Natalie` (*H-France Salon* 11.15.6, 2019) provides unusually explicit evidence for Martin Guerre's pedagogical use in the history of knowledge. Daston explains why she assigned *The Return of Martin Guerre* in a University of Chicago seminar on classic works of history. Her stated aim was not merely to teach microhistory as a genre. She wanted students who had grown up amid contemporary documentation, smartphones, biometrics, selfies, and social media to see how historically produced their confidence in personal identity actually is.

The pedagogical operation is a reversal of the familiar modernization narrative. Instead of presenting the sixteenth century as deficient because it lacked photographs, fingerprints, identity cards, and continuous documentary records, the historical case is used to make the twenty-first-century regime strange. Students are invited to notice the infrastructures that make contemporary sameness appear self-evident: serial documentation from birth onward, institutional records, visual self-repetition, and biometrically mediated access.

This produces a distinct epistemic use:

`early-modern uncertainty` → `defamiliarization of modern certainty`.

The affair therefore functions as more than a historical example. It becomes a **counterfactual subtraction machine**: remove continuous records, photographs, state-issued identifiers, biometric habits, and modern mirrors of the self, then ask what recognition has to depend on. The resulting comparison shifts attention from intrinsic identity to the infrastructures that stabilize re-identification across time.

Daston also makes a second methodological point relevant to the first sidecar. She distinguishes Davis's microhistory from an inductive `case study` intended to yield a general law. For Daston, Davis chooses an anomaly whose strangeness reveals ordinary life. That distinction helps explain Martin Guerre's unusual portability. The episode need not be representative in the statistical sense to be epistemically productive. It is useful precisely because the failure case exposes normally invisible conditions of successful recognition.

### Knowledge-history payoff

Martin Guerre can be treated as a **revealing anomaly** rather than a representative sample. This matters for the wider project vocabulary. Portability need not depend on representative typicality; it can depend on the ability of an anomalous case to expose the relations and supports that routine cases leave tacit.

## 2. French National Assembly, 2020: Martin Guerre enters state digital-identity discourse

The strongest new evidence for institutional uptake is the French National Assembly's *Rapport d'information sur l'identité numérique*, no. 3190, registered 8 July 2020. The report is not a historical essay. It is a parliamentary information report on digital identity, authentication, biometrics, FranceConnect, eIDAS, public/private identity providers, privacy, interoperability, and the design of a future sovereign digital identity.

Its historical section reconstructs a long movement from interpersonal identification toward state-issued identity. The report describes medieval and early-modern identification as combining face-to-face recognition, signs, group memory, growing written registration, and eventually state documentation. Immediately after this historical outline, it invokes the Martin Guerre affair as evidence for the vulnerability of identification based principally on face-to-face recognition and collective memory. The footnote points readers back to Natalie Zemon Davis.

The significant point is not that parliamentarians mention a famous historical anecdote. It is **where** the anecdote is placed in the report's conceptual architecture. Martin Guerre appears immediately before the report moves into contemporary identity theft and then into a formal distinction between `identification` and `authentication`.

The report's operational grammar is roughly:

`historical identification` → `risk of usurpation` → `digital identity` → `identification` / `authentication` distinction → `enrolment` → `biometric comparison` → `proof of liveness`.

The report defines identification as establishing the user's identity and authentication as the user's proof that they are entitled to use that identity. It further distinguishes biometric authentication/comparison from biometric identification against a larger image bank. In the enrolment process, it discusses `preuve de vivant`: liveness procedures intended to establish that the applicant is a live human presently performing the enrolment action rather than a fraudulent representation.

This creates a striking epistemic escalation within one official document. Martin Guerre begins as a sixteenth-century failure of person-to-person re-identification; within a few pages the problem has become machine-mediated proof that an enrolling user is both the right person and presently live.

### Why this matters

This is evidence that Martin Guerre has crossed from historiographical afterlife into **governance vocabulary**. The affair is being used to narrate why modern identity infrastructures exist and why digital verification must distinguish multiple proof operations.

The historical case therefore participates in a state-level genealogy of trust:

`interknowledge` → `civil registry` → `state credential` → `digital credential` → `biometric matching` → `liveness`.

Again, this should not be read as an objective linear progress from error to certainty. The same report warns that facial-recognition correspondence is probabilistic rather than absolute. A new verifier does not abolish uncertainty; it relocates uncertainty into algorithms, databases, enrolment procedures, thresholds, and trusted third parties.

That relocation is one of the strongest history-of-knowledge findings in the whole dossier.

## 3. Laura Huttunen, 2025: from imposture to disappearance and the state's knowledge grid

Laura Huttunen's *Missing Persons, Political Landscapes and Cultural Practices: Violent Absences, Haunting Presences* (Manchester University Press, 2025) opens its historical discussion of disappearance with Martin Guerre. Huttunen uses the case to historicize absence and identification rather than simply retell a famous imposture.

The conceptual shift is substantial. In the classic narrative, Martin Guerre is the person whose identity is stolen. In Huttunen's anthropology, he is also a **missing person whose absence creates an epistemic and social vacancy**. This vacancy is important because modern states treat disappearance as an anomaly in a `knowledge grid`: persons are expected to remain locatable within administrative, documentary, familial, and often biomedical systems.

Huttunen contrasts the sixteenth-century situation with present-day identification practices dominated by state documents and specialized techniques, including biomedical identification and facial recognition. But she also complicates any triumphalist account. Modern states possess a much stronger capacity to know, classify, and reconnect persons, while their interest is unevenly distributed. Undocumented migrants, unidentified dead, marginalized populations, and politically disappeared persons may remain outside or at the margins of the same systems that promise near-continuous identification.

Martin Guerre is therefore relocated from the history of imposture into the anthropology of **absence, state legibility, and differential knowability**.

### Knowledge-history payoff

This adds an axis absent from the first sidecar:

`verification power` must be analyzed together with `obligation/desire to know`.

A system can possess powerful identification techniques while declining, failing, or being politically prevented from identifying particular persons. The crucial history is therefore not simply an increase in technical resolution. It is also a changing distribution of who becomes a compulsory object of knowledge and who remains `disappearable`.

The Martin Guerre problem can now be decomposed into at least two distinct questions:

1. `Can this person be identified?`
2. `Which institution has an interest, obligation, or authority to make this person identifiable?`

That second question sharply widens the case from epistemology to political epistemology.

## 4. 2026 eIDAS discourse: `Martin Guerre 2.0`

A March 2026 French cybersecurity/identity discussion reported by *Alliancy* makes the reuse explicit in contemporary industrial language. The article, headed `Martin Guerre 2.0`, reports a Forum INCYBER discussion of the European digital identity wallet under eIDAS 2.0. The Martin Guerre affair is introduced to describe the basic vulnerability of a digital environment in which a claimant may be believed because robust verification is absent.

The contemporary substitution is clear:

`face-to-face impostor occupying another man's social position`

becomes

`remote claimant opening accounts, renting assets, moving funds, or presenting manipulated documents without physical encounter`.

The crucial term is no longer identity in the philosophical sense but **cost of impersonation versus cost of proof**. Dematerialization lowers the cost of presenting claims while the identity-wallet project is framed as an attempt to restore a trustworthy verification layer.

This is a particularly important afterlife because the historical analogy is being used by a technical-industrial field actively building identity infrastructure. Martin Guerre has become a shorthand for a design problem.

### Analytical caution

The analogy is productive but historically flattening. Arnaud's success depended on long-duration social integration, local memory, intimate knowledge, household labor, kinship, and property relations. Digital fraud may instead exploit copied documents, synthetic media, breached credentials, or remote onboarding. Calling the latter `Martin Guerre 2.0` preserves the relation `claimant ↔ verifier` while radically changing the carrier and social ecology.

That difference is exactly why the reuse is useful for our purposes. It demonstrates **relation-level persistence under carrier substitution**.

## 5. Jill Lepore, 2026: from proof of identity to proof of humanity

Jill Lepore's `Are You a Human?` (*The New Yorker*, 10 August 2026) creates the most dramatic twenty-first-century relocation found so far. The article is about biometric identity verification, bots, AI agents, cybersecurity, iris scanning, and commercial `Proof of Humanity` systems. Lepore places Arnaud du Tilh and Martin Guerre inside a long genealogy of the technologies and performances by which claims about persons are made believable.

The conceptual sequence of the article moves from personal imposture through identity documents and biometric systems toward an internet in which the new uncertainty is whether an apparent interlocutor is human at all. Martin Guerre is one waypoint in a history that ends, for the moment, with iris-based verification and proof-of-personhood infrastructures.

This produces a category shift:

`Is this Martin Guerre?`

→ `Is this user the credentialed person?`

→ `Is this claimant a live human?`

→ `Is this online agent human rather than artificial?`

The move is historically significant because `personhood` becomes an object of technical certification. Earlier identity systems presumed a human subject and attempted to distinguish one person from another. Proof-of-humanity systems add a prior classifier: human / machine. They make `humanity` itself a credentialable attribute.

Lepore also points to an institutional displacement. Identification was historically a paradigmatic state function; proof-of-humanity projects can be operated by private, transnational firms that create their own enrolment devices, databases, credentials, and trust networks. The authority question therefore changes along with the object of proof:

`Who are you?` is joined by `who gets to certify that you count as a human participant?`

That is a much larger political-epistemic problem than ordinary identity fraud.

## 6. A revised operator map

The second sweep suggests replacing a generic `identity` column with a set of explicit operators.

| Regime | Target proposition | Main operator | Typical carrier | Authorizing body |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Artigat / court | `this claimant is Martin` | recognition + testimony + comparison | memory, body, speech, intimacy, marks | household, village, court |
| documentary state | `this body corresponds to this civil identity` | registration + re-identification | certificate, passport, registry, number | state administration |
| biometric state/digital ID | `this applicant matches the enrolled credential` | authentication / matching | face, fingerprint, chip, database | state / regulated provider |
| remote enrolment | `this is the credential-holder acting now` | liveness + credential binding | video, movement, device, biometric signal | identity provider / state framework |
| AI-era proof of humanity | `this participant is a unique human rather than a machine` | proof of personhood/humanity | iris, cryptographic credential, behavioral trace | private network, platform, sometimes state-regulated ecosystem |

The table exposes a major historical transformation: **the subject remains superficially singular while the target proposition keeps changing**. `Identity` is therefore an unstable umbrella term. The knowledge history lies in specifying exactly what proposition a given verifier is designed to warrant.

## 7. Recognition, identification, authentication: stop treating them as synonyms

The French parliamentary report makes a distinction that should be imported into the analytical vocabulary of the sidecar.

### Recognition
A person or group judges a claimant to be familiar or continuous with a previously known person. This can be socially rich and institutionally weak; it can also be embedded in formal proceedings.

### Identification
A system establishes which identity record or socially recognized person the claimant is to count as.

### Authentication
A previously identified subject demonstrates entitlement to use a particular identity or credential in a specific transaction.

### Liveness verification
A system tests whether the presenting source is a live subject rather than a static or replayed artefact.

### Proof of humanity / personhood
A system warrants membership in the class of unique human participants rather than merely matching one human identity record.

Martin Guerre is useful because the case can be inserted before each distinction as a reminder that **recognizability is not the same thing as warranted identity**.

## 8. The hidden continuity: verifier failure, not impostor cleverness

Most popular retellings foreground Arnaud's extraordinary performance and memory. The twenty-first-century knowledge history suggests a different stable object: the **fallibility and design of the verifier**.

Across the reuses, the recurrent structure is:

`claim` + `available traces` + `verification procedure` + `authority threshold` → `accepted / rejected identity proposition`.

The claimant changes. The traces change. The procedure changes. The authority changes. The threshold changes. The recurring question is whether the verifier has enough independent leverage over the claim to distinguish simulation from continuity.

This formulation prevents a presentist mistake. A passport, biometric, or iris scan is not simply `better memory`. It creates a different relation between subject, trace, institution, and prior enrolment. Modern verification works partly by manufacturing durable traces in advance so that later claims can be checked against them. The crucial technical-historical shift is therefore from **retrospective reconstruction** toward **prospective enrolment**.

Artigat tries to reconstruct Martin after a long absence from memories and surviving signs. Modern identity systems attempt to record a person before the later verification event occurs.

That is a deep change in temporal architecture:

`absence → reconstruct identity backward`

becomes

`enrol now → authenticate later against stored prior state`.

This may be the cleanest genuinely history-of-knowledge result in the entire Martin Guerre afterlife dossier.

## 9. Prospective enrolment as the missing concept

The prior sidecar emphasized carriers and warrants but did not isolate **enrolment**. The digital-identity material shows that enrolment is essential.

A verification regime becomes substantially more powerful when it can control the creation of the reference state against which later claims are tested. Fingerprints, passport records, civil registries, biometric templates, and cryptographic credentials all depend on some prior act of enrolment or registration.

Martin Guerre's world lacked a continuously maintained, institutionally authoritative reference record at the required resolution. The court was forced into ad hoc comparison among memories, bodily descriptions, family recognition, intimate knowledge, and testimony.

This yields a useful operator contrast:

`ad hoc evidentiary reconstruction` vs `pre-authorized reference-state comparison`.

Modern identity infrastructures are not merely collections of more accurate signs. They reorganize the **time at which evidence is produced**.

## 10. From state legibility to private trust infrastructure

The 2020 French parliamentary report and the 2026 Lepore article together reveal another shift in authority.

The classical modern story runs:

`local recognition` → `state monopoly/privilege of authoritative identity`.

The contemporary story is more fractured:

`state identity` ↔ `regulated identity provider` ↔ `platform account` ↔ `private biometric credential` ↔ `cross-platform proof-of-humanity network`.

Thus the current question is no longer only whether the state can make a population legible. It is also **which actors are permitted to issue portable claims about personhood and humanity**.

This means Martin Guerre's latest afterlife enters histories of privatized authority, platform governance, cybersecurity, and AI, not just histories of surveillance or the state.

## 12. Comparative implications for the active Lovejoy method layer

The Martin Guerre control now sharpens several distinctions useful elsewhere without implying genealogy.

### Relation persistence under carrier change
The stable relation is `claimant ↔ verifier ↔ authorized identity proposition`; its carriers change from memory and body to document, biometric, device, and cryptographic credential.

### Same word, different target proposition
`Identity` can refer to social recognition, civil identity, authenticated account use, biometric continuity, or human/non-human classification. Vocabulary continuity therefore cannot establish object continuity.

### Prospective evidence production
Modern systems often create evidence in advance through enrolment. This is distinct from later retrospective reconstruction. Histories of knowledge should track when reference states are manufactured, by whom, and for which later adjudications.

### Verification threshold as an historical variable
No regime requires absolute metaphysical certainty. Each establishes a threshold sufficient for a transaction, legal judgment, administrative action, or platform permission. The threshold and consequences of false acceptance / false rejection are historically specific.

### Authority migration
The actor authorized to settle the identity proposition can move from community to court, state, platform, private provider, algorithmic system, or hybrid trust network.

### Portability without genealogy
Martin Guerre's reappearance across these regimes shows precisely how a case can remain analytically portable without any continuous institutional genealogy between the original affair and later technologies.

## 13. New source-critical cautions

1. **Do not use `identity theft` uncritically for the sixteenth-century affair.** The contemporary legal/digital term carries assumptions about informational ownership and credential systems that differ from the social, marital, property, and juridical relations of Artigat.
2. **Do not treat biometrics as certainty.** Contemporary official sources themselves distinguish probabilistic matching, identification, authentication, and liveness.
3. **Do not infer a continuous genealogy from Martin Guerre to eIDAS or proof-of-humanity.** The evidence establishes later actors' analogical reuse, not historical descent.
4. **Do not collapse missing-person identification with imposture.** Huttunen's use preserves a different problem: absence and the political distribution of search/identification obligations.
5. **Do not collapse proof of personhood with proof of a particular civil identity.** Some contemporary systems aim only to establish unique human participation, not a legal name or citizenship identity.

## 14. Strongest revised formulation

**Martin Guerre's twenty-first-century afterlife is increasingly organized around verification rather than identity. The sixteenth-century affair is repeatedly recalled as a failure case whenever a modern field needs to explain why recognition is insufficient and why a stronger relation between claimant, trace, reference state, verifier, and authority must be built. In historical identification studies it exposes the media of person-knowledge; in Daston's pedagogy it defamiliarizes the biometric certainty of the present; in the French National Assembly's 2020 digital-identity report it becomes a prehistory of identification, authentication, biometrics, and liveness; in Huttunen's 2025 anthropology it moves from imposture to absence and the state's uneven knowledge grid; in 2026 eIDAS discourse it is explicitly rebranded `Martin Guerre 2.0`; and in Lepore's AI-era account the original question `which person is this?` expands into `is this participant a human at all?` The deepest structural transformation is temporal: Artigat reconstructs identity retrospectively after continuity has broken, whereas modern systems increasingly manufacture a reference state prospectively through enrolment so that later claims can be authenticated. The durable object is therefore not a stable meaning of identity but a recurrent problem of how enough independent leverage can be created over a claimant to turn recognition into warranted verification.**
