# II. Yarrow 1881 closes the moon/coyote carrier

H. C. Yarrow's paper appears in the *First Annual Report of the Bureau of Ethnology* under the exact title:

> *A Further Contribution to the Study of the Mortuary Customs of the North American Indians*.

Its cremation section says that an account of the origin of cremation among the Nishinam of California is given by Stephen Powers. Yarrow then prints the distinctive myth:

- moon and coyote jointly create things;
- the moon wants human souls, after death, to return after two or three days as the moon itself returns;
- coyote insists that death should be final and bodies should be burned;
- the moon subsequently causes the death of coyote's son;
- coyote tries to evade his own rule;
- the rule is enforced and permanent death/cremation is thereby established.

The correspondence with Lovejoy's marginal `moon & coyote` is exact in subject, and the notebook's abbreviated title independently points to the same Yarrow paper.

Source path:

`STEPHEN POWERS, TRIBES OF CALIFORNIA (1877), p.341`
→ `H. C. YARROW, FIRST ANNUAL REPORT BUREAU OF ETHNOLOGY (1881)`
→ `LOVEJOY 005 p.10 CROSS-REFERENCE / CASE NOTE`.

### Immediate carrier versus lower witness

At present Yarrow is the strongest immediate printed carrier because Lovejoy appears to note Yarrow's article title on the same page. Powers is the explicitly acknowledged lower ethnographic witness.

If future image reinspection shows `Powers` directly in Lovejoy's margin, the relation can be upgraded to dual consultation rather than Yarrow-only routing.

The notebook punctuation also remains compatible with a functional note of the form:

`American legends — v. Brinton`

where `v.` = `vide`, `see Brinton`.

This is now the preferred semantic interpretation, but the author name itself remains a paleographic HOLD until image closure.

## 2. Brinton is nevertheless genuinely relevant to the surrounding problem

The non-title reading is not a rescue by mere plausibility. Brinton's pre-1899 corpus directly occupies the surrounding conceptual field.

Most importantly, *Essays of an Americanist* (1890), in `The Path to Mictlan`, states that among primitive American peoples there is no conception of natural death: a person is always thought to have been `killed`, and disease death is assigned to sorcery or another specific hostile cause.

That proposition strongly matches 005 p.7/MS11, where Lovejoy opens the death dossier by arguing that death is not initially conceived as a natural necessity and that each death requires a special explanatory cause such as sorcery.

Brinton's *The Myths of the New World* likewise supplies broad American comparative material on soul, afterlife and myth, and was already identified in Round 13 as a strong content-level control for Lovejoy's non-moral afterlife problem.

So the safest source role is:

`BRINTON CORPUS = AMERICAN MYTH / AFTERLIFE FIELD POINTER OR SYNTHETIC CONTROL`

not

`BRINTON = IMMEDIATE SOURCE OF EVERY AMERICAN LEGEND ON p.10`.
