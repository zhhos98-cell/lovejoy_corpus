# I. 1898–1914: pre-JHI Lovejoy — relation-specific proof problems before institutional history of ideas

Existing repository direct-primary/archive controls establish:

### 1898 Buddhist technical work

Lovejoy's 1898 JAOS article and notebook 004 make semantic/classificatory identity, temporal priority, causal role, and textual derivation **separate proof burdens**.

High-value archive control: notebook 004 MS p.123 / PDF p.42. `viññāṇa` is classificatorily/logically located inside `nāma/nāmarūpa` while temporal antecedence carries a distinct epistemic status.

Safe result:

`CLASSIFICATORY / LOGICAL RELATION`
!=
`TEMPORAL RELATION`
!=
`CAUSAL RELATION`
!=
`HISTORICAL DERIVATION`.

### 1906 `Primitive Energetics`

Lovejoy aggregates heterogeneous ethnographic evidence into an impersonal-power type and promotes it toward `the fundamental concept of primitive philosophy`.

This introduces a new burden:

`LOCAL CASE / MECHANISM EVIDENCE`
→ `CROSS-CULTURAL TYPE`
→ `POPULATION / FUNDAMENTAL PROMOTION`.

### 1914 Leuba review

Direct primary already locked in repo. Lovejoy simultaneously:

- identifies Leuba's `dynamism` as essentially/precisely his own 1906 theory;
- distinguishes strong `non-animistic` type/content from only probable, non-demonstrable `pre-animistic` chronological priority;
- distinguishes stipulative naming, common elements across historic uses, and analytical description of complex observable phenomena.

Thus by 1914:

`TYPE / CONTENT`
!=
`CHRONOLOGICAL PRIORITY`
!=
`NOMINAL DEFINITION`.

### Why this matters for the master history

Do **not** write a direct `Buddhism -> unit-idea` genealogy.

The potentially durable continuity is a **proof-burden grammar**:

> analytical sameness, chronology, historical connection, and causal operation are propositions with different warrants.

Later JHI methodological debates repeatedly reconstruct exactly these separations.

# II. 1923: Johns Hopkins History of Ideas Club institutionalizes an interdisciplinary jurisdiction

Direct source:

Johns Hopkins University Libraries, `History of Ideas Club records`, RG-15-120:  
https://aspace.library.jhu.edu/repositories/3/resources/1029

Founded 1923 by a small Hopkins circle including Lovejoy, George Boas, and H. C. Lancaster.

The constitution defines the field as historical study of development/influence of general philosophical conceptions, ethical ideas, and aesthetic fashions **in occidental literatures**, together with their relations to philosophy, science, and political/social movements.

Key structure:

`GENERAL IDEA / TENDENCY`
→ `DEVELOPMENT + INFLUENCE`
→ `MULTIPLE DISCIPLINARY MANIFESTATIONS`.

But the institutional jurisdiction is explicitly `occidental` at this stage.

The archives contain minutes, abstracts, correspondence, membership lists, speaker/topic lists, and later financial records. This remains a **major direct archival target**, especially for specific sessions rather than broad programme claims.

# III. 1924–35: `primitivism` becomes a recurrent Club object and publication programme

Direct retrospective programme source:

*Studies in Intellectual History* (Johns Hopkins History of Ideas Club, 1953), especially Appendix B `Speakers and Their Topics, 1923–1952`. Hopkins Press open edition metadata:  
https://press.jhu.edu/books/title/5291/studies-intellectual-history

Secure Club sequence already recovered:

- 13 Nov 1924 — Lois Whitney, `An Early Scotch Primitivist`;
- 23 Nov 1926 — Gilbert Chinard, `Nationalistic Primitivism in Eighteenth Century England`;
- 22 Nov 1928 — Lovejoy, `Notes on Primitivism in Antiquity`;
- 8 Mar 1934 — Paul E. Dumont, `Primitivism in Ancient India`.

Other field-neighboring Club topics already recovered include diffusion, animism, China/Europe, India, noble savage/Indian, and later revolutionary ideas in China.

### 1934 → 1935 direct publication edge

Dumont's Club paper becomes `Primitivism in Indian Literature`, published as a **supplementary essay** in Lovejoy and Boas, *Primitivism and Related Ideas in Antiquity* (1935), alongside W. F. Albright on Ancient Western Asia.

Hopkins Press current book page:  
https://www.press.jhu.edu/books/title/2186/primitivism-and-related-ideas-antiquity

This is a secure institutional sequence:

`CLUB SEMINAR OBJECT`
→ `COMPARATIVE / REGIONAL EXTENSION`
→ `PUBLISHED HISTORY-OF-IDEAS PROJECT`.

### 1935 methodological significance

Existing repo control of the volume framing shows:

- philosophical/literary `systems` can be analytically decomposed into unit-ideas and regrouped;
- analytical taxonomy need not imply that historical actors held neatly separated categories;
- non-Western regions enter as `Supplementary Essays`, extending perspective beyond the main classical/Western address.

Safe current historical form:

`OCCIDENTAL MAIN ADDRESS`
+
`NON-WESTERN COMPARATIVE / SUPPLEMENTARY EXTENSION`.

This is inclusion with asymmetrical historiographical role, not simple exclusion.

# V. 1939–40: immediate intellectual and institutional prehistory of JHI

### Lovejoy 1939

`Present Standpoints and Past History`, *Journal of Philosophy* 36.18 (1939), 477–489. DOI 10.2307/2017901.

This is an immediate pre-JHI methodological text and should be direct-read alongside 1940 `Reflections`. It may clarify how Lovejoy conceived historical standpoint, objectivity, presentism, and selection immediately before launching the journal.

### 1940 founding

JHI vol.1 no.1 begins January 1940. JSTOR issue:  
https://www.jstor.org/stable/i327235

Lovejoy's `Reflections on the History of Ideas`, pp.3–23, is the opening programmatic article.

The journal is commonly and reliably described as founded by Arthur O. Lovejoy and Philip P. Wiener. Philip Wiener undertook the managing/editorial labor of the new journal; later testimony by Morris Cohen also describes Wiener as the gifted former student who had taken on the arduous managing-editor role.

The first volume was published by Johns Hopkins University Press.

### Lovejoy's founding programme

Secure current controls:

- disciplinary/departmental boundaries do not match actual migration of historical ideas;
- history of ideas should cross philosophy, science, literature, religion, political/social thought, etc.;
- analytical relations must ultimately return to historical evidence;
- Lovejoy explicitly names `primitivism` among exemplary broad ideas/problems suitable for the field.

This makes `primitivism` an **actor-level founding JHI object**, not an external retrospective bridge.

### 1940 editorial pluralism

Daniel J. Wilson's bibliography records Lovejoy's `Editorial Note`, JHI 1 (1940), p.503, clarifying that the journal is open to contributors whose interpretations of history differ from those of the editors.

Current status: bibliography/searchable-text controlled; original page should be direct-locked.

Important institutional implication:

`LOVEJOY PROGRAMME`
!=
`JHI ORTHODOXY`.

# VI. 1943–48: JHI / adjacent methodological debate becomes explicit

### Harold A. Taylor, 1943

`Further Reflections on the History of Ideas`, *Journal of Philosophy* 40.11 (1943), 281–299.

This is an early extended examination of Lovejoy's programme before the Spitzer controversy. It is underused in the present repo and should be direct-read.

### Spitzer–Lovejoy, 1944

Leo Spitzer, `Geistesgeschichte vs. History of Ideas as Applied to Hitlerism`, JHI 5.2 (1944), 191–203.

Lovejoy, `Reply to Professor Spitzer`, JHI 5.2 (1944), 204–219.

New near-direct control already written elsewhere in repo:

Lovejoy characterizes his procedure as two `working hypotheses`:

1. discriminable ideas can recur across more than one writer/school/period;
2. a writer/school/period can contain more than one discriminable component.

Most important: how far these hypotheses hold is a **question of historical fact verified by texts**.

Thus mature Lovejoy's methodological defense can be reconstructed as empirical/textual rather than as a bare ontology of timeless atoms.

Spitzer's pressure should be typed:

`COMPONENT DISCRIMINATION`
!=
`WHOLE STATUS`
!=
`CONTINUITY`
!=
`CAUSAL EXPLANATION`.

### Pearce, 1947–53

Roy Harvey Pearce's programme becomes an internal extension rather than an external repudiation.

1947 captivity narrative work already asks how apparently similar textual materials function differently under changing audiences/cultural needs.

1948 `A Note on Method in the History of Ideas`, JHI 9.3, 372–379:

`ANALYTIC PIONEER WORK`
→ `SYNTHETIC SECOND STAGE`
→ ask how ideas function inside specific cultural processes.

His worked methodological example includes `primitivism + progress`.

1953 *The Savages of America* develops `Idea -> Symbol -> Image -> thought/action`, with `savagism` and `the Indian` at the center.

This gives a strong internal JHI/Hopkins genealogy in which **primitive/savage objects themselves help drive methodological transformation**.

### Lovejoy seventy-five, 1948

JHI vol.9 no.4 is a Lovejoy special issue: `Arthur O. Lovejoy at Seventy-Five: Reason at Work`.

Contents include:

- George Boas, `A. O. Lovejoy as Historian of Philosophy`, 404–411;
- Maurice Mandelbaum, `Arthur O. Lovejoy and the Theory of Historiography`, 412–423;
- W. P. Montague, `My Friend Lovejoy`, 424–427;
- Marjorie Nicolson, `A. O. Lovejoy as Teacher`, 428–438;
- Theodore Spencer review of *Essays in the History of Ideas*, 439–446.

JSTOR issue:  
https://www.jstor.org/stable/i327270

### Mandelbaum 1948 significance

Near-contemporary JHI interpretation stresses Lovejoy's concern with causal relationships and a plural past.

Mandelbaum's later distinction also clarifies:

`CONTINUOUS IDEA` = historically connected/influenced;

`RECURRENT IDEA` = independently reappearing.

Analogy: anthropological `diffusion` vs `independent invention`.

This turns unit-idea identity into a source-route/transmission problem rather than a self-executing continuity claim.

# VII. 1953: the Hopkins Club publishes its own retrospective disciplinary dossier

*Studies in Intellectual History* (1953) is itself a disciplinary artifact.

Current Hopkins Press table of contents includes:

1. George Boas — `Some Problems of Intellectual History`;
2. Harold Cherniss — history of ideas and ancient Greek philosophy;
3. Ludwig Edelstein — `The Golden Chain of Homer`;
4. Leo Spitzer — language/science/philosophy/poetry;
5. Gilbert Chinard — progress/perfectibility;
6. Owsei Temkin — concept of infection;
7. Bentley Glass — Mendel/discovery;
8. Philip P. Wiener — `Lovejoy's Rôle in American Philosophy`;
9. Dorothy Stimson — `The History of Ideas Club`;
10. appendices on Club officers and speakers/topics 1923–52.

This makes the volume a **self-documenting snapshot of the Hopkins tradition's interdisciplinarity**, ranging across classics, language, medicine, biology, philosophy, literature, and science.

It should be treated as both historiography and institutional primary source.

# VIII. 1961–75: consolidation, methodological differentiation, and challenge

### Wiener 1961

Philip P. Wiener, `Some Problems and Methods in the History of Ideas`, JHI 22.4 (1961), around pp.531–548 (exact range to direct-lock).

This is a major underused bridge between Lovejoy-era programme and later Dictionary work.

### Lovejoy death / memory

Lovejoy dies 1962. JHI memory continues institutionally rather than ending with him.

Ludwig Edelstein, `In Memory of A. O. Lovejoy`, JHI 24.4 (1963), 451–456.

### 1964 historiographic self-placement

Robert Allen Skotheim, `The Writing of American Histories of Ideas: Two Traditions in the XXth Century`, JHI 25.2 (1964), 257–278.

This places history-of-ideas writing inside broader American historiographical traditions and the internalist/externalist problem.

The same 1964 issue includes `Political Theory and the History of Ideas`, showing methodological self-reflection is part of regular JHI practice.

### Mandelbaum 1965

`The History of Ideas, Intellectual History, and the History of Philosophy`, *History and Theory*, Beiheft 5 (1965), 33–66.

Mandelbaum explicitly distinguishes Lovejoy's narrower technical movement from broader intellectual history and from history of philosophy, comparing it with German Begriffsgeschichte traditions.

This is a key canon-forming methodological text.

### 1969 Skinner

Quentin Skinner, `Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas`, *History and Theory* 8.1 (1969), 3–53.

Canonical contextualist critique later makes Lovejoy a standard foil for reified, context-free doctrine/unit histories.

Important control: the present project must separate Skinner's polemical target from Lovejoy's actual practice and from the already existing **internal JHI debates before Skinner**.

### 1972–73 internal JHI response field

Joseph Mazzeo, `Some Interpretations of the History of Ideas`, JHI 33.3 (1972), 379ff.

Bhikhu Parekh, `The History of Political Ideas: A Critique of Q. Skinner's Methodology`, JHI 34.2 (1973), 163ff.

Thus JHI does not simply absorb the contextualist critique; it becomes a venue where Skinner's methodology itself is debated.

### 1973 Lovejoy centenary

Philip P. Wiener, `Towards Commemorating the Centenary of Arthur O. Lovejoy's Birthday`, JHI 34.4 (1973), 591–598.

This is a major memory/canonization source and should be direct-read.

### Dictionary culmination

Philip P. Wiener, ed., *Dictionary of the History of Ideas: Studies of Selected Pivotal Ideas* (multi-volume, 1968/73–75 publication history).

Later scholarship often treats the Dictionary as a culmination/institutionalization of the Lovejoyian programme.

The Dictionary preface should be compared directly with 1935, 1940, 1948 and 1961 programme statements.

# IX. 1985–2006: editorial migration and JHI self-historicization

Donald R. Kelley 2005 retrospective (`From the Executive Editor`, JHI 66.4, 475–476) supplies a direct editorial succession account:

- by 1985 the journal's editorial base was at Temple University under Philip Wiener;
- in 1985 JHI moved to the University of Rochester and Kelley replaced Wiener after Wiener's forty-five years as editor;
- Robin Ladrach joined in 1986;
- Kelley and JHI moved to Rutgers in 1991;
- in 2006 the journal moved to the University of Pennsylvania under a new executive-editor team (Warren Breckman, Martin Burke, Anthony Grafton, Ann Moyer).

Source:  
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236777912_From_the_Executive_Editor

### 1987 double self-reflection

JHI 48.1 includes Donald Kelley, `Horizons of Intellectual History: Retrospect, Circumspect, Prospect`, 143–169.

JHI 48.2 contains a Great Chain / Lovejoy retrospective cluster:

- Daniel J. Wilson, `Lovejoy's The Great Chain of Being after Fifty Years`, 187–206;
- Gladys Gordon-Bournique, `A. O. Lovejoy and the "History of Ideas"`, 207–210;
- Edward Mahoney, `Lovejoy and the Hierarchy of Being`, 211–230;
- Francis Oakley, `Lovejoy's Unexplored Option`, 231–245;
- Melvin Richter, `Begriffsgeschichte and the History of Ideas`, 247–263.

JSTOR issue:  
https://www.jstor.org/stable/i346291

The same issue then includes Pocock and Blumenberg-related work, making it a striking site where Lovejoy's legacy is explicitly placed beside alternative historiographical programmes.

### 1990 half-century self-audit

Donald Kelley, `What Is Happening to the History of Ideas?`, JHI 51.1 (1990), 3–25.

Kelley frames American history of ideas around Lovejoy, colleagues, epigones, and critics, but also insists on longer and international traditions (Ideengeschichte, Begriffsgeschichte, histoire des idées, etc.).

Kelley also edits *The History of Ideas: Canon and Variations* (1990), collecting major programme/critique texts.

### 2000 / 2005

Kelley's `JHI 2000` and 2005 farewell describe substantial continuity with Lovejoy's interdisciplinary and cross-cultural vision while acknowledging change through new fields (human sciences, gender, globalism, etc.).

Important interpretive warning:

`KELLEY RETROSPECTIVELY CALLS LOVEJOY'S VISION CROSS-CULTURAL`

is itself a historiographical claim to decompose historically, not a self-evident description of symmetrical global coverage in the 1930s–40s.

# X. 2006–26: ecumenical intellectual history, renewed self-history, and archive re-reading

### 2006 relaunch / institutional move

JHI moves to University of Pennsylvania and University of Pennsylvania Press with executive editors Warren Breckman, Martin Burke, Anthony Grafton, Ann Moyer.

The editorial statement defines intellectual history expansively across philosophy, literature/arts, natural/social sciences, religion, political thought, and emphasizes diversity of region, chronology and method.

Current Penn Press page:  
https://www.pennpress.org/journals/journal/journal-of-the-history-of-ideas/

### Anthony Grafton 2006

`The History of Ideas: Precept and Practice, 1950–2000 and Beyond`, JHI 67.1 (2006), 1–32.

This is one of the most important later histories of JHI/history-of-ideas practice. It emphasizes the journal's unusual midcentury status between disciplinary journals and its broader cultural visibility.

### 2010 Leo Catana

`Lovejoy's Readings of Bruno: Or How Nineteenth-Century History of Philosophy Was "Transformed" into the History of Ideas`, JHI 71.1 (2010), 91–112.

Catana argues Lovejoy's grand methodological declarations can function as rhetorical/institutional boundary-making and may describe actual practice imperfectly.

This is highly relevant to the present project because it supports **practice-before-programme / programme-vs-practice** analysis.

### 2014 Wickberg / later reassessment

Daniel Wickberg's work reconstructs Lovejoy's history of ideas as a form of cultural history/environment of ideas rather than reducing him to atomism.

Nico Mouton 2023 reopens Lovejoy's long-range method and directly challenges several standard caricatures, especially around logical relations, historical operation and recurrence.

These modern reassessments are the immediate historiographical field for the proposed article.

### JHI Blog 2014–present

The JHI Blog begins 2014/15 as an explicitly ecumenical, expansive public-facing extension of the journal.

Mission page:  
https://www.jhiblog.org/mission/

It now explicitly presents the JHI archive itself as an object of historiographical reconstruction through `Virtual Issues`.

Particularly important current retrospective:

`Reimagining the History of Ideas – The JHI in the Early Cold War` (2022), which interprets 1940–59 JHI as participating in anti-totalitarian/anti-communist political-intellectual formation and calls the journal in part an `anti-communist laboratory`.

https://www.jhiblog.org/2022/11/21/3-1-the-jhi-in-the-early-cold-war/

This is a major reminder that the early journal must be studied politically as well as methodologically.

Present-day JHI Blog also hosts explicit discussion of intellectual history from below, global history of ideas, nonhuman intellectual history, political economy, materiality, etc.

Thus the current self-description is not simply a preserved Lovejoyian method but **continuous expansion of what counts as an intellectual-historical actor, object, medium, and scale**.

# XII. Master methodological succession map

The strongest current sequence is **not** linear replacement. It is cumulative disagreement:

### Lovejoy early practice

`TYPED RELATIONS + SEPARATE WARRANTS`.

### Lovejoy 1935/40 programme

`DECOMPOSE`
→ `DISCRIMINATE COMPONENTS`
→ `TRACK MIGRATION / COMBINATION`
→ `RETURN TO HISTORICAL EVIDENCE`.

### Spitzer 1944

`COMPONENT PRESENCE / SIMILARITY`
↛ `HISTORICAL WHOLE / CONTINUITY / CAUSAL EXPLANATION`.

### Lovejoy 1944 reply

`REPEATABILITY + COMPOSITENESS = WORKING HYPOTHESES`
→ `VERIFY BY TEXTS`.

### Mandelbaum 1948/65

`RECURRENCE`
!=
`CONTINUOUS TRANSMISSION`;

`HISTORY OF IDEAS`
!=
`INTELLECTUAL HISTORY`
!=
`HISTORY OF PHILOSOPHY`.

### Pearce 1948/53

`ANALYTIC PIONEERING`
→ `SYNTHETIC CULTURAL PROCESS`
→ `IDEA / SYMBOL / IMAGE / ACTION`.

### Skinner 1969

`DOCTRINE / IDEA IDENTITY WITHOUT SPEECH-ACT CONTEXT`
= anachronism risk;
restore historical linguistic action/intention.

### Parekh / JHI 1973

Skinner's contextual method itself becomes contestable and insufficient as a universal solution.

### Richter 1987

American history of ideas is explicitly compared to German Begriffsgeschichte and alternative units of analysis.

### Kelley / Grafton / present JHI

The field becomes self-consciously plural in units, scales, media, regions, and methods.

Thus the historical object should be:

> **a long argument over what can count as an intellectual-historical unit and what evidence licenses movement from identity to continuity, causation, context, function, and action.**

# XIII. Cross-cultural / non-Western jurisdiction map

At least four historically different roles for non-Western material are now visible:

### R1 — internally reconstructed intellectual object

Lovejoy 1898 Buddhism: source/text/category relations reconstructed within an Indic/Buddhist scholarly object.

### R2 — comparative / primitive evidence field

1906 comparative religion / ethnography: heterogeneous living peoples and indigenous terms become evidence for a cross-cultural analytical type.

### R3 — supplementary comparative corpus

1935 Ancient India / Western Asia extend a primitivism project whose principal historical address remains classical/Western.

### R4 — antecedent / source in Western genealogy

Lovejoy 1933/48 `Chinese Origin of a Romanticism`: Chinese reports/missionaries/translations become causal/source infrastructure for European intellectual change.

### R5 — recipient / reaction / local transformation

By early postwar JHI, non-Western actors also become recipients/rejecters/reworkers of imported ideas (e.g. early 1950s China-related pieces already identified in prior sweep).

### R6 — present global intellectual history

Current JHI explicitly seeks diverse regional coverage; JHI Blog features global, colonial, Asian, African, subaltern and material histories.

The article should **not** collapse these into `cross-cultural history`. They are different historical jurisdictions.

# XIV. Political history layer now required

The JHI Blog's 2022 Early Cold War virtual issue introduces a major line that the current article architecture has not yet fully integrated:

> early JHI was also a political institution operating inside anti-totalitarian liberal and Cold War intellectual culture.

This matters because Lovejoy himself was a politically active liberal internationalist, anti-communist, academic-freedom organizer, and public intellectual.

Research implication:

`METHOD FORMATION`
+
`OBJECT SELECTION`
+
`EDITORIAL NETWORK`
+
`POLITICAL LIBERALISM / ANTI-TOTALITARIANISM`

must be tested together rather than assuming methodological debates are politically autonomous.

`primitivism`, `progress`, `nationalism`, `racism`, `human nature`, `totalitarianism`, `Marxism`, and `civilization` are especially likely bridge objects.
