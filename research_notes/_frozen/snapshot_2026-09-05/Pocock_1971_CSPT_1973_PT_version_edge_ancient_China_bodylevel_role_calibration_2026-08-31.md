## Core result

J. G. A. Pocock's `Verbalizing a Political Act: Toward a Politics of Speech`, *Political Theory* 1.1 (Feb. 1973), 27–45, supplies two different proposition-level controls which should not be collapsed.

1. The author's note directly says that **an earlier version** of the paper was delivered to the Conference for the Study of Political Thought at the City University of New York Graduate Center in April 1971.
2. The published article's opening directly invokes two schools in **ancient China**, identifying Confucians and Taoists as contrasting responses to the problems generated by verbal statement.

These establish two distinct relations:

`CSPT PRESENTATION VERSION -> LATER PT ARTICLE VERSION`

and

`NON-EURO-AMERICAN MATERIAL INSIDE ARTICLE BODY -> COMPARATIVE / THEORETICAL EXEMPLUM ROLE`.

Neither should be flattened into generic `network overlap` or a title-level globality count.

## I. Direct version-state edge

The printed author's note states that an **earlier version** was delivered to CSPT at CUNY Graduate Center in April 1971.

This is unusually strong because the author himself identifies:

- a prepublication state;
- a venue/institutional carrier;
- a date;
- a later published state.

Secure sequence:

`APRIL 1971 CSPT PRESENTATION — EARLIER VERSION`
->
`FEBRUARY 1973 POLITICAL THEORY ARTICLE`.

The relation is stronger than merely saying Pocock was a CSPT founder and later a PT author.

It is a **text-family / version-history edge**.

However:

`EARLIER VERSION`
!= `TEXTUALLY IDENTICAL VERSION`.

Without the 1971 script/notes, do not infer:

- which passages were already present;
- whether ancient-China examples were in the presentation;
- whether title/argument changed;
- what audience comments occurred;
- whether CSPT discussion caused any revision;
- whether editors solicited publication because of the presentation.

Core firewall:

`PRESENTATION -> PUBLICATION TRACE`
!= `DEMONSTRATED REVISION CAUSATION`.

## III. Ancient China is directly present inside the published article body

The opening of the 1973 article says that there have been schools of political philosophy founded on a rejection of the word and names two examples in ancient China.

Pocock then characterizes:

- Confucians as preferring shared ritualized patterns of behavior for communicating/internalizing values with fewer contradictions than verbal imperatives;
- Taoists as withdrawing from the fictions/confusions/lies generated by words toward an anti-politics of transcendence.

He uses the two as contrasting reactions to the difficulty of constructing verbal statements that cannot be refuted or distorted.

The relevant role is therefore not:

`INTERNAL HISTORY OF CHINESE POLITICAL THOUGHT`.

It is closer to:

`COMPARATIVE / THEORETICAL EXEMPLUM INSIDE A GENERAL POLITICS-OF-SPEECH ARGUMENT`.

This distinction matters for the regional-object census.

## VI. The juxtaposition itself is analytically useful

The opening moves rapidly among:

- a Los Angeles anecdote;

- ancient Chinese Confucians/Taoists;
- Popper/refutability;

- English treason law.

The point is not that these are treated as historically equivalent traditions.

They are assembled under a theoretical problem about speech/action/verbalization.

This is a different aggregation rule from an internally reconstructed history of one tradition.

`CROSS-CIVILIZATIONAL JUXTAPOSITION`
