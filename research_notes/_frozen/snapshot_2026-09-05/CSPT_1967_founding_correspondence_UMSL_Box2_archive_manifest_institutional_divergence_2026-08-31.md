## III. Founding motivation includes a publication-jurisdiction crisis

The archival reconstruction shows Richter worried that the behavioralist Austin Ranney's accession to the editorship of the *American Political Science Review* would make it more difficult for political theorists to publish there.

Other participants described alienation from APSA agendas and affinity with historical/humanistic venues.

Therefore the founding problem included:

`INTELLECTUAL / METHOD DISAGREEMENT`
+
`PUBLICATION ACCESS / VENUE JURISDICTION`
+
`PROFESSIONAL ISOLATION`.

This is a stronger causal architecture than `theorists disliked behavioralism`.

The perceived epistemic problem was already infrastructural.

---

## IV. Heterogeneous coalition: anti-behavioralism overrode internal method/political disagreement

Pocock, Richter and Wood were methodologically and politically heterogeneous.

Recent scholarship reconstructs the founders as:

- Pocock — linguistic/historical contextual reconstruction;
- Richter — comparative/history-of-political-thought trajectory;
- Wood — Marxist orientation.

They were personally/politically reluctant to recruit Straussian figures such as Mansfield and Cropsey, but pressure from Shklar/Walzer and the larger anti-behavioralist coalition led them to do so.

Mansfield described interest in an organization that might now need to `protect` the subject.

Thus:

`COALITION MEMBERSHIP`
was partly organized by
`SHARED EXTERNAL THREAT`
not
`INTERNAL METHOD HOMOGENEITY`.

This is important for interpreting later `schools`.

## V. Founders disagreed about disciplinary separation

This is the strongest substantive control from the Box 2 correspondence.

### Neal Wood

Envisioned CSPT as potentially prefiguring a new constellation of:

- financial institutions;
- departmental structures;
- professional organizations;

that could allow political theory to separate from political science.

He even reflected on the absence of a single name for the new discipline and asked Pocock for naming suggestions.

### Pocock, 18 Dec 1967

Rejected a general campaign to split departments or wage war on behavioralists.

His preferred tactic was essentially:

- continue doing their own work;
- insist on its **relatedness** to behavioralist work;
- fight only defensively.

This is a crucial direct actor-level relation rule:

`METHOD DIFFERENCE`
!=
`DISCIPLINARY INDEPENDENCE`.

It is strikingly consistent with the present project's anti-leakage rule:

`A != B` does not prove `A` is historically/functionally independent of `B`.

### Richter

The foundation-meeting report likewise indicates Richter had little enthusiasm for building a new separate discipline.

Thus the society's later infrastructure should not be retroactively read as the inevitable institutional expression of separatism.

## VI. The early manifesto defines a humanistic jurisdiction rather than one method

The 1968 CSPT programmatic document, as quoted in recent scholarship, argues that reducing political study to behavioral science abandons the domain where ethics and politics meet in discussion of purposes/goals.

It also gives humanism a privileged role in rational dialogue over political ends and permissible means.

This creates a broad jurisdictional claim:

`POLITICS AS BEHAVIORAL OBJECT`
!=
`POLITICS AS ETHICAL / NORMATIVE / HISTORICAL HUMAN ACTION`.

But the CSPT coalition contained multiple ways to study the second domain.

Hence the manifesto is more a **jurisdiction-protection document** than a unified method manual.

## VII. Institutional formation is a negotiation outcome

The founding case now supports a stronger infrastructure model:

`PERCEIVED DISCIPLINARY / PUBLICATION CRISIS`
-> `CORRESPONDENCE NETWORK`
-> `HETEROGENEOUS COALITION`
-> `NEGOTIATION OVER SEPARATION VS RELATEDNESS`
-> `SOCIETY / CONFERENCE INFRASTRUCTURE`
-> later `CHAPTERS / JOURNAL / BOOKS / NEWSLETTER`.

This differs from a simple model:

`COMMON METHOD`
-> `INSTITUTION`.

CSPT shows that shared infrastructure can precede, contain and sustain methodological disagreement.
