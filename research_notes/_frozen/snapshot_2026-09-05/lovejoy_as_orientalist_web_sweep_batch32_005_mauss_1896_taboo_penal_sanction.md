## Research move

Batch 31 used Van Gennep 1904 as a post-Marillier control for contagion, intrinsic efficacy and layered religious/social sanctions. The higher-value chronological bridge is actually earlier and closer to Lovejoy’s Paris work: **Marcel Mauss’s 1896 “La religion et les origines du droit pénal d’après un livre récent,” a long review of S. R. Steinmetz.**

This matters because the already established 1898–99 EPHE report says Marillier’s conference attended actively by Lovejoy **resumed and refuted Steinmetz’s objections** to Marillier’s earlier work on survivance and justice. Mauss’s 1896 article therefore exposes, in print and before Lovejoy’s Paris year, a large part of the controversy, source-base and analytical vocabulary that 005 pp.16–19 are visibly working through.

The strongest new result is not “Lovejoy read Mauss.” That remains unproved. It is stronger in a different way:

> **Mauss 1896 documents Marillier’s own source circulation and taboo argument inside the same controversy → Marillier’s 1898–99 seminar explicitly resumes Steinmetz → Lovejoy 005 pp.16–19 performs the same distinctions on concrete cases.**

## 1. Primary bibliographic control: Mauss 1896

Marcel Mauss, “La religion et les origines du droit pénal d’après un livre récent,” *Revue de l’histoire des religions*:

- vol. 34 (1896), pp.269–295, “Étude analytique”;
- vol. 35 (1896), pp.31–60, “Étude critique.”

Electronic full text: Les Classiques des sciences sociales / UQAM.

The article is a review of S. R. Steinmetz, *Ethnologische Studien zur ersten Entwickelung der Strafe* and turns into an independent argument about the relation among vengeance, public punishment, religion, taboo, clan reaction and ritual interdiction.

## 2. Mauss explicitly uses Marillier’s 1896 taboo dossier

The key footnote in the critical section is unusually concrete. In discussing Melanesian taboo sanctions, Mauss directs readers to:

> L. Marillier, `Tabou mélanésien`, pp.53–56 of the separate extract from *Études de critique et d’histoire religieuse* (EPHE, 1896), **where most of the texts relating to Melanesia proper are gathered**.

This identifies Marillier’s `Sur le caractère religieux du tabou mélanésien` as a source dossier rather than merely a theoretical essay: it gathered ethnographic texts that could be re-used in a comparative argument.

Bibliographic control for Marillier’s study:

- Léon Marillier, “Sur le caractère religieux du tabou mélanésien,” *Études de critique et d’histoire*, 2e série, Bibliothèque de l’École des hautes études, Sciences religieuses, 1896, pp.35–74.
- Persée/SUDOC authority records independently list the separate work, Paris, 1896.

This fits the already established material model of the Marillier workshop: thematic source aggregation, extraction, rearrangement and reuse.

## 3. Mauss also says he borrowed references directly from Marillier’s course

A second footnote makes the transmission mechanism even more explicit. Discussing the Tahitian taboo story reported by Gaussin and Ellis, Mauss says:

> he owes the two references to M. Marillier and **borrowed them from Marillier’s course on flood myths**.

This is direct contemporary evidence that Marillier’s teaching circulated **bibliographic references and source cases** into student/colleague research writing. It is especially important alongside Söderblom’s later statement that Marillier lent him personal note-files.

For the 005 paper, this means we can describe the EPHE setting more concretely as a research workshop in which **references, extracts, dossiers and arguments circulated across lecture/seminar and publication**. It still does not prove that Lovejoy used Mauss’s sheets or the identical references.

## 4. Mauss 1896: taboo violation can endanger the clan without being a moral-desert relation

Mauss’s section on `la sanction des interdictions rituelles` argues that violation of a taboo can threaten the existence/integrity of the clan and provoke immediate collective reaction. He explicitly treats exogamy/sexual prohibitions among the relevant cases.

The analytical structure is crucial:

- the offence is against a **ritual/religious interdiction**;
- the group reacts because its religious sentiments and integrity are implicated;
- the sanction can be immediate and collective;
- this is analytically separable from a later rational/moral theory of punishment.

This is exceptionally close to 005 PDF p.17, where Lovejoy’s legible fragments include:

- `moral fault`;
- `Violating Chastity`;
- `violation of taboo or ritual interdiction`;
- `natural order`;
- `ethnography`;
- the relation between taboo and `sentiment / value of chastity`.

The claim should remain operational rather than textual: **005 is performing the same distinction between moral evaluation and ritual-interdict mechanism that structures the Steinmetz–Marillier–Mauss controversy.**

## 5. Mauss 1896: contagion expands punishment beyond the individual

Mauss then asks why punishment may extend to the offender’s family, property or things that have touched him. His answer is explicitly that **taboo is contagious**. The violator can himself become taboo; objects and people associated through contact can be treated as dangerous and destroyed or excluded.

This provides a direct near-contemporary control for 005 PDF p.18 / folio 35. The notebook moves from suicide/murder to:

- an act against one clan member as an act against the clan as a whole;
- `exclusion` from social and religious rights/connections;
- blood;
- immediate or indirect `contact`;
- then back to afterlife placement.

The point is not that Lovejoy copies Mauss’s prose. The **problem-structure** is the same: how can sanction spread beyond an individual act, and is the extension produced by moral guilt, collective solidarity, ritual status or contagious contact?

## 6. Mauss 1896: ritual interdiction has its own supernatural efficacy

The article goes further than social reaction. Mauss writes that ritual interdiction can possess supernatural power `par elle-même`: touching taboo makes the individual taboo; transgression may be believed to cause death, illness or misfortune; the violator’s own superior magical power / `mana` can sometimes defeat the interdiction.

This is a major bridge from the **early 005 future-life / justice pages** to the **later 005 efficacy / sacrifice pages**.

The same notebook first asks whether consequences are generated by:

- moral desert;
- status;
- manner of death;
- ritual performance/interdiction;
- clan/social relation;
- contact/contagion;

and later asks whether ritual efficacy is:

- personal/animistic;
- intrinsic in an object;
- transferable;
- direct upon natural forces;
- quasi-mechanical.

Mauss’s 1896 article shows that `taboo`, contagion and supernatural efficacy already sat together in the immediate French controversy that Marillier resumed in 1898–99. This makes the continuity inside 005 much less accidental.

## 7. The Mauss–Marillier disagreement is useful, not noise

Mauss does not simply reproduce Marillier. His broader sociological argument pushes toward collective religious sentiment and the social origin of punishment, whereas Marillier’s own work often keeps more psychological/evolutionary explanatory machinery in play.

That disagreement helps the Lovejoy reconstruction. 005 repeatedly refuses to let one axis settle the evidence prematurely. At pp.16–19 it tests:

- `moral` vs non-moral determinant;
- missionary contamination vs indigenous account;
- social/clan sanction vs individual destiny;
- ritual interdict vs natural order;
- contact/blood vs culpability.

The notebook therefore looks less like passive uptake of one master’s doctrine and more like an **active classificatory response to a live controversy with competing explanatory levels**.

## 8. 005 p.19 confirms the source-critical side of the same operation

OCR and image review of PDF p.19 recover a Murray/Pawnee case and a methodological turn to contact chronology and missionary influence. The page asks how early a report is, whether later statements are compromised by missionary contact, and how to treat an apparently moralized future-life account when contamination is plausible.

So pp.16–19 form a coherent sequence:

> **p.16:** fate determined by ritual/social factors, with moral elements treated as later or mixed;
>
> **p.17:** chastity/suicide tested as moral fault versus taboo/ritual interdiction;
>
> **p.18:** murder/suicide, clan-wide relation, exclusion, blood/contact;
>
> **p.19:** source criticism, chronology and missionary contamination of moralized eschatology.

This is a compact example of the paper’s central claim: **classification of causal/normative mechanism and criticism of historical transmission are one operation in 005.**

## 9. Revised immediate genealogy

The tightest sequence now is:

> **Marillier 1893–94 survivance/justice memoir**
> → **Marillier 1896 Melanesian-taboo dossier**
> → **Mauss 1896 public use of Marillier’s dossier + direct borrowing from Marillier’s course, within the Steinmetz dispute**
> → **Marillier 1898–99 EPHE conference explicitly resumes/refutes Steinmetz; Lovejoy is an active auditor**
> → **Lovejoy 005 pp.16–19: source criticism + moral/ritual/social/contact decomposition**
> → **Marillier 1900 `Religion`: mana / sacred-impure contagion / mechanical versus psychological-moral efficacy**
> → **Lovejoy 005 later sacrifice/efficacy pages**
> → **Lovejoy 1906 `Primitive Energetics`.**

This is currently a stronger archival-intellectual chain than a generic claim that Lovejoy absorbed “French sociology” or “comparative religion.” It identifies a concrete controversy, named source packets, methods of circulation, notebook operations and later abstraction.
