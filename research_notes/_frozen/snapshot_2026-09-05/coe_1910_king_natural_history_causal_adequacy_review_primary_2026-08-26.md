# George A. Coe 1910 on Irving King — natural history, antecedent structure, and causal adequacy

Date: 2026-08-26
Status: **DIRECT PRIMARY / AMERICAN REVIEW-CONTROVERSY CONTROL**

## Core result

George A. Coe's full 1910 review of Irving King's *The Development of Religion* is now directly controlled. It changes the interpretation of the review environment behind King's 1911 restatement.

Coe does **not** primarily attack King's historical-priority claim about impersonal power. His deeper criticism is that King applies a natural-history/social-psychological programme in a way that risks making religion a merely derivative by-product whose own causal role and whose antecedent psychological structure are underdescribed.

The review therefore opens a second proof problem alongside the already controlled `comparative attestation ≠ historical anteriority` problem:

> **a successful genealogy of emergence does not by itself establish causal exhaustiveness.**

Or more concretely:

`religion can be generated from prior social/psychophysical conditions`

≠

`religion is only an inert by-product of those conditions and contributes no new causal organization`.

This is Coe's problem, not Lovejoy's formula. The structural comparison should not be converted into a method-transmission claim.

## I. Coe accepts the natural-history project as the shared starting point

Coe opens by saying that religion has a natural history and belongs within biology, psychology and sociology; this no longer requires argument. The question is whether the principle is applied consistently.

He reconstructs King's polemical target accurately: Max Müller's perception of the infinite, Jastrow's religious instinct, Tiele's innate sense of infinity, Brinton's postulated religiosity and theological revelation models all place an already formed religious consciousness into history instead of explaining its genesis.

King's project, as Coe presents it, is therefore genuinely genetic:

`pre-religious life`
→ `practices / ceremonial`
→ `religious consciousness`
→ `gods / higher religions`

under strictly natural conditions.

Coe regards this as a major and serious programme and praises King's anthropological range and trained psychological method.

## II. Coe accurately isolates King's action-first architecture

Coe identifies the central thesis as an `overt action` or reaction-first model. Religious states are generated from antecedent overt acts called forth by conditions of existence, especially social existence.

Religious practices initially need not have religious motive or aspiration. Accessory acts, emotional overflow, festival/play and activities tied indirectly to practical ends become consolidated through association and imitation. Religious reaction is therefore secondary to a social process originating outside a specifically religious need.

Coe's reconstruction yields the backward genealogy:

`individual religious consciousness`
→ `religious group-practice`
→ `primary social adjustment/reaction`
→ `undifferentiated non-religious social existence`.

This is useful for the American controversy because it shows that King's dispute with critics was not merely over isolated ethnographic examples. It concerned what explanatory level a natural history of religion should privilege.

## III. Coe directly flags King's impersonal-power inference

Coe gives a concise synopsis of King's `mysterious power` chapter: manitou/wakonda/orenda/mana are treated as an apparently universal early field of impersonal, quasi-mechanical power; animism requires a later mental capacity for spirit/person concepts.

He then raises a direct epistemic objection. If the primitive mind could not yet form the idea of personality, Coe asks whether King's repeated description of the mysterious power as **impersonal** may itself reflect the modern analyst's distinction-making rather than the undifferentiated condition of the historical object.

Thus Coe's objection is not simply `the category is false`.

It is:

`analyst can distinguish personal vs impersonal`

≠

`historical subject necessarily possessed or instantiated the distinction at the same resolution`.

This is particularly important for the later Jung 1928 control, where Jung accepts efficacy/power while objecting that a modern comparative abstraction has been projected back as an indigenous abstract `concept`. Coe 1910 is not a source for Jung and no genealogy should be claimed, but it proves that the analyst-category/native-object problem was already explicit in the American reception environment around King.

## IV. Coe accepts much of King's social genealogy but challenges causal exhaustiveness

Coe summarizes King's strongest generalization as follows: social life controls religious transformation; religions have no autonomous identity or continuous natural series; higher religions do not simply evolve out of lower forms; religion is the flowing product of discontinuous social forces.

Coe then presses the explanatory cost of this model.

In his reconstruction of King:

- religion is a product of psychophysical organisms reacting socially;
- no special instinct or primordial religious factor is required;
- religion becomes nearly a by-product or `aside` rather than a direct adjustment;
- gods symbolize values already achieved or anticipated;
- religion appears to have little independent causal work.

Coe asks whether such widespread and persistent religious activity can plausibly remain a mere by-product. If religion emerges from social action, one should expect it also to feed back into social development. Ancestor worship and Israel/Christianity are invoked as cases where religious organization appears itself causally constitutive of the group.

The criticism is therefore relational rather than simply theological:

`social processes → religion`

may be true,

but it does not establish

`social processes → religion with no reciprocal causal return`.

## V. Antecedent structure: genesis cannot begin from an empty organism

Coe's most technical criticism concerns King's starting datum: psychophysical organisms reacting overtly and socially to conditions of existence.

Coe grants that there may be no innate religious principle or `religious instinct`. But an organism necessarily has structure, including psychological structure. The specific form of later religious, artistic, scientific or political responses may require antecedent differences even if none is a fully formed innate idea.

Coe argues that King himself tacitly relies on such structure when discussing:

- the accumulated momentum of valued objects;
- `form` as conditioned by the structure of the worshipping body;
- individuality / directed variation in higher religions;
- ideal projection beyond actual social achievement;
- specific grounds for specific variations.

Thus King's anti-innatist programme cannot license treating the pre-religious subject as analytically structureless.

A useful reconstruction is:

`reject fully formed innate religious content`

≠

`reject antecedent psychological organization relevant to later religious differentiation`.

Again, this is Coe's criticism, not a Lovejoyian rule.

## VI. Coe's final verdict: natural history is being governed by a stronger metaphysical frame

Coe's last move is sharper. He argues that King's reduction of deities to functional symbols and religion to derivative valuation depends not merely on natural-history procedure but on an underlying metaphysical/epistemological position close to absolute empiricism.

For Coe, King's own standard of a complete scientific explanatory `system of definite relations` is too severe. He praises the book's facts and many interpretations while rejecting the ambition to make them form a closed explanatory system.

This makes the review controversy more complex than `evolutionary natural history versus theology`.

Coe accepts natural history. His objection is internal:

> naturalistic genesis can be valid without warranting ontological or causal exhaustiveness.

## VII. Relation to King 1911

King's 1911 `Some Problems in the Science of Religion` explicitly cites Coe's review and says reviews of the 1910 book revealed misunderstandings requiring restatement.

Current controlled review ecology therefore contains at least two partly different pressures:

### Coe 1910

- accepts natural-history programme;
- questions analyst-level `impersonal` discrimination;
- demands antecedent psychological structure;
- rejects religion as causally inert by-product;
- challenges the slide from genetic explanation into causal/metaphysical exhaustiveness.

### Leuba-side / King 1911 priority problem

- comparative impersonal-power field can be retained;
- `actually primitive` / historical priority becomes a stronger and more speculative proposition.

Do not collapse these into one criticism or assign every 1911 requalification to Coe.

## VIII. JHI-facing consequence

The American epilogue can now be described as a **multi-axis controversy over evidentiary and explanatory license**, not merely a reception chain around `primitive energetics`.

The two strongest axes are:

1. **attestation / category → historical priority**: does a widely distributed comparative pattern prove that it is historically primitive?
2. **genesis → causal exhaustiveness**: does showing how religion emerges from social/psychological antecedents prove that religion has no distinct downstream causal efficacy or relevant antecedent organization?

Both are problems about what a successful lower-level explanation licenses at a stronger level.

This is adjacent to, but must not be genealogically collapsed into, the notebook project's governing rule that a stronger proposition does not automatically inherit the warrant that established a weaker proposition.
