# Lovejoy’s Harvard Orientalist / comparative-religion training, 1895–1898

Status: **major evidentiary upgrade — direct training confirmed**  
Created: 2026-08-18

## Core discovery

The earlier working hypothesis that Lovejoy merely inhabited a strong Harvard Orientalist environment is now too weak. Daniel J. Wilson’s *Arthur O. Lovejoy and the Quest for Intelligibility* explicitly states, on the basis of a **list of courses taken by Lovejoy supplied by Harvard University Archives**, the *Harvard University Catalogue* for 1896–97, and Lovejoy’s October/November 1896 letters to his father, that Lovejoy actually took the relevant courses.

Wilson’s account says:

- comparative religion was a major secondary interest for Lovejoy at Harvard and continued into several early publications;
- he took courses with **Charles Carroll Everett**, including the psychological basis of religious faith and comparative religion;
- Everett’s comparative-religion course treated **Vedic religion, Hinduism, Buddhism, Mazdaism, and Chinese religions**;
- Lovejoy studied **Hebrew religion with Crawford H. Toy**;
- Lovejoy studied **Sanskrit and some sacred Buddhist texts under Charles Rockwell Lanman**.

Wilson’s note 44 cites:

1. “List of courses, supplied by Harvard University Archives”;
2. *Harvard University Catalogue*, 1896–97, pp. 73, 75, 99;
3. Arthur O. Lovejoy to Wallace W. Lovejoy, 8 October and 10 October 1896, and [October or November 1896].

This makes the academic year **1896–97** the key training window and turns the Lanman connection from circumstantial context into documented pedagogy.

## Why this matters

### 1. “Lovejoy as Orientalist” is no longer based only on one JAOS article

We now have a documented training sequence before the 1898 publication:

**Everett comparative religion → Toy Hebrew religion → Lanman Sanskrit/Buddhist texts → notebook 004 “Sankhya + Buddhism” → 1898 JAOS technical Pāli/Buddhist paper → 1901 “Philosophy of Buddhism” lectures.**

This is a coherent research-and-teaching trajectory rather than an isolated curiosity.

### 2. The range of the training helps explain both notebooks

Everett’s course range — Vedic religion, Hinduism, Buddhism, Mazdaism, Chinese religions — is almost a miniature map of the comparative horizon that later appears in Lovejoy’s early notebooks and publications. Toy supplies a direct background for notebook 005’s Hebrew religion / `nephesh` / `ruach` materials; Lanman supplies the strongest formal context for notebook 004’s Sanskrit/Buddhist technical apparatus.

This does not prove that every notebook page was produced for these classes. It does make it methodologically inappropriate to treat the Oriental/religion material as merely self-taught reading added to a philosophy curriculum.

### 3. Language competence must be described with precision

What is now secure:

- Lovejoy formally studied **Sanskrit** with Lanman.
- He worked on **some sacred Buddhist texts** under Lanman.
- His 1898 article works technically with Pāli terms and canonical editions.

What remains uncertain:

- how advanced his Sanskrit became;
- whether he had formal Pāli language instruction as opposed to reading Buddhist texts through Sanskrit/Pāli apparatus, dictionaries, editions, and translations;
- whether he could independently translate long Pāli passages without aids.

The defensible wording is therefore: **Lovejoy received formal Sanskrit and Buddhist-text training from Charles R. Lanman; his published Buddhist philology also engaged Pāli terminology and canonical texts, but the exact level and institutional form of his Pāli training remains to be established.**

## Primary/institutional corroboration found on the web

### Charles Rockwell Lanman

Harvard University Archives confirms that Lanman was Professor of Sanskrit at Harvard from 1880 to 1926 and that his papers contain teaching materials, correspondence, and Harvard Oriental Series records. Lanman and Henry Clarke Warren founded the Harvard Oriental Series in 1891.

Finding aid: `Papers of Charles Rockwell Lanman, 1825–1941`, HUG 4510.xx, Harvard University Archives.

The finding aid also points to:

- Records of Harvard’s **History of Religions Club**, 1892–1952;
- Lanman, *The King of Siam’s edition of the Buddhist scriptures and the Harvard copy of the first Sanskrit book ever printed*, reprinted from the Proceedings of the American Oriental Society, April 1895;
- Lanman’s Harvard Oriental Series records.

These are now direct follow-up targets.

### Lanman’s public Buddhism lectures, April 1896

The Harvard Crimson on 29 April 1896 printed the outline of the first of Lanman’s three Buddhism lectures. Its topics included:

- relation of Buddhism to antecedent Vedism/Brahmanism;
- relation to contemporary Sāṁkhya and Vedānta;
- practical purpose of Indian speculation;
- Sanskrit and Pāli sources;
- Tipiṭaka, its divisions and contents;
- Pali Text Society;
- King of Siam’s edition.

Because Wilson now confirms that Lovejoy was studying Sanskrit and Buddhist texts with Lanman, this lecture series is no longer merely an interesting coincidence. It belongs to the documented intellectual environment of an actual student of Lanman. Attendance at these particular public lectures remains unproved unless an attendance note or Lovejoy letter is found.

### Henry Clarke Warren

Warren’s *Buddhism in Translations* appeared as Harvard Oriental Series III in 1896, explicitly under Lanman’s editorship and dedicated by Warren to “My Friend and Teacher, Charles R. Lanman.” Lovejoy’s notebook 004 and 1898 paper repeatedly use Warren.

This places one of Lovejoy’s principal working books directly inside the Lanman pedagogical/institutional network in which he was studying.

### Charles Carroll Everett

Independent histories of the academic study of religion confirm that Everett taught comparative religion at Harvard Divinity School with a focus on religions of eastern and southern Asia, and also taught a long-running psychology-of-religion course variously titled “The Science of Religion,” “The Psychological Basis of Religion,” and “The Psychological Elements of Religious Faith.”

Wilson’s course evidence therefore fits Everett’s documented teaching portfolio rather than relying on retrospective inference.

### Crawford H. Toy

Toy was Harvard Professor of Hebrew in this period. Wilson’s course list explicitly places Lovejoy in Hebrew religion with Toy. This is especially important for notebook 005, which contains sustained notes on Hebrew eschatology, Sheol, `nephesh`, `ruach`, blood/life, and comparisons with Greek soul terminology.

## Biographical motive, according to Wilson

Wilson interprets these religion courses as central to Lovejoy’s effort to distinguish what in religion is historically determined from what might be universal. He says Lovejoy was strongly interested in the historical development of religion and sought the essential elements not only of Christianity but of religion in general.

That formulation should be treated as Wilson’s biographical interpretation, not automatically as Lovejoy’s explicit methodological manifesto. It is nevertheless grounded by Wilson in Lovejoy’s correspondence, including a letter to his father dated 3 April 1896.

## Extracurricular religious study

Wilson also records:

- Lovejoy’s membership in the Harvard Religious Union, which he described as an organization of the “religiously miscellaneous and undefined”;
- in fall 1897 he organized a small group to read and study Isaiah;
- participation in the Cambridge Conferences organized by Mrs. Ole Bull, where speakers included Hindus and others discussing ethics, history, or philosophy.

These activities enlarge the picture from coursework to a wider Cambridge religion/intellectual milieu.

## Revised evidentiary judgment

Earlier status: **formal Sanskrit/Pāli training unresolved; Lanman connection contextual only.**

Revised status:

- **Formal Sanskrit training under Lanman: confirmed.**
- **Study of sacred Buddhist texts under Lanman: confirmed.**
- **Comparative religion under Everett: confirmed.**
- **Hebrew religion under Toy: confirmed.**
- **Pāli as a separately enrolled language course: still unresolved.**
- **Direct attendance at Lanman’s April 1896 public Buddhism lectures: plausible but unproved.**

## Research consequence

The strict-core story can now be stated much more strongly: **Lovejoy was formally trained at Harvard in Sanskrit, sacred Buddhist texts, Hebrew religion, and comparative religion before publishing technical Buddhist philology in the Journal of the American Oriental Society.** Whether we label him “an Orientalist” without qualification is still a historiographical choice, but the underlying training and practice are no longer speculative.
