## p. 48 / ms p. 135 — Senart and the five skandhas

The right page is headed `Upādāna` and cites Senart's discussion of the twelve nidānas. The decisive passage is directly legible enough to correct the first-pass summary.

Image-secure core:

> `... at least 3 of the skandhas although included collectively under upādāna, appear separately in numbers 2 (saṅkhāra), 3 (viññāṇa), & 7 (vedanā); a fourth, rūpa, is at least directly recalled by nāmarūpa (4) ...`

The continuation then asks how the fifth, `saññā`, is represented by other terms in the formula; the exact lower wording and Pāli-definition quotation remain difficult.

### Required transcription correction

The current first-pass clean summary says that Lovejoy notes `separately enumerated forms of upādāna such as sensuality, false views, rites/observances and belief in self.` That is not what this passage is doing. The image shows that the relevant repeated items are the **skandhas**: Lovejoy is testing the hypothesis that the five skandhas are included collectively under `upādāna` while several of them recur separately elsewhere in the nidāna formula.

Correct conceptual summary:

> `Lovejoy tests Senart's collective-designation reading by asking whether the five skandhas are included under upādāna while at least three recur separately in the series: saṅkhāra, viññāṇa, and vedanā; rūpa is recalled through nāmarūpa, while the representation of saññā is argued through other terms.`

This is a substantive delta because it changes the object of the alleged repetition from `forms of upādāna` to `members of the skandha classification`.

## p. 49 / ms p. 137 — composite formula, non-homogeneous temporality, intelligible arrangement

This page is now one of the strongest direct manuscript controls in notebook 004.

Lovejoy first grants a great deal to the critical account. The image clearly supports the following concessions:

- the terms of the formula are not original to Buddhism but are brought together from pre-existing sources;
- objective and subjective elements are mingled;
- several logical interrelations are obscure;
- the sequence is not homogeneous in a strictly temporal sense. The exact overwritten wording around `strictly ... temporal` remains visually complicated, so the normalized proposition should be kept rather than over-diplomatized.

He then rejects the stronger conclusion. The right page is unusually explicit:

> `But it is entirely false to say that t. formula is illogical & unintelligible & th. t. various terms have been muddled together in an order more or less accidental.`

Immediately after this he writes that there is a `discoverable logical ... system` governing the arrangement and that `the repetitions are intentional`; one inserted word between `logical` and `system` is visually uncertain and should remain bracketed rather than silently normalized.

He continues that Senart's misapprehension is due to two errors, including missing the point in the arrangement of nidānas 3–7 and wrongly supposing `upādāna` to be a collective designation for the five khandhas.

### Interpretive consequence

This is more precise than saying Lovejoy merely defended the formula against a charge of inconsistency. He accepts **composite origin + mixed relation types + imperfect temporal homogeneity**, while still arguing for a reconstructable arrangement. The operative distinction is therefore:

`heterogeneous provenance / heterogeneous relations != unintelligible structure`.

That is exactly the kind of non-isomorphism already visible on pp. 41–43, now carried into an explicit Quellenkritik argument.
