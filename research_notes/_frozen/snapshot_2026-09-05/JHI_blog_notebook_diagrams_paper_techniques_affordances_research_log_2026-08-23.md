# Research log: Lovejoy's notebook diagrams, paper techniques, and affordances

Date: 2026-08-23
Project: Arthur O. Lovejoy, Buddhist philology, comparative religion, and the history of ideas
Status: image observations collated against existing transcriptions; mature working papers not yet located or inspected

## 1. Research question and change of emphasis

The central problem is not simply what Lovejoy thought about Buddhism, which authorities he read, or whether early notebooks contain visually interesting diagrams. It is how he took notes and what his particular combinations of page orientation, facing-page division, branches, brackets, parallel lines, numbering, lists, tables, deletion, and continuous prose made possible.

Working question:

> What did Lovejoy's ways of taking notes allow him to see, distinguish, and compare?

Historically cautious proposition:

> In the two inspected notebooks, Lovejoy repeatedly translated complex textual or ethnographic relationships into spatial arrangements that could be juxtaposed, subdivided, cross-referenced, revised, and tested against written argument.

An affordance is an analytical description of what a particular arrangement permits or facilitates. It is not proof that the arrangement mechanically determined a conclusion, that Lovejoy invented a unique method, or that one graphic tradition passed unchanged from medieval manuscript culture into twentieth-century intellectual history.

The analytical unit is therefore the **working page or opening**, including interactions between graphic arrangement and prose, rather than an isolated diagram treated as an illustration.

## 3. What the openings materially permit

### 3.1. Simultaneous visibility

Linear prose usually introduces terms successively. A diagram, bracket, table, or opening can keep several terms and relations visible together. The importance of this difference is especially clear when the same item belongs to more than one explanatory arrangement.

Safe description:

> Lovejoy's graphic arrangements made multiple classifications simultaneously inspectable.

Unsafe stronger description:

> The diagrams caused Lovejoy to invent relational thought.

### 3.2. Facing-page division

Pages 89, 121, and 149 visibly distribute work across an opening. A diagram or numbered outline on one side remains available while continuous prose occupies the other. On pages 89 and 121, the left-page graphics are oriented differently from the right-page prose.

This arrangement could support movement between a synoptic structure and a local argument. It does not by itself establish which side was written first, whether Lovejoy physically rotated the notebook at a specific moment, or whether this layout was exclusive to him.

### 3.3. Branches, brackets, and inclusion

Page 123 divides *nāmarūpa* into *rūpa* and *nāma* and groups further mental terms under *nāma*. The page's prose also states:

> “Viññāṇa is temporally (?) an antecedent of nāmarūpa, and logically a subdivision of it.”

This is the strongest current example of a graphic arrangement and an explicit interpretive claim operating together. The same term can occupy different positions because the relations differ:

- in one arrangement: temporal or causal antecedence;
- in the other: logical or classificatory inclusion.

The diagram is not merely a decorative illustration of a finished conclusion. It displays a configuration within which those incompatible-looking positions can be separately maintained and examined. Nevertheless, the precise causal order between drawing and writing is unknown.

Repository control: the upstream classifications are mediated by Childers and other Buddhist scholarship; the precise “temporally / logically” formulation has not been located in the inspected upstream sources. It may be identified as strong notebook-level authorial synthesis, not as a demonstrated priority claim.

### 3.4. Nonexclusive classification

Page 129 describes *saṅkhāra* through “52 divisions not mutually exclusive.” Numbering, horizontal ruling, juxtaposed English labels, and Pali terms permit an extensive set of potentially overlapping items to be tracked across a page.

This is important because a repeated item does not automatically establish that two appearances have the same semantic, logical, causal, or classificatory role. Conversely, the grid's apparent regularity should not be mistaken for proof that all categories were stable, mutually exclusive, or fully resolved.

Useful question:

> Does recurrence mark identity, overlap, a different classificatory level, or a distinct explanatory function?

### 3.5. Serial outlines and cross-mapping

Page 149 numbers the dependent-origination links while annotating their relation to the *khandhas*. The facing prose compares technical vocabulary and philosophical arrangements. The opening permits the same item to be located in a serial order and compared with another classificatory system.

This is a concrete material counterpart to the notebook's recurring distinction between temporal sequence, conceptual inclusion, causal contribution, and historical derivation.

### 3.6. Revision, deletion, and provisionality

The photographed pages contain cancellations, rewritten terms, added cross-references, abbreviated authorities, differently weighted marks, and unevenly distributed writing. These features show a working surface open to adjustment.

They do not independently establish the exact order in which every layer was written. Any future claim about sequence of inscription, inks, pencil pressure, erasure, or later additions requires image-level or physical examination.

## 4. Relation to Warren, Senart, and the problem of inference

The diagrams change how the existing disputes should be described. They do not replace textual source criticism; they identify the material setting in which relations were tested.

### Warren

Warren's own account treats *paccaya* as expressing more than one possible relation and requires individual links to be examined separately. He nevertheless assigns attachment and desire a relation of “identity.”

Notebook 004, manuscript page 75:

> “But not so if we are to follow [the] present text.”

The disagreement concerns the consistent application of an already shared relation-sensitive procedure. The graphic cross-mappings elsewhere in the notebook show one paper-based means of keeping competing relations available for scrutiny.

Do not claim that page 123 directly caused the objection on page 75 or that the two pages were produced in an established chronological sequence.

### Senart

Senart explicitly recommends:

> “l’analyse et la filiation des termes” (295).

He and Lovejoy therefore share significant genealogical and source-critical premises. Lovejoy disputes the specific semantic identification of *upādāna* with *upādānakkhandha* and resists the further conclusion that composite provenance or repetition establishes present unintelligibility.

The notebook's nonexclusive classifications and cross-cutting diagrams materially clarify why repetition alone does not settle identity or function.

Safe inference:

> Borrowed components and repeated classifications do not, without further semantic or functional evidence, establish accidental arrangement or absence of systematic relation.

Unsafe inference:

> Senart lacked a method and Lovejoy invented diagrammatic source criticism.

### *Upādisesa*

Lovejoy's 1898 article states:

> “Both derivations seem to be etymologically possible; the meaning of upadi must therefore be settled rather by an examination of its use than by etymological arguments.” (134)

Here the issue is the evidentiary competence of etymology versus textual usage. The graphic pages support a broader claim about note-taking and relation-management but do not show that this particular semantic judgment was itself produced by a diagram.

## 5. Ayelet Even-Ezra, Ann Blair, and Matthew Daniel Eddy

Ayelet Even-Ezra, *Lines of Thought: Branching Diagrams and the Medieval Mind* (University of Chicago Press, 2021):

- Publisher: https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/L/bo63098990.html
- Relevant analytical problem: how lines, branches, notation, and the surface of a page participate in organizing concepts, language, and argument.

Her work supplies an analytical comparison, not a demonstrated historical transmission. There is currently no evidence that Lovejoy directly inherited the medieval horizontal-tree tradition studied by Even-Ezra. Nor should all of his graphic forms be assimilated to a single tree-diagram model: his pages include brackets, parallel alignments, numbered lists, cross-references, grids, facing-page divisions, and cancellations.

Distinct uses of the secondary literature:

- Ann Blair: collaborative scholarly labor, books, and material conditions of intellectual work.
- Matthew Daniel Eddy: notebooks as active paper-based devices for handling information.
- Ayelet Even-Ezra: diagrammatic and spatial arrangements as practices through which relations can be formed, inspected, and revised.

None of these authors should be added merely as a prestige reference. Each must help explain a visible historical operation in the manuscript.

## 6. Cross-notebook comparison: 004 and 005

Notebook 004 concerns Buddhist terminology, philosophical systems, dependent origination, classifications, and textual authorities. Notebook 005 belongs to the Paris comparative-religion environment and engages ethnographic reports, funerary practices, sacrifice, agricultural rites, and competing historical explanations.

On 005 manuscript page 193, a small branching sketch juxtaposes cultivated plants, domestic animals, and sacrifice. The neighboring prose tests whether an explanation of agricultural sacrifice assumes an unsafe sequence between agriculture and domestication or produces a vicious circle.

What the comparison currently supports:

> Both notebooks contain spatial arrangements used alongside prose to examine relations, sequences, and classifications across heterogeneous evidence.

What it does not establish:

- that the same exact graphic method was used continuously throughout Lovejoy's career;
- that one notebook caused the other;
- that notebook 005 is a literal draft of the 1906 article;
- that cross-notebook similarity proves direct influence from any particular teacher;
- that a graphic similarity automatically entails an identical intellectual or political function.

The next useful comparison is between **operations**, not merely shapes: what gets divided, what gets related, what counts as a sequence, what remains simultaneous, what becomes an explanatory unit, and what gets omitted.

## 7. Affordances and limitations: open research matrix

| Paper operation | What the arrangement may enable | Possible limitation or analytical risk | Current manuscript control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Branching | Visible inclusion, subdivision, and alternative groupings | May force historically heterogeneous terms into a stable-looking hierarchy | 004, pp. 121, 123; 005, p. 193 |
| Bracketing | Temporary grouping across adjacent items | Can make a provisional classification appear settled | 004, pp. 121, 123 |
| Parallel alignment | Comparison of two or more sequences | Spatial correspondence can suggest semantic identity where none has been proved | 004, pp. 89, 121 |
| Numbered serial outline | Tracking sequence and cross-referencing components | May encourage temporal or causal readings of a merely classificatory order | 004, p. 149 |
| Multi-column inventory | Simultaneous tracking of numerous overlapping items | Regular rows may imply mutually exclusive or commensurable units | 004, p. 129 |
| Facing-page division | Maintaining a global structure while developing a local argument | Composition sequence and intentional page assignment cannot be inferred automatically | 004, pp. 89, 121, 149 |
| Deletion and rewriting | Revising labels and preserving traces of alternatives | Exact chronological layering cannot be reconstructed without closer inspection | Multiple supplied openings |
| Cross-domain comparison | Testing whether relation-management recurs across distinct materials | Can produce a false origin story or erase differences in object, context, and political stakes | 004, p. 123; 005, p. 193 |

The crucial point is that a form has no universally fixed affordance. A branching diagram can help preserve multiple relationships in one setting and impose an artificial hierarchy in another. The historically relevant question is what a particular mark or layout does in a particular argumentative context.

## 8. *The Great Chain of Being*: controlled publication comparison

### 8.1. What was checked

The local repository contains a page-structured OCR JSON of a 1966 edition of *The Great Chain of Being*, totaling **408 PDF pages**.

An audit of OCR layout labels returned:

- one `seal`, on PDF page 2;
- one `table`, on PDF page 3, representing a library “DATE DUE” card;
- two `image` blocks, both on PDF page 9, in preliminary matter;
- no OCR-identified substantive diagram, chart, or table in the body of the scanned edition.

This is a result for the **available scan and OCR layout detection**. It does not prove that every edition lacks illustrations or that OCR would successfully identify every handwritten or printed graphic form.

### 8.2. A primary-text occurrence of “diagram”

On PDF page 346, printed page **328**, Lovejoy describes a philosophical conception of the universe as furnishing empirical inquiry with:

> “a sort of diagram of the general pattern of the universe”

and enabling the observer to:

> “know in outline what to expect”

The surrounding passage discusses a rationally ordered world and the principles of plenitude and continuity.

This is **textual evidence that diagrammatic language belongs to Lovejoy's description of a historical worldview**. It is not proof that he drew such a diagram while drafting the book, nor that the passage directly describes his own note-taking procedure.

### 8.3. Four levels that must remain distinct

1. The cosmological structures attributed to historical actors.
2. Lovejoy's published analytical description of those structures.
3. Lovejoy's actual graphic practices in his surviving working papers.
4. Subsequent interpretations of *The Great Chain of Being* as a model of intellectual history.

These levels may interact, but they cannot be collapsed into one another without documentary support.

### 8.4. Open hypotheses, not findings

Possible questions for future work:

- Did mature working papers continue to use branches, brackets, cross-mappings, or tables?
- Did their units become larger, more portable, or less tied to named textual witnesses?
- Did graphic arrangements preserve incompatible relation types or reduce them to a dominant hierarchy?
- Did temporal succession, logical inclusion, historical derivation, and conceptual similarity remain separately marked?
- Were Asian traditions treated as structural participants, comparative instances, peripheral branches, or supplementary material?

None of these questions can presently be answered from the available mature publication alone.

### 8.5. Avoid a predetermined developmental story

Do not write:

> Early branching diagrams became the later Great Chain.

Do not write:

> Early diagrams were open and plural; later history was closed and linear.

Do not write:

> The absence of an illustration from the printed book proves the absence of diagrams from its drafts.

A defensible formulation is:

> The early notebooks permit investigation of the paper operations by which Lovejoy distinguished multiple kinds of relation; whether, and in what form, comparable operations survived in the preparation of *The Great Chain of Being* remains an archival question.

## 9. Political stakes: conditional extension

The question of affordances intersects with the existing primitivism argument but should not replace it with an unsupported formal analogy.

The relevant operations include:

- turning contemporary cultural difference into historical sequence;
- assigning living populations the evidentiary position of another population's earlier past;
- distributing explanatory authority between general theorists and regional specialists;
- treating certain archives as central and others as supplementary;
- converting repeated labels or resemblances into claims of historical identity.

In *Primitivism and Related Ideas in Antiquity* (1935), “chronological” and “cultural” primitivism are distinct analytical senses; their combination can place living peoples in the evidentiary role of earlier ancestors. The volume's main chapters and designated “Supplementary Essays” offer documentary evidence for a hierarchy in presentation and scholarly labor.

But no historical claim should be made that a specific notebook bracket, diagram, or list caused those political effects. The open question is how particular arrangements and classifications may permit, encourage, or conceal certain distributions of historical position and authority.

## 14. Concise research conclusion

The new evidence shifts the project from a history of extracted propositions toward a history of **note-taking as a material practice of comparative reasoning**. The strongest claim concerns what can be observed directly: Lovejoy used several page formats to hold different kinds of relationship in view, test whether repeated items were identical, distinguish sequence from inclusion, and revise comparative judgments.

The role of those practices in his mature work, and especially in the preparation of *The Great Chain of Being*, remains a sharply defined and potentially consequential archival question rather than an established developmental narrative.

## 17. Cross-field sweep: primitive as a problem of classification and institutional assignment

Date: 2026-08-23/24. This section consolidates a public-web sweep against the repository's already transcribed notebooks, existing research memoranda, full club register, and user-supplied OCR books. Its purpose is not to recast established repository findings as discoveries. It identifies where a new reading changes the evidentiary level, corrects a page range, or connects materials that had previously been treated in separate research tracks.

The question is how persons, historical materials, and explanatory claims were assigned across Buddhist philology, Indian philosophy, comparative religion, anthropology, and the institutionalized history of ideas. Four distinct uses of `primitive` require separate treatment: chronological priority; anthropological or evolutionary classification; the normative idealization of simplicity or origins; and the specialist jurisdiction to which an object or population was assigned. They can overlap historically, but no one occurrence automatically establishes all four meanings.

The strongest model is **uneven and changing incorporation**. Lovejoy studied Buddhist technical language, actively participated in a Paris seminar on peoples classified as uncivilized, wrote on `primitive philosophy`, used `primitive Buddhism` in his mature book, included a sustained China-to-Europe transmission argument in his collected essays, and helped build a research setting that could host an Indianist's presentation while placing that Indianist's eventual essay in a supplementary position. The resulting problem is the movement of materials between different evidentiary and institutional positions, not a simple presence/absence verdict.

## 18. Jastrow 1899: advanced Buddhism, primitive religion, and disciplinary jurisdiction

Primary witness:

- Morris Jastrow Jr., `The Historical Study of Religions in Universities and Colleges`, *Journal of the American Oriental Society* 20 (1899): 317–325, especially printed pp. 318–321: <https://www.jstor.org/stable/592338>.
- Existing repository control: `lovejoy_as_orientalist_web_sweep_batch65_Jastrow1893_1899_period_native_US_France_institutional_model.md`.

The earlier repository memorandum already identified Jastrow's comparative-philological genealogy and his distribution of specialist tasks. The additional reading isolates the preceding classification on pp. 318–319: Jastrow groups Buddhism with Christianity, Judaism, and Islam as `advanced religions`, then contrasts them with the religions of peoples in a `primitive condition of culture`. In his account, the latter appear more useful for investigating certain fundamental questions about religion's earliest formation.

On p. 321 he makes the institutional assignment explicit: Indian religions belong to an Indologist; the religious phenomena attributed to `primitive man` require an anthropologist. He names the Paris EPHE religious-sciences structure as an institutional model. The two claims must be read together: a religion may be treated as intellectually developed and still be assigned to a regionally or philologically defined specialist domain, while the anthropological category receives a different theoretical use.

This text does not say that Buddhism is culturally primitive. Nor does it exclude Buddhism from university teaching altogether: Jastrow also describes the educational relevance of major religions. The precise finding is a **differentiation of research function**, not a general claim of prohibition or contempt.

No evidence recovered here shows that Lovejoy read Jastrow's article, attended the specific meeting at which it was presented, or adopted its prescriptions. Jastrow is a period-native institutional witness, not an established personal source.

It does not prove that every contributor worked together, agreed about the meaning of `primitive`, or influenced Lovejoy directly. Washburn Hopkins's use of `primitive religion` should not be automatically converted into a claim specifically about Buddhism.

## 20. AOS membership, William James, and asynchronous Buddhist practice

The complete 1899 volume and its member lists can be checked at <https://www.scribd.com/document/289241022/Journal-of-the-American-Oriental-Society-vol-20-part-2>.

On printed p. 377, the corporate-member roll gives `ARTHUR ONCKEN LOVEJOY` followed by `1897`. Its introductory explanation identifies the number as the year of election. On printed p. 381, the roster for the Section for the Historical Study of Religions gives `WILLIAM JAMES` followed by `1899`; the annual proceedings also include James among the section's newly elected members.

These are different membership categories within the same umbrella society. They establish formal organizational proximity but do not show that Lovejoy and James attended one meeting together. Lovejoy was in Paris during much of the 1898–99 academic year.

Existing repository memorandum `lovejoy_as_orientalist_web_sweep_batch70_AOS_exit_window_1899_1903_Buddhism_teaching_membership_proxy_limits.md` further establishes that Lovejoy appears on the 1899 roll but is absent from the revised January 1903 roll. The safe interval is therefore **after the 1899 list and before January 1903**; an exact resignation date, nonpayment, or administrative removal has not been proved.

This chronology must be compared with the 1901 Buddhist syllabus and the 1902 reuse of Buddhist material. Institutional affiliation and continued intellectual or pedagogical practice do not have the same timeline.

The Society's own membership rules state that a corporate member need not be a professed Orientalist. Membership is therefore evidence of elected affiliation, not sufficient evidence of a stable occupational identity; disappearance from the membership roll is evidence of institutional change, not sufficient evidence of an intellectual break.

## 22. Paris: official participation, separate specialist tracks, and the Mauss control

The official EPHE report by Léon Marillier is <https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0002_1898_num_12_8_19501>. Its printed p. 33 explicitly names Lovejoy among the `Auditeurs ayant pris une part active aux travaux`. The category is distinct from `élèves titulaires`; the same report names both groups separately. Repository memorandum `lovejoy_as_orientalist_web_sweep_batch64_EPHE_status_active_auditor_vs_eleve_and_research_pipeline.md` records the exact distinction.

Consequently, a cautious web-only formulation such as `Lovejoy was in Paris and his notebook mentions Marillier` understates the available evidence. His **active participation in Marillier's conference work is officially documented**. Conversely, the document does not establish that he was a diploma candidate, formally enrolled as an `élève titulaire`, assigned a thesis, or responsible for any surviving seminar paper.

The same annual volume places Marillier's `Religions des peuples non civilisés` alongside the separate Indian-religion teaching of Sylvain Lévi and Alfred Foucher. The juxtaposition provides an actual institutional counterpart to Jastrow's proposed division between anthropological and Indological jurisdictions; it does not mean the tracks never interacted.

The EPHE's official prosopography reports that Marcel Mauss studied with both Marillier and Sylvain Lévi in 1895–1897: <https://prosopo.ephe.psl.eu/marcel-mauss>. Mauss's educational path documents a bridge across the same teaching environment. It does not prove that Mauss and Lovejoy met, attended the same year's classes, or transmitted a particular proposition to one another.

Wilson's intellectual biography further reports Lovejoy's private reading of Buddhist texts with Sylvain Lévi, drawing on Lovejoy family letters. Until the underlying letters or Wilson's transcription are inspected, the claim should be attributed to Wilson rather than represented as a newly read first-person letter.

## 24. The semantics of primitive: five documentary moments

Five texts need to be compared without conflating their vocabularies:

**1898 Buddhist article.** The full text of `The Buddhistic technical terms upādāna and upādisesa` contains no occurrence of `primitive`: <https://dlbs.liberal.ntu.edu.tw/FULLTEXT/JR-ENG/love.htm>. Lovejoy instead argues that borrowed terms or ideas can have an `original and characteristic` arrangement and application. This is an article-specific lexical finding, not a claim about his entire later oeuvre.

**1899 Jastrow.** Jastrow calls Buddhism `advanced` within a comparison of religious development while designating certain ethnographically described living populations as culturally `primitive`. His classificatory distinction bears on the assignment of objects to Indologists and anthropologists.

**1906 Lovejoy.** `The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy`, *The Monist* 16 (1906): 357–382, reconstructs a causal category from ethnographic reports concerning living populations. This use is actor-attributive and anthropological: the analyst claims to recover a more fundamental conception allegedly present in the thought being described. The repository already holds a direct article-level comparison and should not substitute later mana scholarship for the primary text.

**1935 Lovejoy and Boas.** `Chronological primitivism` and `cultural primitivism` are explicitly distinguished as historical-analytical categories. The former concerns the location of value at an earlier historical moment; the latter concerns the comparative valuation of simpler social life. Neither is interchangeable with every occurrence of the adjective `primitive`.

**1936 Lovejoy.** The repository's scanned 1966 printing of *The Great Chain of Being* uses `primitive Buddhism` on printed pp. 30, 97, and 218. The first two passages compare early Buddhist otherworldliness and doctrinal refusal; p. 218 compares Buddhist pessimistic formulas with a structurally similar argument for optimism. These are direct primary witnesses, and they block any claim that Lovejoy never applied the adjective to Buddhism.

`Advanced religion` in Jastrow and `primitive Buddhism` in Lovejoy do not automatically contradict one another: one can concern a religious tradition's civilizational status within a contemporary research taxonomy, while the other refers to its early historical stratum. Both usages require contextual examination; neither can be silently assimilated to the other or treated as politically innocent by definition.

The mature book also contains an explicit `indigenous ... Occidental` versus `imported Oriental` distinction on p. 35 and a racialized characterization of Indian imagination on p. 142. Those passages show that a historically temporal use of `primitive Buddhism` coexists with civilizational hierarchies elsewhere in the same work; the analysis must preserve both rather than flatten either.

## 25. China, Classics, Lois Whitney, and the limits of an exclusion model

Lovejoy's `The Chinese Origin of a Romanticism` first appeared in 1933 and was revised in *Essays in the History of Ideas*, printed pp. 99–135. Its China-to-Europe argument is substantial, and printed p. 111 expressly records a Chinese-language consultation undertaken by Y. Z. Chang at Lovejoy's request. The essay refutes a blanket claim that Lovejoy excluded all Asian materials from intellectual history. Its framing still centers the formation of a European aesthetic, so the relevant historical question concerns how Chinese knowledge and specialist labor enter the argument.

The Society for Classical Studies lists Yale Sanskritist William Dwight Whitney as its 1870 president and Harvard Sanskritist Charles R. Lanman as its 1890 president: <https://classicalstudies.org/about/past-presidents>. Harvard initially located Sanskrit within Latin electives before creating a separate Indo-Iranian institutional home. Classics and Indology were therefore historically entangled even while their objects and positions were being differentiated.

Lois Whitney must also remain distinct from William Dwight Whitney. The club register records her presentation on primitivism in 1924, and her sole-authored book *Primitivism and the Idea of Progress in English Popular Literature of the Eighteenth Century* appeared in 1934 with a Lovejoy foreword. This places a woman scholar's independently authored work inside the prehistory of the Lovejoy–Boas volume. It does not establish that either author originated the other's categories.

These cases make `institutional exclusion` too coarse as a general explanatory label. Oral participation, authorship, specialist consultation, editorial control, book structure, bibliography, and retrospective canon formation are materially different positions.

## 26. Historiography, originality, source identities, and JHI Blog use

### Existing scholarship and specialist memory

Erhard Schüttpelz, Irene Albers, and Andreas Schmid, `Sur la définition du primitivisme littéraire`, *Gradhiva* 38 (2024): 154–167, already place Lovejoy and Boas's primitivism project at the center of early American history of ideas, note its ancient West-Asian and Indian supplements, distinguish chronological from cultural primitivism, and connect the project with *The Great Chain of Being*: <https://journals.openedition.org/gradhiva/8458>. The French publication translates an argument first published in a 2013 German collection and adds a later introduction; its date must not be treated as the first appearance of every underlying claim. Its `433–447` reference also reproduces the one-page overextension corrected against the repository witness above.

Nicolas Meylan's *Mana: A History of a Western Category* already discusses Lovejoy's 1906 place within mana historiography: <https://relbib.de/Record/892982985>. Bhattacharya 2000 had already noticed Dumont's explicit Buddhist/Jain omission. Daniel J. Wilson 1982 catalogued both the 1898 article and the 1901 syllabus. None of these facts should be presented as entirely new to scholarship.

At the same time, Lovejoy's Buddhist contribution was not uniformly forgotten. Amrita Nanda, `The Stage of Non-returning`, in *The Theory of the Four Stages of Liberation in Pāli Literature* (2024), explicitly cites `Lovejoy (1898:126–36)` in note 28: <https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-55873-3_3>. The useful historiographical distinction is **differential memory across fields**: specialist Buddhist scholarship, Lovejoy bibliographies, mana histories, and histories of primitivism retain different parts of the same scholarly career.

The defensible originality claim is the conjunction of materially and institutionally specific witnesses: notebook 004's source-critical and graphic operations; the 1898 Buddhist controversy; the 1897 AOS election and bounded later membership exit; Jastrow's 1899 juxtaposition of `advanced` Buddhism, `primitive` anthropological material, and specialist jurisdiction; the official EPHE record of Lovejoy's active participation; the 1901 Buddhist syllabus preserved in a mature collection's bibliography; the 1906 anthropological paper; the 1934 Dumont club presentation; the page-446 Buddhist/Jain omission; the China counterexample; and the mature book's own temporally inflected `primitive Buddhism`.

The public web search did not locate a published study that connects all these page-locked objects in this way. That is a bounded statement about the materials searched, not proof that no inaccessible dissertation, archival study, or paywalled article exists.
