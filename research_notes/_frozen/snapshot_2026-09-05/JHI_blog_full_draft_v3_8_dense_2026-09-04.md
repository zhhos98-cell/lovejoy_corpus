# What Was Arthur Lovejoy Doing with Buddhism?

Status: **DENSE REWRITE / v3.8 / JHI BLOG**
Date: 2026-09-04

The bibliography appended to Arthur O. Lovejoy’s *Essays in the History of Ideas* gives him an odd beginning. Its first entry is a 1898 article on two Buddhist technical terms. Lovejoy had already published on Lord Monboddo in 1895, so the bibliography creates a false start. The false start is useful. It places a small piece of Buddhist scholarship at the edge of a career later organized around the history of ideas.[^1]

That Buddhist work belonged to a crowded training sequence. Lovejoy began Sanskrit at Harvard in 1895–96. In 1896–97 his graduate record lists `Sanskrit 5`, grade A. The course announcement identifies course 5 as Charles Rockwell Lanman’s advanced Pāli class, “The Sacred Books of Buddhism,” with the *Dhammapada*, *Sutta Nipāta*, and *Majjhima Nikāya*. The same record gives him an A in Crawford H. Toy’s course on Hebrew religion and comparative Semitic religion. In April 1897 the American Oriental Society announced a paper by Lovejoy on the *Milinda-pañha*. In 1897–98 he also took Anthropology 1 and received an A. A year later, in France, he described his problem with unusual clarity. Philologists knew the texts and often lacked philosophy. Philosophers reconstructed Buddhism while neglecting textual technicalities. Lovejoy wanted both. He hoped to work with Sylvain Lévi and expected to read Buddhist texts with him privately when no Pāli course was offered.[^2]

The surviving notebook labeled “Sankhya + Buddhism” shows what that combination looked like in practice.[^3] It is full of Rhys Davids, Warren, Childers, Oldenberg, Senart, Burnouf, and other authorities. Their claims sit beside lexical notes, comparisons, questions, and local interventions. The interest lies in the points where Lovejoy changes the relation among inherited materials. Henry Clarke Warren treated *taṇhā* and *upādāna* as equivalent. Lovejoy stopped over the equation and appealed instead to “the present text.” He then compared four kinds of attachment with six forms of desire. Émile Senart supplied a larger argument about the composite character of the twelve *nidānas*. Lovejoy accepted much of the historical diagnosis and then rejected the inference that the arrangement was therefore accidental. “There is a discoverable logical system,” he wrote. On another page he asked, in a line signed `A.O.L.`, whether an alleged historical development required any “ontological function” at all.

The notebook keeps changing scale. On manuscript p. 123 Lovejoy wrote that *viññāṇa* is “temporally (?) an antecedent of nāmarūpa, and logically a subdivision of it.” On the same page, *viññāṇa* also sits inside the *nāma* side of a classification. Temporal sequence and logical inclusion remain separate relations. A few pages later the question changes again. Lovejoy tests lexical derivation against actual Buddhist usage. He compares terms that differ in origin and asks when they converge in systemic function. Near the end of the notebook he writes, “This is all that is required for the purposes of my paper.” The sentence closes a larger philosophical question because the immediate paper needs less. Another page sorts “Original Elements” from “Derived Elements.” The list is historically speculative, yet its form is revealing. Lovejoy keeps asking what kind of claim a given piece of evidence can carry.

The route into print is now unusually visible. On 10 April 1897 the American Oriental Society’s final circular announced Lovejoy’s “Critical summary of the argument of the Milinda-pañha.” At the Baltimore meeting later that month, where Lovejoy was absent and his communication was read by title, the Proceedings recorded a different object: “On the meaning of the Buddhist technical terms” *upādāna* and *upādāna-kkhandhā*.[^4] In 1898 the published article became “The Buddhistic Technical Terms Upadana and Upadisesa.” The second technical term had changed. The late-April title points directly into the notebook’s sustained argument over *upādāna* and the *upādānakkhandhas*. The published article keeps that problem in its opening pages, then turns to *upādisesa*. Three documentary states therefore preserve three public objects within little more than a year.

The printed article also shows what survived the reduction. Lovejoy grants that Senart’s terms, “perhaps even the ideas,” may have been borrowed, while their “arrangement and application” could remain characteristic. He treats usage as stronger evidence than etymology for *upādisesa*. He retains awkward counterexamples. He ends with the issue still open. The movement from notebook to article is selective. A broad dossier of textual, semantic, causal, and historical problems becomes a short publication built around a few claims that could survive public argument.

A second notebook, now catalogued with a strong Paris 1898–99 core, widens the field.[^5] Its pages move through sacrifice, blood, fetish, magic, afterlife, agrarian rites, social differentiation, and the transfer of efficacy. Marillier, Robertson Smith, Frazer, Tylor, Trumbull, Mannhardt, and ethnographic reports supply much of the vocabulary and many of the cases. Lovejoy’s work appears in the handling of these materials. On one page he ranks sources by evidential restraint. On another he reclassifies a cannibalism case as alimentary rather than ritual. Elsewhere he separates fused ritual rationales, asks which social bearer can perform a mechanism, tests a genealogy against chronology, rejects a substitution sequence, and leaves rival explanations open. The pages themselves are crowded with inserted sheets, folded slips, lists, schemes, cancellations, and branching hypotheses. The notebook works by rearrangement as much as by accumulation.

Several examples show the point quickly. A differentiated fate after death can arise from status, manner of death, taboo, ritual provision, social relation, or moral desert. A blood rite can be propitiatory, alimentary, incorporative, magical, funerary, or something else. A visible resemblance between two practices can support comparison while leaving genealogy unsettled. One branching scheme links cultivated plants, domestic animals, and sacrifice, then immediately raises a chronological objection. Another passage rejects circumcision as a simple survival of substitute human sacrifice. At a later point Lovejoy records uncertainty between rival mechanisms. The notebook repeatedly turns large inherited classes into smaller questions about mechanism, bearer, sequence, and evidence.

This early work did little to lock Lovejoy into one scale. In “Religion and the Time-Process” (1902), he called the affinity between Aristotle’s theology and Vedānta “obvious” and still rejected eastern influence when an internal Greek route was available. Resemblance and transmission remained separate problems. In 1906 he used the same habit in several directions. “Kant’s Antithesis of Dogmatism and Criticism” tested a large inherited category against local propositions and discarded it. His review of Paul Elmer More returned to the *Sutta Nipāta* to resist a positive reconstruction of Nirvāṇa. “The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy” moved the other way. It gathered heterogeneous ethnographic and lexical materials into a broad claim about impersonal, communicable efficacy in “primitive philosophy” and “savage thought.” The same year, “Democracy in the Twentieth Century” divided Enlightenment “generic Man” by stage of culture and by race or nationality, then carried those distinctions into colonial policy and franchise questions.

That political essay matters because fine distinctions carry consequences. Lovejoy’s classificatory work could restrict an inference in one setting and authorize a larger social judgment in another. The issue is therefore larger than caution. It concerns scale. A relation separated at the level of a text can later become part of a claim about a religion, a people, a stage of culture, or a historical world. The notebooks show the small operations. The later essays show several possible destinations.

By the time Lovejoy published *The Great Chain of Being*, Asian materials still appeared, but their job had changed. The book defines its main field through “Occidental philosophy” and “Western thought.” Buddhism and Vedānta remain as comparative forms and boundary cases. In the 1890s notebooks, Buddhist and Indian materials were local objects of philology, semantics, causation, and genealogy. In *Great Chain*, they more often serve a Western historical argument from the side.

The early Buddhist episode therefore offers more than an eccentric prehistory. It shows Lovejoy at work before his career acquired its familiar retrospective shape. He reads other scholars closely, cuts inherited problems into smaller relations, limits some claims, recombines others, and changes the scale again in print. The surviving pages also show how much of that labour belonged to shared scholarly worlds. Warren, Senart, Oldenberg, Childers, Marillier, Frazer, Tylor, and others remain inside the archive. Lovejoy’s part often appears in the small connective acts among them. Those acts later support both restraint and generalization. The strange first item in the *Essays* bibliography becomes less strange once the notebooks are read at their own scale.
