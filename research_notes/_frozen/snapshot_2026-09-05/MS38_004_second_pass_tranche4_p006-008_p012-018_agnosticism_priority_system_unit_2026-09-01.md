## 1. PDF p. 6 / ms p. 21 — substantive transcription correction

The heading `Buddh. Agnosticism` is clear. The source line names the `Sabbasava Sutta` of the Majjhima Nikaya in abbreviated form.

The first-pass clean file previously had the nonsensical phrase:

> `First of F. several is F.`

Direct image control instead supports:

> `1. first source of all āsavas is t. “considering of things wh. ought not to be considered.”`

This is a genuine technical-term correction, not merely smoother paraphrase. Lovejoy is identifying a **source of all āsavas**, and immediately follows it with the series of questions about past, future, and present self-existence.

The correction has been propagated to the authoritative clean JSON.

### Consequence

The local topic is more exact than a generic note on agnosticism. Lovejoy is reading speculative self-questioning through a technical Buddhist causal/moral vocabulary: improper consideration is treated as generative of `āsavas`.

This does not yet license a claim that he has reduced philosophical error to one psychological cause. It does show that the notebook is relating **question-form / cognitive operation / doctrinal consequence** rather than merely collecting anti-metaphysical sayings.

## 2. PDF pp. 7–8 / ms pp. 23–25 — anti-philosophical appearance versus practical selectivity

P. 7 continues the six heretical notions and then turns to the `Sutta Nipata`, especially the `Atthaka-vagga`. Some syntax in the first sentence of this transition remains difficult even under direct image control and should stay HOLD in the clean transcription.

What is secure is the argumentative direction:

- Lovejoy considers passages that can look anti-philosophical;
- he allows that much of the rhetoric can be read as contempt for `false & fruitless reasoning` and disputation pursued for its own sake;
- he then notes more extreme formulations, including S.N. 911 and the refusal to identify the sage by `opinion`, `tradition`, or `knowledge`;
- he explicitly says these must be taken together with other passages.

P. 8 supplies the counter-control under the heading `Buddh. indiff. to theoretical kn.`: the Siṃsapā-leaves comparison is used to distinguish truths not taught because they do not conduce to turning away from earthly things, suppressing desire, and purifying intelligence.

### Consequence

Already by pp. 7–8 Lovejoy is not treating Buddhist agnosticism as a flat absence of theory. He is testing a **selection rule for knowledge**: some inquiry is rejected because of its relation to liberation, attachment, or practical efficacy.

The stronger formulation appears only on p. 18 and should not be back-projected mechanically into pp. 7–8. The local sequence nevertheless prepares it.

## 3. PDF pp. 12–14 / ms pp. 53–57 — chronology without retrojecting a finished system

P. 12 turns from impermanence notes to the heading:

> `Philos. Material & Comp. Date of Sankhya & Buddhism.`

Lovejoy copies a secondary claim according to which Sāṃkhya logically stands between Vedānta and Buddhism and could have functioned as a stepping-stone; the same passage invokes Kapila and the alleged chronological priority of Sāṃkhya.

P. 13 then shifts out of quotation/secondary claim into explicit source criticism. The key sequence is visually continuous and clear:

- the chronological point is `most doubtful`;
- all extant Sāṃkhya texts are much later;
- even if a worked-out system can be pushed earlier than the surviving texts, there remains `a great gap to be bridged over`.

The sentence is broken materially by the page turn. P. 13 ends:

> `On weighing all t. evidence it seems to me t. only conclusion`

and p. 14 begins with the conclusion itself:

> `to be rightly drawn is th. there is no evidence th. Kapila was t. real author of t. school, but th. there were, bef. t. time of t. Buddha, isolated thinkers of whose works we have no trace, who elaborated views similar to those out of wh. t. Sankhya was eventually developed.`

### Material-form observation

This page break matters. The manuscript physically separates:

`EVIDENTIARY GAP / DOUBT`

from

`MINIMUM LICENSED HISTORICAL CLAIM`.

The minimum claim is not `the Sāṃkhya system existed before Buddhism`. It is that **isolated thinkers** may have held views similar to components from which the later system developed.

### Consequence

This is direct evidence for a source-critical distinction among at least three historical units:

1. isolated thinkers / unattested antecedents;
2. individual views similar to later Sāṃkhya doctrines;
3. the Sāṃkhya `school` or `system` as a worked-out whole.

Chronological priority of a component does not establish chronological priority of the finished system.

That distinction is manuscript-explicit in practice even though Lovejoy does not name it as a general historiographical rule.

## 4. PDF pp. 14–16 / ms pp. 57–61 — detached doctrines are not separate systems

Immediately after the chronology conclusion, p. 14 pivots to the sixty-two Brahma-jala / Digha-Nikaya heresies and begins an extended classification.

P. 15 contains the most important local methodological sentence for this block:

> `It is to be noted th. these do not all represent separate systems, but detached doctrines; many of them might or might not be combined w. many of t. others.`

Lovejoy then adds that Buddhism `as a system` regards them all as false, `and that for a coherent or systematic reason`.

### Material-form observation

The manuscript page itself changes analytical format:

- pp. 14–15: numbered taxonomy of positions;
- bottom of p. 15 into p. 16: compression of the taxonomy into a positive statement of Buddhist positions;
- p. 16: numbered synthesis rather than source transcription alone.

The page is therefore performing a rescaling operation:

`62 ITEMIZED POSITIONS -> DETACHED DOCTRINES -> POSSIBLE COMBINATIONS -> SYSTEMATIC BUDDHIST RESPONSE`.

This is stronger evidence than a prose summary that simply says Lovejoy read the Brahma-jala.

## 5. PDF pp. 16–17 / ms pp. 61–63 — testing and rejecting a whole-system identification with Sāṃkhya

P. 16 tentatively aligns one of the Brahma-jala positions with Sāṃkhya and copies the language about an eternal self and eternal world/matter.

P. 17 then performs the control test. Lovejoy notes:

- the Sāṃkhya technical term `Prakriti` is not used;
- the vague word `world` might be read as `orig. matter`, but this is only a possibility;
- a contradiction follows: in Sāṃkhya, visible world and living beings are produced from original matter and individual souls, whereas the heretics in the quoted passage hold that self and world `give birth to nothing`.

He then states, in a sentence that is direct-image secure in substance:

> `I am at a loss to understand how t. passage can be considered good evidence th. t. Sankhya system existed as a whole, just as we find it in t. many centuries later.`

Lovejoy subsequently distinguishes the eternity of Matter and Self from what he takes to be Sāṃkhya's more central doctrine, the `separateness of t. Self & t. World`, its `thorough-going dualism`. He identifies a more likely genuinely Sāṃkhyan parallel in Majjhima-Nikaya Sutta 63:

> `t. soul is one thing & t. body is another`

### Consequence

This is the clearest control in the early notebook block for the proposition:

`DOCTRINAL RESEMBLANCE != EVIDENCE FOR AN ALREADY-EXISTING SYSTEM AS A WHOLE`.

The reasoning is not merely chronological. Lovejoy checks:

- technical vocabulary;
- causal/ontological compatibility;
- central versus peripheral doctrine;
- level of historical unit being claimed.

He can accept a more limited doctrinal parallel while rejecting the stronger system-identity claim.

## 6. PDF p. 18 / ms p. 65 — agnosticism re-described as an objection to question-form

After summarizing ten disputed propositions, Lovejoy reduces them to four subject areas:

1. infinity;
2. eternity of the world;
3. relation of soul to body;
4. future life.

He then explicitly revises the apparent reading of Buddhist agnosticism. It may look at first like the `philosophical agnosticism of a purely practical teacher` who simply despises such questions. But closer scrutiny, he says, shows that this is not necessary. The refusals to assert are based on an objection to the **form of the questions put**, not merely the impatience of a `common-sense` moralist. Buddhism is itself `a doctrine of salvation based on insight`.

### Consequence

The pp. 6–8 material can now be read more exactly through the p. 18 control:

`REFUSAL TO ANSWER != ABSENCE OF DOCTRINAL STRUCTURE`.

The refusal can depend on the way a problem is formulated and on the relation between a proposition and the system in which it is being asked.

This is manuscript evidence for **question-form sensitivity**, not a license to identify a mature later method.

## 7. Combined result of this tranche

The strongest source-level result is a recurring distinction among analytical scales:

`VIEW / DOCTRINE / QUESTION`

is not automatically identical with

`SCHOOL / SYSTEM / HISTORICAL WHOLE`.

Across pp. 12–18 Lovejoy repeatedly resists upgrading partial resemblance into system identity:

- possible antecedent views do not prove an early fully formed Sāṃkhya;
- detached Brahma-jala doctrines do not each constitute separate systems;
- a passage resembling one Sāṃkhya proposition does not prove that `the Sankhya system existed as a whole`;
- Buddhist refusal to answer certain propositions does not by itself prove simple anti-philosophical agnosticism.

A compact manuscript operator is therefore:

`WHAT IS THE UNIT OF THE CLAIM? -> WHAT FEATURES ACTUALLY MATCH? -> WHAT STRONGER WHOLE-LEVEL INFERENCE IS NOT LICENSED?`

This should remain an interpretive description of Lovejoy's working practice, not be converted into a claim that he had already formulated the later unit-idea method.
