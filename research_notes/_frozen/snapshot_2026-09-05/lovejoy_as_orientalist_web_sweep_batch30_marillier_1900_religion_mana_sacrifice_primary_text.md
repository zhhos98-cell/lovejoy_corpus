This electronic text must still be checked against the public-domain facsimile (`Grande Encyclopédie XXVIII.djvu`, IA source `lagrandeencyclop28dref`) before exact page/quotation citation in publication. But the content-level result is now decisive enough to revise the 005→1906 genealogy.

## 1. Marillier explicitly defines a diffuse, homogeneous magical/divine force

In the section on sacred and impure power, Marillier describes powerful persons and things as **reservoirs of divine force**. Contact with them can be dangerous; their efficacy is contagious; taboo protects both ordinary people from concentrated power and sacred persons/objects from harmful contact.

He repeatedly uses `mana` for this power and says the force can be:

- concentrated in chiefs, sorcerers, priests, sacred places and objects;
- transmitted by direct or indirect contact;
- present in a part as the whole force of the being/object;
- simultaneously fecundating and destructive;
- dangerous to the weak or unqualified;
- sacred without being morally good.

The most important formulation later in the sacrifice section describes a primitive conception of:

> **`un même mana, d'une même force magique et divine, de qualité partout identique, éparse dans les choses`**

— a common mana / magical and divine force, qualitatively identical and dispersed in things.

This is extremely close to Lovejoy's 1906 account of impersonal, diffused, transferable efficacy. The relation is no longer dependent on a later Britannica summary or on Söderblom's single 1901 `le mana de l'homme` passage.

Evidence limit: exact printed-page location inside pp.341–364 still requires facsimile collation.

## 2. Taboo is explained through contagious power, not moral impurity

Marillier's `sacré / impur` section makes the distinction central to 005 unusually explicit.

Sacred chiefs, sorcerers, priests, secret-society members, corpses, menstruating women, sacred sites and other powerful persons/things can all be dangerous because of the force they contain or radiate. `Sacred` and `impure` are not originally moral opposites; both can express dangerous concentrated power. Objects touched by taboo persons/things can themselves become taboo.

This supplies a direct French theoretical context for 005 pp.14–20 and pp.112–117:

- ritual danger need not imply moral guilt;
- social exclusion or restriction can be generated by contact/power mechanisms;
- ritual qualification and bodily state can determine efficacy;
- a prohibition can have a causal-magical rationale without being an ethical judgment.

## 3. Marillier's sympathetic magic is already more than Frazerian similarity

Marillier accepts that imitative practices can be read through similarity — rain-making by water, clouds by dust, action on image/reflection/name as action on the being — but warns against reducing them simply to objectified association of ideas.

He notes that such practices are usually accompanied by:

- incantations;
- ritual gestures designed to release divine force;
- a performer whose personal status matters;
- powerful charms;
- sometimes **blood effusion**.

This is important for Lovejoy 005 because the notebook repeatedly asks what actually makes a rite effective rather than classifying it by outward resemblance alone.

## 4. The 1900 sacrifice theory confirms Thomas's later summary in Marillier's own wording

The electronic reproduction gives an unusually explicit opening to the sacrifice section.

Marillier argues that at its primitive level sacrifice is not primarily gift or prayer. To sacrifice is to release, by shedding the victim's blood identified with life, **a force intensified by incantations and ritual gestures** which acts upon the gods and bends them to the sacrificer's will.

He therefore calls primitive prayer/incantation and sacrifice genuinely **magical acts**: they do not initially persuade or please the god but constrain him, neutralize him if hostile, or make him an instrument of the worshipper.

This substantially verifies the later N. W. Thomas / Britannica relay that had previously been treated cautiously.

## 5. Marillier himself uses the mechanical / psychological distinction

A particularly high-value passage distinguishes magical sacrifice from later alimentary / propitiatory exchange.

When the rite becomes an offering that the god may accept or reject, Marillier says the means of constraint is no longer:

> **`un moyen mécanique, si j'ose dire`**

but a **psychological and moral** means.

The deity can refuse. The sacrifice must be accepted by him; by contrast:

> **the alimentary sacrifice is accepted by the deity; the magical sacrifice is suffered / undergone by him.**

This is one of the strongest French prehistories yet found for Lovejoy's 005 language of `machine`, `quasi-mechanical`, direct action on forces, and later 1906 `Primitive Energetics`.

The correspondence should nevertheless be described as conceptual/mechanistic continuity, not as proof Lovejoy copied this printed sentence.

## 6. Blood restores and transfers life/power

Marillier uses the cult of the dead as a clear example of non-gift efficacy. Ancestors receiving blood at their tombs drink it because the blood **infuses new life** and strengthens them as protectors.

Elsewhere sacred flesh transfers the qualities/power of the being consumed, while blood can establish contact and alliance between human and divine parties.

These mechanisms map directly onto 005:

- pp.84–90: blood/body parts as carriers of transferable qualities;
- pp.88–90: intrinsic efficacy vs indwelling spirit;
- pp.99–105: fecundative transfer to crops;
- p.119: selected organs transfer intelligence/courage/sexual power.

## 7. Communion is historically secondary and can change meaning

Marillier's 1900 account no longer treats sacramental communion as the universal original meaning of sacrifice.

He places alliance/communion later than the magical and alimentary forms, and describes the meaning of communal/sacramental sacrifice as changing as social organization and concepts of divinity change. When the older conception of a common dispersed mana fades and divine transcendence increases, communion is reinterpreted as mystical union rather than the original production of kinship/identity.

This is entirely consonant with 005's persistent claim that one ritual complex can contain elements with different histories and that later sacramental interpretations should not be projected backward.

## 8. The exact `mana` → transcendence transformation is now visible

Marillier proposes an evolutionary sequence in which an early conception of a common, qualitatively identical magical/divine power dispersed through things gradually loses force. New political/social structures isolate chiefs and kings from the mass of the people, and analogous representations contribute to the transcendence of supernatural rulers.

The important point for the Lovejoy project is not whether this evolutionary sociology is correct. It is that in 1900 Marillier already treats **mana / impersonal magical force** as analytically prior to later sharply personalized/transcendent deity concepts.

Lovejoy's 1906 paper's attack on anthropomorphic/animistic master theories therefore sits much closer to Marillier's own published 1900 conceptual field than previously established.

## 9. Expiation is also decomposed by mechanism

Marillier divides expiatory sacrifice into logically distinguishable forms even when one ceremony can serve several ends.

He distinguishes:

- appeasing an offended deity;
- mechanically/magically transferring and eliminating evils;
- communal danger generated by a violator's act;
- scapegoat mechanisms in which evils/crimes are loaded onto a person, animal or object and expelled/destroyed;
- agricultural ceremonies simultaneously fecundative, communial and expiatory.

He explicitly says that in some scapegoat-type cases the rite is **not a symbol of moral lustration but a magical operation** that destroys or removes the evils affecting the community.

This is a direct theoretical control for 005's refusal to equate expiation, purification, propitiation and moral guilt.

## 10. Marillier explicitly narrates the moralization of ritual relations

The article then reconstructs a transition from magical constraint to contractual/social relations with deities and eventually to moralized religious obligation.

Once gods and worshippers become parties to reciprocal obligations, breach can come to be experienced not merely as imprudence but as **guilt**. Collective cult, reciprocity and transformed concepts of divinity contribute to moralization.

This gives a striking counterpart to the first third of 005, where Lovejoy repeatedly isolates naturalistic, ritual, social/community and finally strictly moral determinants.

The early and late halves of 005 are therefore more tightly connected than they first appeared:

> **future-life notes:** distinguish ritual/social/status conditions from moral desert
>
> **sacrifice notes:** distinguish magical/direct, alimentary, propitiatory, communial and moralized mechanisms
>
> **common operation:** classify by the mechanism/norm producing an outcome rather than by superficial form or later interpretation.

## 11. Revised Marillier → Lovejoy genealogy

Before this capture, the strongest model was:

> Marillier dossiers / seminar classification → Lovejoy 005 → Lovejoy 1906 abstraction.

The 1900 `Religion` text now inserts a much more specific conceptual node:

> **Marillier 1897 anti-single-origin critique**
> → **Marillier 1900: common dispersed `mana` + contagious power + magical blood efficacy + mechanical constraint vs psychological/moral exchange**
> → **same-seminar Söderblom 1901: `le mana de l'homme` attributed to Marillier**
> → **Lovejoy 005: direct/intrinsic/transferable/quasi-mechanical efficacy distinctions**
> → **Lovejoy 1906: impersonal, diffused, transferable causal efficacy / `Primitive Energetics`.**

This is now a much tighter genealogy than a generic `Marillier influenced Lovejoy` statement.
