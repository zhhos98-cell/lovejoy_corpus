# What Was Arthur Lovejoy Doing with Buddhism?

Status: **FIRST ORDER LONGFORM / v3.11 / JHI BLOG**
Date: 2026-09-04
Body word count: **8,314 words**

Arthur O. Lovejoy’s bibliography gives him an awkward beginning. The list appended to *Essays in the History of Ideas* opens with a Buddhist article from 1898. Its title names two Pāli technical terms, *upādāna* and *upādisesa*. Later readers know Lovejoy through unit ideas and intellectual genealogy. *The Great Chain of Being* dominates the mature image. The first bibliographical item points somewhere else. Lovejoy had already published an essay on Lord Monboddo in 1895, so even the chronology behind this first item is crooked. The Buddhist paper sits inside an earlier career already under way. Its own scale is narrow. Eleven printed pages move through two terms and several canonical passages. A dispute among European scholars of Buddhism fills the rest. Around those eleven pages survives a much larger body of working evidence. Harvard records survive. An American Oriental Society program survives too. Two notebooks preserve the working field. Paris letters and later articles widen it further.[^1]

Lovejoy first registered at Harvard on 26 September 1895. His graduate record places him in Philosophy. During 1895 to 1896 he took two Sanskrit half courses. He received A in each. Philosophy occupied the rest of the card. The entries include Philosophy 6 and Philosophy 13. Philosophy 9 appears too. Philosophy 20d completes the block. His grades range from B to A. The following year the language work became more specific. The card lists Sanskrit 5, grade A. Harvard’s 1896 to 1897 announcement identifies course 5 under Indo Iranian languages. Charles Rockwell Lanman taught it as advanced Pāli. The title was “The Sacred Books of Buddhism.” The printed description names the *Dhamma Pada*. It names the *Sutta Nipāta*. It also names the *Majjhima Nikāya*. The course met several times each week. Lovejoy therefore entered specialist Buddhist textual work through a formal language sequence, inside a graduate program still administratively centered on philosophy.[^2]

Another entry on the same card is Semitic 13, grade A. Crawford H. Toy taught the course. Harvard described it as a history of Hebrew religion with comparison of other Semitic religions. Lovejoy also took Philosophy 20c, grade A. A student paper survives from that course. Dated 15 February 1897, it asks whether Kant’s characteristic contributions to philosophy have permanent value. The paper itself belongs to another branch of Lovejoy’s work, yet its date matters. In February he was writing on Kant. His Pāli course was still in session. Toy’s comparative religion course was also in session. Harvard kept these activities administratively distinct. Lovejoy simply occupied them together. The student record therefore gives a compact picture of his second graduate year. Sanskrit had led into Pāli. Comparative Semitic religion occupied another course. Philosophy continued at full strength. The later division between “orientalist Lovejoy” and “philosopher Lovejoy” has little purchase on the card.

Lovejoy’s third Harvard year adds another first order detail. The 1897 to 1898 block on his record card includes Anthropology 1. The grade is A. The exact course description still needs a same year catalogue crosswalk, so the card itself is the useful fact. Philosophy remained active beside it. A surviving manuscript dated 10 June 1898 is titled “The Ethics of Authority and the Ethics of Value.” Johns Hopkins identifies it as a Philosophy 20d essay. Lovejoy also held the status of University Scholar. The year before Paris therefore contains anthropology on the individual record, another philosophy paper, plus the Buddhist publication moving toward print. The card supplies a dense chronology of simultaneous work. Course boundaries remain explicit.

On 10 April 1897 the American Oriental Society issued the final circular for its 108th meeting. Item 24 named “Mr. ARTHUR O. LOVEJOY, Harvard University.” The announced communication was a “Critical summary of the argument of the Milinda-pañha.” This title is more revealing than the later bibliography. The *Milinda-pañha* had become a concrete research object during the same academic year as Lanman’s advanced Pāli course. Lovejoy’s name was entering the program of the principal American learned society for Asian languages and texts. The circular supplies only a title. It gives neither an abstract nor a manuscript. Even so, its specificity is striking. Lovejoy was preparing something on the argument of a Pāli Buddhist dialogue. The surviving publication list has no corresponding item. The AOS circular preserves an intermediate scholarly object that later bibliographies leave almost invisible.[^3]

The Baltimore meeting opened on 22 April. Lovejoy was absent. His paper was among the communications read by title. The final Proceedings reordered it as No. 30. The title had changed. Indexed text allows the term pair to be recovered at content level as *upādāna* and *upādāna-kkhandhā*. The exact typography still awaits direct visual closure on the printed page. The change of object is secure. Twelve days earlier the circular had promised a critical summary of the *Milinda-pañha*. The final meeting record presents a paper on two Buddhist technical terms. A year later the printed article keeps *upādāna* and replaces the second term with *upādisesa*. Three public states survive: the *Milinda-pañha* argument, *upādāna* with the aggregates, then *upādāna* with *upādisesa*. The sequence requires no recovered draft to be historically useful. It records Lovejoy changing the public shape of his research before the 1898 article reached print.[^3]

The late April term pair reaches directly into the first half of the published article. Senart had treated *upādāna* as an abbreviated collective expression for the five *upādānakkhandhas*. The equation mattered because the five aggregates already appeared elsewhere in Buddhist classifications. Senart used such recurrences to support a historical argument about the twelve *nidānas*. The formula looked composite. Its elements came from heterogeneous sources. Repetition helped expose the seams. Lovejoy’s 1898 paper spends several pages on the relation between attachment and the aggregates. He rejects their equation. Attachment relates to the aggregates as grasping relates to what is grasped. That separation changes the apparent duplication inside dependent origination. The 1897 Baltimore title therefore names a problem that clearly survived into print, even though the second printed term later became *upādisesa*.

A Harvard Cooperative Society notebook in the Johns Hopkins papers catches this work at much greater length. Lovejoy labeled it “Sankhya + Buddhism.” The notebook contains seventy one scanned pages in the present repository concordance. Its surface is crowded from the beginning. Rhys Davids appears. Warren follows. Childers supplies lexical material. Oldenberg enters repeatedly. Senart occupies a sustained run. Burnouf fills later leaves. Garbe belongs to the surrounding comparative discussion. Canonical passages sit beside dictionary entries. Lovejoy copies arguments. He rewrites formulas. He draws relations among terms. He asks abrupt questions in the middle of source packets. The notebook never settles into the voice of a treatise. It works more like a desk. Books are opened one after another. Their propositions are carried into the same paper space. Lovejoy’s own interventions often occupy a line or two between large blocks of inherited scholarship.[^4]

Henry Clarke Warren supplied an early problem around dependent origination. Warren had already warned readers against treating the twelve links as one simple causal chain. Different links expressed different forms of dependence. Yet he also treated *taṇhā* and *upādāna* as equivalent in a local passage. Lovejoy copied the equation and interrupted it. The surviving line appeals to “the present text.” He then compares classifications. Four kinds of attachment sit on one side. Six forms of desire sit on another. Their mismatch matters. Lovejoy does very little rhetorical work around the point. He checks one relation against another body of textual evidence and moves on. The scene is characteristic of the notebook. An authority supplies the large problem. Lovejoy tests a smaller proposition inside it. The result may leave the larger framework intact while altering one connection.

Another leaf contains a short intervention after Rhys Davids: “To R.D.’s remark it shld be added.” Elsewhere a lexical packet from Childers ends in a fresh semantic question. These moments are easy to lose amid the notebook’s volume. Most propositions on a page may belong upstream. A connective sentence can still change the page. Lovejoy uses such clauses to redirect a source, narrow a claim, or reopen a relation. The method is visible through action rather than declaration. He rarely writes a general rule for handling evidence. He writes a question beside a specific Buddhist term. Then he turns the page.

Manuscript p. 123 is unusually vivid. Lovejoy places *viññāṇa* inside the *nāma* side of a classification. Nearby prose gives the same term another position. “Viññāṇa is temporally (?) an antecedent of nāmarūpa, and logically a subdivision of it.” The page therefore holds two orderings at once. The question mark attaches to temporal antecedence. Logical subdivision receives a firmer statement. The classification on the page reinforces the second relation. Nothing here requires an abstract theory of relation types. The textual problem itself produces the distinction. *Viññāṇa* can precede *nāmarūpa* in one sequence while occupying a place within a classificatory structure. The notebook lets both statements stand.

European debates over dependent origination made such distinctions unavoidable. A term could precede another term in a canonical formula. It could be historically older. It could also be logically prior. Causal dependence created another relation. Philological derivation created yet another. Senart’s historical argument moved rapidly among several of these levels. Oldenberg challenged parts of his semantic case. Warren approached the formula through the character of dependence. Lovejoy’s notebook sits inside this dispute. It keeps returning to the question of what kind of ordering a particular passage can support. On p. 123 the answer is visible in the syntax. Temporal order gets a question mark. Logical inclusion gets a separate clause.

Senart’s 1896 article occupies the most important later cluster. He treated the twelve *nidānas* as a secondary construction. Repetition helped reveal the formula’s composite character. Ready made scholastic elements seemed to have been fused. The order could therefore carry a substantial accidental element. Lovejoy copied and digested this argument across several pages. Then p. 49 creates a boundary in plain language: “On Senart’s account of the matter there is simply this to be said.” What follows is Lovejoy’s reply. He grants inherited terms. He grants heterogeneity. He accepts a loose temporal series. Repetition can also remain. His quarrel concerns what these historical premises establish about the resulting arrangement.

Lovejoy’s answer is sharp. He writes that a “discoverable logical system” governs the arrangement. Repetition becomes intentional within that reconstruction. Senart’s compositeness survives. The claim of unintelligibility falls away. Page 50 then tests the repetition more closely. If *upādāna* already means the aggregates, the alleged duplication produces a stranger result. Lovejoy points toward a triple enumeration. *Nāmarūpa* itself can function as a collective designation. Some relations are temporal. Others are logical. The recurrence of an item therefore says little until its role is specified. Lovejoy answers a historical argument by asking whether the supposedly repeated elements occupy equivalent places in the system.

Page 51 resumes Senart’s source packet. The opening material concerns possible historical development around *avijjā* and *saṅkhāra*. Then the right leaf contains an abrupt question: “But is there necessarily any ontological function involved? A.O.L.” The initials are rare enough to matter. Lovejoy is marking his own interruption. The sentence severs one possible inference. A historical account of how terms entered a formula leaves the corresponding ontological function open. The next page continues the pattern. “The whole enumeration is secondary as to origin & composite as to character!” compresses Senart’s thesis. Then Lovejoy asks, “And what of nāmarūpa?” The whole can be historically composite while one term still requires a fresh functional account.

The argument shifts again around the present aggregates. Lovejoy asks what happens to the existing group of *skandhas*. Present moral action cannot simply abolish the group now operating. It continues toward dissolution. A separate question concerns whether dissolution is followed by another grouping. Here attachment and karma enter. This causal distinction survives into the 1898 article. Lovejoy’s prose there is cleaner, but the structure is already visible in notebook 004. One problem concerns persistence of the current aggregate complex. Another concerns production of a future complex. The terms touch the same doctrine while answering different questions.

A lexical problem follows. Childers supplies the ordinary semantic field for *āsava*. The root evokes flowing, oozing, leakage, or influx. Buddhist technical use often pushes toward a moral sense such as impurity. Lovejoy tests the passage from ordinary meaning to technical meaning. Canonical examples become the deciding evidence. Etymology opens a range of possibilities. Usage narrows the range. The operation looks small beside the grand debates over Buddhist origins, yet it later becomes crucial for *upādisesa*. Lovejoy repeatedly asks what the actual textual environment allows a technical noun to mean.

Page 55 redraws the twelve *nidānas* against the aggregates. The constituent definitions come from the literature. The cross mapping is notebook labor. The same page also compares the *Milinda-pañha* with later Piṭaka material. Lovejoy sketches a historical development around Nirvāṇa. On the next page he marks the proposal with “I incline to suspect.” The phrase matters because the hypothesis is genuinely broad. He distinguishes a philosophical development of impermanence from another line that softened Nirvāṇa. That second line could later feed Buddhist mysticism. The page allows a historical conjecture, but the conjecture carries its own verbal brake.

That same page also performs the reverse operation. *Upādhi* and *āsava* have distinct roots. *Saṁyojana* adds another lexical history. Their connotations differ. Lovejoy gathers them around a shared systematic function. They can express attachment to objects of physical experience. Here lexical difference yields to functional convergence. The notebook therefore gives no stable preference for multiplying distinctions. Lovejoy separates terms when one relation fails. He combines terms when another relation appears secure. The criteria change with the question.

Pages 57 through 60 are dominated by source extraction. Burnouf occupies a long quotation. Rhys Davids supplies material on *upādhi*. Oldenberg shapes much of the Nirvāṇa packet. The *Mahāvagga* appears. The *Milinda-pañha* appears again. These pages carry the philological mass behind the later argument. Lovejoy’s own voice recedes. Then pages 61 and 62 tighten the dossier. The Arhat reaches Nirvāṇa while the present aggregates remain. Their later disappearance concerns another moment. Lovejoy works toward the extinction of the cause of rebirth. At p. 62 he stops himself with a sentence that sounds almost bureaucratic: “This is all that is required for the purposes of my paper.” A stronger philosophical question remains outside the present job.

The next cluster concerns *upādisesa*. Oldenberg already supplied much of the semantic direction and many of the textual witnesses. The notebook adds a more pointed usage argument. Page 66 turns to the *Sutta Nipāta*. Lovejoy argues from context. An interpretation tied directly to physical death fits poorly. Ethical residue fits better. The wording carries almost verbatim into the 1898 publication. Print adds a public calibration. Lovejoy treats the evidence as relevant. He leaves the issue open. The article gives actual textual use greater authority than etymological speculation. Contrary passages remain visible. The final claim is smaller than the most ambitious notebook possibilities.

Page 68 then expands the historical scale again. Lovejoy writes “Original Elements.” Under that heading he places the conception of flux as a reason for suffering. The formula of dependent origination follows. “Derived Elements” receives the theory of sense perception. Psychology follows. The aggregates come last. Historical stratification belonged to the wider field. Senart had made secondary construction central. Oldenberg worked through textual layers differently. Garbe and Warren supplied other historical possibilities. Lovejoy’s exact allocation is his own local scheme. It sits at the end of a notebook filled with technical disputes. It reads as a local hypothesis about Buddhism. A future history of ideas lies elsewhere.

The recovered AOS title now makes these pages look less isolated. The late April 1897 communication named *upādāna* with *upādāna-kkhandhā*. Notebook pages 47 through 70 repeatedly work on attachment in relation to the aggregates. The 1898 article preserves that problem across its opening pages. A direct manuscript genealogy remains out of reach. The object level continuity is still unusually strong. The meeting title records an intermediate stage between the broad *Milinda-pañha* announcement and the later *upādisesa* article. The second technical term changes. The article becomes easier to read as a selective publication cut from a larger Buddhist dossier.

Lovejoy’s printed opening enters Senart’s dispute directly. Terms can be borrowed. Ideas may also be borrowed. “Arrangement and application” can still have another history. That sentence compresses the notebook’s answer to Senart. Provenance and current organization remain separate questions. The article then rejects the equation of attachment with the aggregates. It follows the current aggregates toward dissolution. It asks what condition governs the rise of another group. The second half turns to *upādisesa*. Usage again carries the argument. The *Sutta Nipāta* receives special weight. The paper closes with unresolved evidence rather than a grand reconstruction.

The 1898 article was appearing while Lovejoy’s work moved into France. A group of letters survives through Daniel J. Wilson’s archival transcriptions. On 9 October 1898 Lovejoy wrote from Auxerre to George Holmes Howison. The bulk of the letter is philosophical. Royce remains present. Pluralism remains a live problem. Lovejoy asks about relations among monads. He worries about numerical plurality. God’s place in a pluralistic universe enters the discussion. At the end he turns to the coming Paris year. The lectures would begin in November. He hoped for “an interesting year’s work there in philosophy and comparative religion.” The phrase precedes the Paris courses themselves. Lovejoy arrived with both fields already in view.[^5]

The surrounding October letters widen the scene without turning it into a syllabus. On 17 October Lovejoy wrote about Higher Criticism and biblical literature. The Old Testament interested him as a record of moral development rather than a fixed collection of oracles. A few days later the Buddhism plan appears beside Hebrew Wisdom. The sequence matters because historical reading was already active across more than one textual tradition. Lovejoy was moving between philosophical construction and historical criticism before the Paris lectures began. His later choice of Lévi belongs inside that crowded practice rather than standing as a single disciplinary turn.

On 20 October he wrote to W. W. Lovejoy. The surviving transcription reaches Buddhism through a discussion of Hebrew Wisdom literature. Lovejoy proposes “to elucidate the theoretical presuppositions and doctrines of Buddhism from a philosophical point of view.” The next sentences describe a division of scholarly labor. Philologists had expounded Buddhist literature while caring little for philosophy. Philosophers had reconstructed Buddhist conceptions from their own heads while neglecting textual technicalities. Lovejoy wanted enough control on both sides to attempt another kind of reconstruction. He chose Sylvain Lévi as the person he wanted to study under for this purpose.

The same letter joins Buddhism to Ecclesiastes through the problem of evil. Lovejoy hears affinities between Buddhist pessimism and Hebrew Wisdom. His fellowship, however, required his principal work to stay within philosophy as the term was ordinarily understood. Comparative religion therefore occupied a delicate institutional position. It was central enough to guide his plans. It remained separate enough to require budgetary discipline. Lovejoy’s own letter makes that distribution concrete. Buddhism and Hebrew Wisdom were simultaneous research lines. Metaphysics remained active beside them.

By December the Paris schedule had imposed its own limits. Lovejoy reported that Paris offered no course in Pāli Buddhism that year. He expected instead to visit Lévi’s house and “read a little of the Petakas with him occasionally.” Wilson’s typescript gives “Petakas.” The likely normalization is Piṭakas, but the archival witness should remain visible. A planned formal course had become private reading. The research interest persisted by changing institutional form. This small logistical fact fits the October project exactly. Lovejoy wanted technical textual competence for philosophical work. When the institutional course disappeared, he sought another way to read.

The December letter is packed with neighboring work. Maurice Vernes’s Psalms course helped with Lovejoy’s “Wisdom work.” Residual Hebrew was becoming useful again. Jean Réville’s course on the Fourth Gospel became a secondary line. The Faculty of Theology library offered better Wisdom commentary resources than the Bibliothèque Nationale. Lovejoy tried Sabatier on Acts once and then dropped it. The course required too much patristic knowledge. It also lay outside his work for that year. In one letter Lovejoy records attendance and abandonment. Private substitution appears beside library choice. These are ordinary academic decisions. They also show him allocating scarce time among distinct evidentiary tasks.

Paris also added figures who belonged outside the Buddhist line. On 29 January 1899 Lovejoy wrote from 5 Rue Rollin about Jean Marie Guyau. He called Guyau fresh and vigorous. Originality mattered too. *L’Irreligion de l’avenir* impressed him for its candor. Guyau’s evolutionary philosophy also interested him as the expression of a powerful personality. The letter sits beside the Lévi and Marillier material without merging with either. It gives the Paris year another live philosophical strand. Lovejoy’s comparative religion work developed amid French debates over irreligion and evolution. Moral life remained part of the same discussion.

Another Paris notebook opens onto a different field. Notebook 005 has a strong core from 1898 to 1899. Lovejoy reused it later. One page is dated 20 December 1905. The object itself is accretive. Inserted slips occur in clusters around pages 29 through 37. Another cluster sits around pages 49 through 54. Folded sheets recur around pages 92 through 100. A small slip appears near p. 119. Numbered schemes sit beside cancellations. Branching diagrams interrupt prose. Material additions make the notebook look less like a continuous composition than a portable research apparatus.[^6]

Buddhism recedes here. Comparative religion fills the pages. Death and afterlife occupy early sections. Sacrifice takes over later. Blood and magic recur. Fetish material follows. Agrarian rites sit beside cannibalism. Marillier stands close to the Paris core. Robertson Smith supplies a major theory of sacrifice. Frazer appears across magical and agrarian problems. Tylor receives special attention. Trumbull brings blood covenant material. Mannhardt enters vegetation theory. Cameron supplies African ethnography. Grout supplies another case. The notebook’s density comes from these sources colliding on the same pages.

The afterlife sections already resist a single developmental key. A person’s posthumous fate may turn on social status. The manner of death can matter. Burial practice can matter. Ritual provision can change the outcome. Moral desert appears as one determinant among several. The pages revise these categories locally. A scheme may start with a broad class and then receive a narrower label. Another page can separate social privilege from moral judgment. The result is a working taxonomy of selectors. It records several reasons why two dead persons might receive different fates.

Sacrifice produces an even more crowded problem. Page 49 contains an inserted slip on Tylor. Lovejoy calls him “the master of t. school Anthropolog’y.” He gives a practical reason for the ranking. Tylor stays closest to evidence. Lovejoy adds that Tylor’s books should always be kept at hand in studying the subject. The line is personal and operational. It says less about Tylor’s theory than about Lovejoy’s hierarchy of sources. A comparative notebook full of large theories begins this sacrifice block by ranking one authority according to evidential restraint.

Page 50 offers a four class scheme. Robertson Smith already supplied the main families. Honorific sacrifice formed one class. Expiatory sacrifice formed another. Mystical or sacramental sacrifice formed a third. Marillier’s Paris teaching treated functional sacrifice classes as an active problem. Lovejoy’s sheet adds magical sacrifice as a fourth class. It also subdivides the mystical class into nourishment of the god and sacrifice of union. The point of the page lies in rearrangement. Existing families are recut. A new local class changes the architecture. The sheet becomes a device for assigning cases rather than a theory announced in prose.

The following leaves load mechanisms into that scheme. Frazer supplies the god man whose force passes to a vigorous successor. Robertson Smith supplies the common meal and the blood bond. Expiation receives another packet. Magical sacrifice becomes especially dense. Frazer had described sympathetic magic as direct action upon nature. Marillier treated sacrifice as a possible magical force used to constrain divine or natural outcomes. Lovejoy combines these grammars. A sacrifice can operate like a charm. A god may become irrelevant to the mechanism. The causal relation can be direct rather than personal.

Then p. 55 introduces Verney Lovett Cameron’s Manyéma material. Cameron supplies the ethnographic description. Lovejoy supplies a short classificatory judgment. He treats the cannibalism case as alimentary rather than ritual. The local move is almost comically small beside the lurid source. Yet the consequence is real. A practice that could easily enter a history of ritual anthropophagy shifts into a food category. The case loses one genealogical use and acquires another. A label does the work.

Pages 56 through 60 return to Robertson Smith. Marillier had already attacked a universal communion genealogy. Hubert and Mauss would soon press related objections in print. Lovejoy’s notebook reaches the criticism through an assembled case field. Honorific giving remains distinct. Expiation has another logic. Magical compulsion forms another class. Alimentary consumption can sit outside ritual. Communion therefore loses its monopoly as an origin story. The pages narrow Robertson Smith by multiplying operative functions across concrete cases.

Page 65 addresses fused ritual elements. Lovejoy calls for “delicate analysis.” A current practice can contain components that once belonged to different rationales. Tylor’s survivance theory already made historical persistence familiar. Marillier’s field supplied similar questions. Lovejoy’s local page asks how to separate elements that now appear together. The present rite becomes a composite object whose parts may have distinct histories. The parallel with notebook 004 is suggestive at the level of action. Senart had made the *nidāna* formula composite. Here Lovejoy works through a composite ritual. In both settings, compositeness begins the analysis rather than ending it.

Page 66 adds a social relation to the causal classification. Collective expiation can belong to a whole community. Magical technique can require a skilled priest. A mechanism therefore implies a competent bearer. The question “what does the rite do?” leads to “who can do it?” The notebook moves from causal type to social jurisdiction. A community can carry collective guilt. A specialist can possess technical magical competence. The visible ritual may be similar while the operative social structure differs.

Two smaller pages deepen the sacrifice sequence. Page 69 carries a counter arrow from Robertson Smith’s field. Animal sacrifice can precede human sacrifice in one proposed development, which immediately complicates any universal story of progress from human victims toward animal substitutes. Lovejoy uses the inherited relation against a simple species sequence. Page 73 shifts a funerary practice between explanations. Destruction of an object can protect the dead in one reading. It can also release or transmit a soul linked to the object. The outward form persists while the operative rationale changes. These pages sit between the large sacrifice schemes and the later chronology tests. They keep the historical route open at a case level.

A later packet uses Grout’s South African material. The source describes prayer to ancestors around a particular cow. Removal of evil belongs to the same rite. Lovejoy calls the transaction a “veritable bargain.” He then rejects a deferred payment reading. The bargain has another temporal structure. He also marks substitution or scapegoat logic. One ethnographic case therefore carries more than one relation. Transaction describes one aspect. Substitution describes another. The relations remain separate.

Trumbull’s blood covenant material fills another long run. Blood carries life in the source field. It can heal. It can rejuvenate. It can transmit qualities. Covenant creates fraternity or incorporation. Circumcision enters through similar ideas. Lovejoy extracts heavily from Trumbull. Later pages use those categories in adjudicating rival genealogies. The important local move comes when a theory of partial human sacrifice competes with initiation and blood covenant. Lovejoy chooses the latter route.

Fetish and charm material creates another distinction. Tylor and Lang had strict criteria for fetishism. West African reports complicated them. Some objects possessed efficacy through an indwelling spirit. Others seemed to work through intrinsic or directly transmitted power. Kingsley supplied much of the ethnographic material in this packet. Blood at a gateway could protect. A spirit could also be bound to an object. The notebook keeps these mechanisms separable. Personal agency and direct efficacy can produce similar outward practices.

Pages 92 through 99 contain a large Marillier mediated layer. Lecture or source packets cover divinity classes and sacrifice. Comparative development supplies another theme. They belong largely to the classroom field. Lovejoy’s own questions become harder to isolate. The material still changes the notebook’s horizon. It places him inside a Paris seminar where human sacrifice had its own problem field. Magical sacrifice had another. Ritual survival remained distinct. The surrounding local judgments therefore occur inside an unusually dense comparative vocabulary.

Page 103 contains a branching hypothesis. Cultivated plants occupy one branch. Domestic animals occupy another. Sacrifice links the branches historically. Then chronology interrupts. Agriculture can exist without animal labor. Domestication may sit too late for one proposed path. The page even raises the danger of a circular explanation. A formal genealogy has encountered evidence from outside the ritual dossier. Lovejoy uses agricultural chronology to test whether the proposed sequence can exist historically.

Page 104 narrows the route. A direct movement from wild animal to domestic animal is excluded for the model under examination. Lovejoy retains another line through human sacrifice and later modification. The categories themselves were familiar in the literature. The local work lies in path selection. Similarity among ritual forms cannot decide the route by itself. External chronology constrains the sequence.

Page 112 repeats the operation with circumcision. One available theory interprets it as a remnant of partial human sacrifice. Lovejoy rejects that path. Initiation provides a stronger historical setting. Blood covenant supplies the mechanism. Incorporation gives the social effect. Trumbull owns much of the positive vocabulary. The notebook owns the local adjudication between rival histories. A familiar practice receives a different genealogy because the mechanism fits another source field better.

Page 105 adds an agrarian version of the same problem. Frazer and Mannhardt supplied vegetation spirit material in plant forms. Animal forms appear too. Human forms complete the range. Lovejoy’s local allocation places one transfer theory closer to magical or quasi mechanical action than to sacramental union. The old form leaves the mechanism open. The relation between force and bearer has to be specified again. Ritual action adds a second question. The page sits close to the branching chronology on pp. 103 and 104. Agrarian evidence is therefore doing two jobs in the same neighborhood. It tests historical routes and it tests causal classification.

Near the end, p. 119 leaves a fork open. A selected body part can matter because the organ itself is thought to contain a desired property. Another explanation treats the part as representative of the whole. The first relation is organ specific. The second uses part whole efficacy. Lovejoy places both possibilities on the page. The source field supplies the ingredients. The notebook preserves the unresolved choice.

These pages make the physical form of notebook 005 important. Lovejoy rarely writes one continuous theory from beginning to end. A slip can interrupt a host page. A lecture packet can be folded into another run. A scheme can branch, then receive an objection. A source can be copied for several pages before a one line judgment appears. The notebook’s argument is therefore distributed. It grows through local decisions rather than chapter level exposition.

In 1902 Lovejoy carried one of these local distinctions into another published field. “Religion and the Time Process” discusses Aristotle and Vedānta. He calls their affinity “obvious.” He then calls eastern influence “entirely gratuitous” because an internal Greek route is available. Resemblance supports comparison. Genealogy requires a historical path. The sentence is brief. It echoes the problem that later appears on notebook 005 p. 103. A formal similarity can survive while a proposed transmission route fails.[^7]

February 1906 changes the stakes. “Democracy in the Twentieth Century” begins with a contrast between eighteenth century Nature and nineteenth century Evolution. Lovejoy says Enlightenment politics imagined “generic Man.” Evolutionary history fractures that generic subject. Humanity is divided “longitudinally” by stage of culture. A second division runs crosswise through race or nationality. The two axes are unequal. Stage carries the stronger developmental hierarchy. Race or national genius differentiates populations across the same historical plane. Lovejoy warns that the race category is overworked, yet he continues to use it.[^8]

The essay moves from classification into political institutions. Colonial policy appears. Female suffrage appears. Black enfranchisement and disfranchisement appear. Lovejoy retains evaluative ends such as welfare and civil liberty. Order and civic virtue matter too. He also invokes maintenance or elevation of a “standard of civilization.” Historical variation affects which institutions can serve those ends. The stage category therefore does practical political work. It is more than a neutral historical descriptor.

Two months later Lovejoy published “Kant’s Antithesis of Dogmatism and Criticism.” The object is completely different. He takes a large historical contrast and breaks it into local propositions. Which doctrines already belonged to Kant’s predecessors? Where did Kant diverge? Which arguments were new? How much historical discontinuity remains after the audit? The large contrast loses coherence. A macro category collapses because the local relations refuse to align.

April 1906 also brought Lovejoy’s review of Paul Elmer More. Buddhism returns. More had offered a positive reconstruction of Nirvāṇa. Lovejoy reaches for the *Sutta Nipāta*. He cites verses 1073 through 1076. The same canonical text had mattered in the 1898 *upādisesa* argument. Eight years later it again serves as a check on a philosophical interpretation. The review praises induction from evidence. It values historical antecedents. Wide comparison also matters. Buddhist textual detail remains one place where comparison has to answer to a specific witness.[^9]

The More review gives the Buddhist continuity a sharper edge than a generic return to Asia. Lovejoy had already treated the *Sutta Nipāta* as an early source for technical meaning in 1898. In 1906 he cites a numbered verse sequence from the same text when More makes Nirvāṇa too positive and concrete. The source performs the same kind of policing across an eight year interval. It restricts a philosophical reconstruction by forcing attention back to a difficult textual witness. Lovejoy’s comparative range had widened by then, yet the old Pāli source still had authority at the moment of adjudication.

July moves in the opposite scalar direction. “The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy” gathers ethnographic and lexical materials from many settings. Lovejoy looks for a recurring type of efficacy. The force is impersonal. It can be communicated. It can operate in a quasi mechanical fashion. Magic supplies cases. Fetish material supplies others. Blood and taboo enter as well. The article assigns these heterogeneous phenomena to a broad conceptual family.[^10]

Lovejoy disputes animist or anthropomorphic master explanations associated with Tylor and other anthropologists. He still keeps the aggregate address “primitive philosophy.” The phrase was already common in anthropological writing. Lovejoy reorganizes the field around another causal relation. Impersonal efficacy becomes central. A relation once used to separate ritual mechanisms in notebook 005 now becomes a basis for comparison across populations.

The 1906 article openly registers evidential pressure. Lovejoy revises an initially categorical formulation. Lexical evidence receives careful attention. Counterexamples are discussed. The large category survives. “Savage thought” remains an address. A causal relation extracted from dispersed cases is returned to those cases as a feature of a broad intellectual type. The same year that saw the Kantian macro category collapse also saw this primitive category expand.

The February political essay sits close enough to sharpen the picture. “Generic Man” is divided by stage of culture. July’s primitive article supplies a cognitive description attached to a broad developmental level. One essay addresses institutional fitness. The other addresses causal thought. Direct textual migration between the two has yet to be shown. Their common year still matters. Lovejoy was comfortable assigning carefully abstracted relations to large population categories while also dismantling other historical abstractions.

Notebook 004 gives a useful contrast because its central objects are so small. *Upādāna* is a disputed technical relation. *Viññāṇa* occupies different places under temporal and logical descriptions. Senart’s historical account can be accepted while his inference about logical arrangement is rejected. The 1898 article compresses a large dossier into two terms. By July 1906 Lovejoy can use a similarly fine distinction about causal efficacy to organize “primitive philosophy” across a far larger field.

The Paris notebook stands between these scales. Its source field already uses evolutionary categories. Tylor writes about stages of culture. Marillier compares ritual forms across peoples. Frazer organizes magical practice through large analogies. Robertson Smith proposes broad genealogies of sacrifice. Lovejoy inherits this comparative grammar. His local pages then recut it. One cannibalism case moves into an alimentary class. One ritual mechanism receives a specialist bearer. One genealogy fails an agricultural chronology test. Those cuts make later comparison more precise rather than less ambitious.

A 1907 essay gives Lovejoy another vocabulary for evidentiary burden. “The Entangling Alliance of Religion and History” distinguishes universal propositions from contingent historical occurrences. Historical propositions carry probabilistic status. Extraordinary events demand a heavier documentary burden. Politics appears there as practical and temporal. The article belongs to a later moment than the Buddhist notebook. It nevertheless shows Lovejoy articulating proof differences that his earlier pages had handled case by case.[^11]

Work from 1908 through 1910 continues the movement. Pragmatism controversies force propositions apart. Historical proof becomes cumulative. Lovejoy’s Kant work changes between 1909 and 1910. He revises an umbrella classification toward domain specific distinctions. These later episodes matter because they confirm that the local audits seen in the notebooks remained active. They also warn against treating local audit as a permanent destination. Lovejoy continued to build large historical objects.

*The Great Chain of Being* supplies the mature example. Its preface defines the principal field as “Occidental philosophy” and “Western thought.” Buddhism still appears. Vedānta still appears. “Primitive Buddhism” remains available as a comparative form. The center has changed rather than the archive becoming exclusively European. Asian materials now perform a different job. They illuminate alternatives. They mark boundaries. They occasionally supply a comparative philosophical type.[^12]

The index of *Great Chain* makes the redistribution tangible. Buddhism appears at pages 30 and 97. Vedānta appears at pages 30 and 42. Their presence is sparse beside the long Western sequence, yet it is explicit. Page 30 places Asian systems close to a comparative philosophical problem. The mature book can therefore use Indic materials as controls while keeping its main chronology Occidental. The same materials once demanded pages of local philology from the young Lovejoy. By 1936 they can enter in a few strokes because the object of the book is elsewhere.

The difference from the 1890s is concrete. Lovejoy once studied Buddhist technical terms as local research objects. He took advanced Pāli. He entered specialist debates over textual usage. He planned further Buddhist work in Paris. He sought private reading with Lévi. In *Great Chain*, Buddhism more often enters a Western genealogy from the side. The material remains available, but the narrative address has shifted.

The *Sutta Nipāta* captures that shift particularly well. Harvard’s course announcement placed it inside Lanman’s advanced Pāli class. Lovejoy used it in the 1898 article to adjudicate *upādisesa*. He returned to verses 1073 through 1076 in the More review of 1906. The text therefore survives across two public acts of Buddhist semantic control. In *Great Chain*, Buddhist material serves a wider comparative function. The same archive can be reassigned as the historical question changes.

The AOS title sequence records a similar reassignment at much smaller scale. The *Milinda-pañha* appears first as the announced object. Two weeks later the title has become *upādāna* with the aggregates. Print keeps *upādāna* and changes the second term to *upādisesa*. The research object is repeatedly recut. A bibliography that lists only the 1898 article hides this motion. The meeting documents recover it.

Paris letters recover another kind of motion. Lovejoy arrives planning philosophy and comparative religion. He defines a problem between philologists and philosophers. He selects Lévi. Paris then offers no Pāli Buddhism course. Private reading takes its place. Vernes serves the Wisdom project. Réville becomes secondary. Sabatier is dropped. The year takes shape through decisions over relevance and technical competence.

Notebook 005 records the same kind of work in comparative form. Robertson Smith enters with one theory. Frazer enters with another. Marillier supplies a dense course field. Tylor is ranked as a source. Cameron supplies a case that changes class. Grout supplies another case that changes causal reading. A ritual genealogy hits chronology on p. 103. Circumcision changes genealogy on p. 112. An explanatory fork remains open on p. 119. None of this requires a methodological narrator inside the notebook. The pages themselves carry the sequence.

By 1906 these choices become public at several scales. A Kantian category is dissolved. A Nirvāṇa reconstruction is checked against Pāli. A primitive category is constructed. A political subject is stratified by stage. These operations happen within months of each other. They resist any career story in which Lovejoy first learns contextual caution and later abandons it for abstraction. The two capacities coexist.

They also resist a story in which fine distinctions automatically produce political restraint. “Democracy in the Twentieth Century” uses differentiated historical categories to make judgments about political institutions. Precision can restrict a claim. Precision can also make a hierarchy more elaborate. The effect depends on the scale at which the category is returned to historical actors.

This is where the primitive article becomes especially important. Notebook 005 contains several distinct mechanisms of efficacy. Magical compulsion can be separated from communion. Alimentary consumption can be separated from ritual. Spirit agency can be separated from intrinsic power. By 1906 one family of relations is gathered together. Impersonal, communicable efficacy becomes a “fundamental concept.” The local distinctions have created the conditions for a broader abstraction.

Lovejoy’s early Buddhist work performs the reverse movement on Senart. Senart begins with a large historical diagnosis of the twelve *nidānas*. Lovejoy accepts much of the compositeness argument. He then breaks the resulting structure into smaller relations. Temporal position differs from logical inclusion. Historical provenance differs from present function. Lexical derivation differs from technical usage. The large diagnosis survives only after its claims are redistributed.

The two movements belong to the same archive. Large objects can be decomposed. Local relations can be reaggregated. Lovejoy does both before his mature history of ideas program. The choice is driven by the problem at hand. A category fails when its instances carry different relations. Another category grows when a relation appears portable across instances.

That portability can be historically dangerous. The primitive article returns an analyst’s abstraction to broad populations. The democracy essay returns developmental stages to living political subjects. A classification that began from comparison can therefore acquire consequences outside scholarship. The political force lies in the scale of attribution. One technical relation inside a Buddhist text has a different address from “primitive philosophy” or “stage of culture.”

The archive makes those changes of address visible. A Harvard record shows Lovejoy learning Pāli. An AOS circular shows him offering a specialist communication. Notebook 004 shows a term under pressure. The Paris letters show disciplinary allocation. Notebook 005 shows comparative cases changing class. The 1906 essays show categories expanding or collapsing. *Great Chain* shows a mature Western narrative center. These are concrete changes in scholarly object and audience.

Lovejoy’s later reputation often begins with the large end of this sequence. Unit ideas move through centuries. Philosophical motifs recur across texts. Intellectual history becomes a genealogy of durable conceptual components. Notebook 004 reveals how much work may precede any claim of recurrence. A repeated word can play a different role. A borrowed term can receive a new application. A conceptual resemblance can lack a historical route. A category can recur while its relation changes.

Notebook 005 adds another warning. A repeated ritual form can carry a different rationale. A blood practice can express incorporation in one setting. Another case can involve direct efficacy. A visible similarity can therefore conceal several causal mechanisms. Lovejoy’s pages often search for the mechanism before they search for genealogy. When chronology intervenes, the genealogy must change.

Yet Lovejoy never becomes a permanent skeptic of broad classes. His praise of Tylor is revealing. Tylor combined evidential caution with enormous developmental ambition. Lovejoy could admire his source discipline while working within the same comparative scale. The supposed conflict between caution and aggregation is already dissolved in the scholarly field itself.

The 1898 Buddhist article belongs to that world. Its textual precision should therefore be read alongside its historical ambition. Lovejoy wants to reconstruct Buddhist doctrine from technical usage. He also wants to say something about borrowed elements and systemic arrangement. Nirvāṇa enlarges the claim. The paper is narrow in vocabulary and broad in implication. Its brevity comes from selection rather than from a small research base.

Page 62 of notebook 004 captures that selection. “This is all that is required for the purposes of my paper.” The sentence closes one line because the public argument needs less. It leaves other questions behind in the notebook. The AOS title changes show another form of selection. The printed article changes the second term. Publication is therefore a process of choosing which relations will carry the paper.

The same choice reappears in Paris course decisions. Sabatier on Acts lies outside the year’s line of work. Vernes serves the Wisdom project. Lévi serves the Buddhist project. A missing course produces private reading. Lovejoy’s intellectual formation occurs through these mundane acts of jurisdiction. They create the archive from which later abstractions become possible.

The archive also shows how social the process was. Warren, Senart, Oldenberg, Childers, Rhys Davids, Frazer, Tylor, Marillier, Trumbull, Cameron, Grout populate the pages. More names follow. Lovejoy’s handwriting carries their propositions. His own local acts often occupy the seam between them. A question, a label, a cross mapping, or a rejection can be more revealing than a page of copied theory.

That crowded authorship makes originality a poor organizing question. Frazer already supplied much of the grammar of impersonal magic. Marillier worked with magical sacrifice. Trumbull supplied the blood covenant dossier. Senart made historical compositeness central in Buddhism. Once these propositions return to their sources, Lovejoy’s work appears in redistribution.

Redistribution can be precise. Cameron’s Manyéma material receives an alimentary label. A community and a magical specialist receive different ritual jurisdictions. A proposed sacrifice route is checked against agricultural chronology. Circumcision is moved from a sacrifice genealogy into initiation and incorporation. *Viññāṇa* receives separate temporal and logical positions. *Upādāna* is separated from the aggregates. These are small intellectual acts with clear local consequences.

Reaggregation is equally visible. *Upādhi* and *āsava* can converge around one systemic function. *Saṁyojana* can join them. Page 68 divides Buddhist elements into original and derived groups. The 1906 primitive article gathers dispersed forms of efficacy under one concept. *Great Chain* later organizes a long Western genealogy through recurrent conceptual units. Lovejoy’s archive contains both directions from the beginning.

This makes the Buddhist paper valuable for a history of intellectual method without turning Buddhism into an origin myth. The evidence offers something more concrete. A young scholar with formal Pāli training enters a live Indological dispute. He works through difficult relations among terms. He prunes the dossier for publication. He continues to use Buddhist texts later. Meanwhile comparative religion supplies another field in which similar acts of classification occur at a larger scale.

The political history enters when those larger scales begin to address peoples. “Primitive philosophy” attributes a cognitive relation to broad human groups. “Democracy in the Twentieth Century” attributes developmental position to political populations. The evidentiary operation changes its social reach. A distinction that looks technical on a notebook page can become part of a judgment about civilization when reaggregated.

The early archive therefore deserves attention precisely because it keeps the scale unstable. Lovejoy can work at the level of one Pāli term. He can also sketch the original elements of Buddhism. He can classify one African case. He can also compare sacrifice across continents. He can dismantle a Kantian category. He can also build primitive philosophy. The movement is continuous even when the objects are unrelated.

*The Great Chain of Being* later stabilizes one major narrative address. Western thought becomes the center. The comparative outside remains present. Buddhism and Vedānta appear as types, alternatives, or boundaries. The young Lovejoy had once approached those materials through direct technical problems. The mature historian deploys them differently. The shift is one of function inside a historical narrative.

The bibliography at the back of *Essays in the History of Ideas* flattens all this into a list. The 1898 Buddhist paper comes first. The item seems eccentric because the later career is already known. The archive restores the disorder behind it. Monboddo came earlier. Harvard Pāli surrounds the paper. The AOS title changes twice. Paris keeps Buddhism active. The *Sutta Nipāta* returns in 1906. Comparative religion feeds another notebook. Primitive philosophy emerges from that larger field.

The first bibliographical item therefore works best as a door into unfinished research rather than as a beginning. Behind it sits a student record and an absent conference paper. A notebook full of other scholars follows. The public object changes several times. The material shows Lovejoy choosing relations before his mature vocabulary existed. Some relations stay local. Some travel. Some fail. Some become the basis for much larger historical categories.

That is what makes the 1898 article worth recovering in detail. Its obscurity preserves a moment before reputation simplified the career. Lovejoy appears first as a philosopher learning Pāli. He then appears as a Pāli reader arguing with Indologists. Comparative work tests how far a relation can travel. The later history of ideas is still distant. The intellectual operations that will make large historical objects possible are already visible on the page.
