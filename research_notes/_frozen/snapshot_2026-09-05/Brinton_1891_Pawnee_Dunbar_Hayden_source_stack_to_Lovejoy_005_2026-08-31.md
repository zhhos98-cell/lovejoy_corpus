## V. Later Pawnee ethnography makes the source history itself part of the problem

Later ethnographic literature does not simply repeat Brinton's 1891 synthesis unchanged.

Ralph Linton, `The Origin of the Skidi Pawnee Sacrifice to the Morning Star`, *American Anthropologist* 28.3 (1926), 457–466, treats the sacrifice as a more specific ritual complex and gives a substantially developed later bibliography.

Metadata:
https://www.jstor.org/stable/661093

Current Great Plains / Nebraska reference material distinguishes the Skiri Morning Star Ceremony from the Pawnee Corn Planting Ceremony more sharply than Brinton's 1891 prose does. The Morning Star ceremony retains a fertility dimension but is described as one of the few Skiri rites not tied straightforwardly to a seasonal cycle.

Reference controls:
- https://plainshumanities.unl.edu/encyclopedia/doc/egp.rel.035.html
- https://history.nebraska.gov/marker-monday-pawnee-villages/

This is historiographically important.

Do not treat the Brinton/Lovejoy `corn planting -> Morning Star human sacrifice -> fertility` relation as a transparent ethnographic datum outside its source history.

Instead ask:

> **Which nineteenth-century synthesis made these ritual relations available to Lovejoy, and what did Lovejoy infer from that synthesis?**

This does not invalidate the historical source relation. It changes the object from `Pawnee practice as directly known fact` to `historically mediated ethnographic claim used in comparative argument`.
