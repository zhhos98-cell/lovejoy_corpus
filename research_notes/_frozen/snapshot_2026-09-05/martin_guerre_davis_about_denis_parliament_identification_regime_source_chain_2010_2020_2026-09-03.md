## Question

Can the appearance of Martin Guerre in the French National Assembly's 2020 report on digital identity be treated as a real history-of-knowledge transmission rather than merely as our retrospective analogy?

## Result

**Yes, with one important qualification.** There is a directly documentable conceptual route from Natalie Zemon Davis's Martin Guerre through the French history of identification and into parliamentary digital-identity discourse. The exact claim should be:

> **Davis's Martin Guerre was retrospectively reclassified by historians of identification as an important precursor to the field; the 2020 French parliamentary report then imported the field's regime-based periodization of identification and separately mobilized Martin Guerre as the emblematic failure case immediately before turning to contemporary identity theft and digital authentication.**

This demonstrates a genuine translation of scholarly knowledge into policy framing. It does **not** prove that the parliamentary authors encountered Martin Guerre only through About and Denis, because the report footnotes Davis separately.

## 1. Davis as a prehistory of the object `identification`

Ilsen About and Vincent Denis, *Histoire de l'identification des personnes* (La Découverte, 2010), explicitly place Natalie Zemon Davis's study of Martin Guerre among older works that addressed the problem before `identification` had become an explicit object of historical research.

Their introduction distinguishes three operations inside identification:

1. **singularisation** — establishing a person's distinctive characteristics;
2. **différenciation** — distinguishing one individual from another;
3. **reconnaissance** — comparing known data/characteristics with a present person in order to establish sameness across time or place.

They then define an historical `régime d'identification` as a configuration of relations among ways of naming, recognizing, distinguishing, presenting, documenting, and guaranteeing persons. The key point is that modes, tools, agents, and institutional purposes of identification vary historically.

This is already a major conceptual transformation of Martin Guerre. Davis's book did not need to use `identification` as its master category for later historians to redescribe the affair as part of the genealogy of identification practices.

The retrospective reclassification is explicit:

`Davis: microhistory of imposture / household / court / identity`

→ `About & Denis: precursor to a history whose object is identification`.

This is a history-of-knowledge event in its own right: a previously existing historical object is reassigned to a newly consolidated research field.

## 2. `Régimes d'identification`: the conceptual apparatus

About and Denis reject the assumption that exact identification is simply a timeless need progressively solved by better technology. They ask instead how historically specific systems combine:

- face-to-face recognition;
- local/familial memory;
- names and signs;
- clothing and visible marks;
- written and iconographic documents;
- public authorities;
- administrative registration;
- later mass and computerized identification.

Their book proposes a five-part European periodization from the eleventh century to contemporary computerized/surveillance systems. The broad sequence is:

1. medieval face-to-face identification plus signs and early public-authority identification at distance;
2. early-modern strengthening of written registers and documents alongside interknowledge;
3. late-eighteenth-/nineteenth-century nationalization and statization of identification under mobility, urbanization, democratization, and transport change;
4. post-First-World-War mass assignment of certain identity, including coercive/totalitarian identification;
5. mass identification extended by computerization and surveillance technologies.

This framework is crucial because it shifts the object from an essence called `identity` to historically variable **relations among verification practices, media, institutions, and subjects**.

## 3. The 2020 National Assembly report imports this periodization

The French National Assembly's *Rapport d'information sur l'identité numérique*, no. 3190 (8 July 2020), contains a section on `la construction de l'identification`. Its historical outline reproduces the same five-period structure at a compressed policy level and footnotes About and Denis, p. 7.

The report's sequence is:

- face-to-face recognition and signs;
- growing written identification in the early-modern period;
- national/state identification under the modern nation-state and industrial mobility;
- universal/certain identification as a post-First-World-War state concern plus totalitarian coercion;
- contemporary mass identification, computerization, and surveillance.

It then summarizes the history as a passage from **interpersonal identity** toward identity issued/recorded by the state and verified through changing procedures.

This is not merely thematic similarity. The parliamentary report explicitly cites the 2010 historical synthesis as authority for its periodization.

## 4. Martin Guerre is inserted at the policy hinge

Immediately after that historical sequence, the parliamentary report opens a subsection on the fact that the issuance of identity is accompanied by the risk of usurpation. It characterizes earlier identification as fallible because of reliance on face-to-face encounter, group memory, and signs, then invokes the Martin Guerre affair as the emblematic example.

The report footnotes the Tallandier edition of Natalie Zemon Davis separately.

This placement matters. Martin Guerre is not floating as an anecdote elsewhere in the document. It sits at the exact hinge between:

`history of identification regimes`

and

`contemporary problem of identity usurpation`.

The policy document therefore assembles two scholarly components:

- **About & Denis** supply the long historical architecture of identification regimes;
- **Davis / Martin Guerre** supply a memorable early-modern failure case inside that architecture.

This is best described as a **policy synthesis**, not a single-source derivation.

## 5. What happens immediately after Martin Guerre

The report then moves from modern identity theft into the functioning of digital identity and formally distinguishes:

- **identification**: establishing the identity of the user;
- **authentication**: allowing an already identified user to prove that identity for a transaction or access request.

It further distinguishes biometric authentication/comparison from biometric identification against a database and discusses remote enrolment, facial comparison, and `preuve de vivant` (liveness proof).

Thus the report's local architecture is unusually revealing:

`About/Denis historical regimes`

→ `Martin Guerre as fallible interpersonal identification`

→ `modern identity theft`

→ `digital identification`

→ `authentication`

→ `biometric matching`

→ `liveness`.

This sequence is stronger evidence than a generic statement that `Martin Guerre is relevant to digital identity`. It shows **how a historical case is made to do conceptual work inside a contemporary policy taxonomy**.

## 6. The knowledge transfer is not one-directional technological progress

The 2010 history and the 2020 report both complicate a simple `old uncertainty → modern certainty` story.

About and Denis insist that identification regimes are configurations with historically specific purposes, actors, and instruments. The parliamentary report likewise treats modern biometric matching as probabilistic and separates multiple verification operations that can fail differently.

The conceptual transfer therefore does two things at once:

1. it supplies a modernization narrative useful for explaining why digital identity systems exist;
2. it preserves enough historical differentiation to show that each new regime produces new sites of uncertainty and control.

The most precise formulation is:

> **Uncertainty is not abolished by the transition from interknowledge to digital identity; it is transferred from witness memory and bodily resemblance into enrolment, reference data, biometric thresholds, algorithms, devices, and trusted identity providers.**

## 7. The critical intermediate event: reclassification

A major history-of-knowledge point follows.

The route is not simply:

`Davis → parliament`.

A necessary intermediate operation occurs when a later field **reclassifies** Davis's object.

Davis's book could be read as microhistory, social history, legal history, history of gender, narrative experiment, or history of self-fashioning. About and Denis make it legible as a precursor to `l'histoire de l'identification`.

That reclassification changes which details become salient:

- the number and disagreement of witnesses;
- bodily marks;
- remembered traits;
- face-to-face recognition;
- absence of durable reference documents;
- the problem of sameness across time;
- the institutional authority of the court.

Once recoded this way, Martin Guerre becomes highly portable into digital-identity policy.

Thus the knowledge chain contains an operator:

`historical case` → **disciplinary recoding** → `policy exemplum`.

The disciplinary recoding is what allows the same sixteenth-century case to speak the language of twenty-first-century authentication.

## 8. A small institutional bridge in 2016

A 2016 programme of the Institut d'Història de la Medicina i de la Ciència López Piñero / University of Valencia advertised a cycle `Identidades e identificaciones: pasado & presente`. The programme included Vincent Denis on police, papers, and identification in eighteenth-century France, forensic and fingerprinting material, and a roundtable `Tres épocas, tres identidades` in which Mónica Bolufer presented `Identificación a la Edad Media: ...` / early-modern `el caso de Martin Guerre` alongside later identification cases.

This is not needed to prove the source chain, but it documents how Martin Guerre was institutionally pedagogized **inside an identification-history field** rather than only inside microhistory courses.

The object was being taught comparatively as one historical regime among others.

## 9. Implication for the phrase `portable epistemic object`

The source chain lets us refine that phrase.

Martin Guerre is portable because later actors do not carry the whole case with them. They select a **relation-specific subset** appropriate to the receiving field.

For the identification historians, the useful subset is:

`same person across time?` + `available marks/documents/memory` + `recognition operation`.

For the parliamentary report, the useful subset is:

`fallible identification` + `usurpation risk`.

For digital identity, that subset is then translated into:

`enrolment` + `authentication` + `biometric matching` + `liveness`.

The case travels through **selective decomposition and reaggregation**, not through preservation of one stable meaning.

## 10. Relation to the 2026 `Martin Guerre 2.0` article

A second sweep checked whether the March 2026 Forum INCYBER event itself publicly advertised Martin Guerre as a frame for its eIDAS/digital-wallet discussion.

The official event page describes threats to digital identity, document repetition, identity theft, the EU eIDAS 2.0 wallet, sovereignty, secure attributes, fraud reduction, and trust. It does **not** mention Martin Guerre.

The phrase `Martin Guerre 2.0` and the historical analogy appear in Fiona Slous's *Alliancy* report on the event.

Current evidence status:

- `Martin Guerre 2.0` = **confirmed journalistic/field-facing framing**;
- `Martin Guerre invoked by one or more speakers during the event` = **unresolved**;
- `Martin Guerre as official Forum INCYBER framing` = **not supported by the public event page**.

Until video/transcript evidence is obtained, do not upgrade the analogy into technical actor vocabulary.

This negative control strengthens the dossier's evidence hygiene. A field-facing article can still demonstrate contemporary portability, but the owner of the analogy matters.

## 12. Strongest compressed claim

**The most defensible knowledge-history chain is not that Martin Guerre somehow anticipates digital identity. It is that a twentieth-century microhistorical object was recoded in the twenty-first century by historians of identification as an exemplary problem of recognition across time; that recoding supplied a regime-based history of identification that French parliamentary authors explicitly imported into a 2020 report on digital identity; and the same report placed Davis's Martin Guerre at the hinge between interpersonal identification and contemporary identity-usurpation before differentiating identification, authentication, biometrics, and liveness. The afterlife therefore consists of a documented sequence of disciplinary reclassification and policy translation.**
