Primary: Arthur O. Lovejoy, review of James H. Leuba, *A Psychological Study of Religion: Its Origin, Function and Future*, *International Journal of Ethics* 24.2 (Jan. 1914), 216–220.  
Direct publisher control: University of Chicago Press / *International Journal of Ethics*, DOI `10.1086/intejethi.24.2.2376508`. Full five-page PDF recovered and image-checked on 2026-08-28.

## Core result

The 1914 review does **not** show Lovejoy retreating from or narrowing the 1906 `Primitive Energetics` hypothesis. It shows the opposite.

Lovejoy explicitly says that Leuba's accepted theory of the origin and content of the primitive `idea of impersonal power` is **essentially the same theory** Lovejoy had advanced in 1906; he says `dynamism` is precisely the conception he had called `primitive energetics` or `manitouism`; and he complains that Leuba makes **far less use of the hypothesis than the facts justify**.

At the same time, the review contains a remarkably explicit methodological distinction among three different things that a `definition` of religion might mean:

1. a stipulative statement of what the analyst chooses to call `religion`;
2. a statement of common elements in all the enormously diverse historical applications of the term;
3. an **analytical description of certain complex observable phenomena occurring at various phases of individual and racial development**.

Lovejoy says the third is what is wanted and suggests that Leuba might have done better, at least initially, to dispense with a definition of religion altogether.

This is now a major actor-level control for the logic/aggregation branch:

> **By 1914 Lovejoy explicitly distinguishes stipulative naming, common-core/lexical-historical definition, and analytical description of complex historical phenomena. He rejects letting a definition decide inclusion/exclusion by fiat.**

It also complicates any developmental story in which Lovejoy simply learned after 1906 to distrust broad comparative abstraction. In 1914 he simultaneously **defends the 1906 abstraction** and **criticizes a broader definitional totalization of religion**.

# II. p.218 — Lovejoy reopens the 1906 dispute himself

After discussing Leuba's critique of empirical apologetics, Lovejoy says there is not enough space to discuss the remaining portions fully but takes the opportunity to correct a `somewhat misleading reference` to an hypothesis of his own concerning primitive beliefs.

He summarizes the field position carefully:

- anthropologists increasingly recognize a non-animistic phase;
- it was **probably, but not demonstrably**, also pre-animistic;
- Marett's 1900 paper gave an important early expression to the view;
- Lovejoy adds that accumulating ethnographic evidence was independently forcing the general view on many students.

This is already useful relation control:

`non-animistic`
≠ automatically
`demonstrably pre-animistic`.

Chronological priority remains separately hedged.

# III. p.219 — explicit identity judgment: Leuba's `dynamism` is essentially Lovejoy's 1906 theory

Lovejoy says a reader of Leuba would naturally think Leuba and Lovejoy disagreed over the primitive `idea of impersonal power`.

He then explicitly denies that appearance.

His judgment is:

- the theory Leuba accepts concerning the **origin and content** of the primitive idea of impersonal power is `essentially` the theory Lovejoy advanced in 1906;
- Leuba's `dynamism`, though Lovejoy dislikes the already over-diverse denotation of that name, is **precisely the conception** Lovejoy proposed to call `primitive energetics` or `manitouism`;
- Lovejoy says he had tried to demonstrate the wide diffusion and importance of this conception chiefly by examining how principal native terms expressing the idea were used;
- the two men differ only over the best name and one `minor question`, itself chiefly a confusion of terms.

### Gate-A significance

This is unusually clean actor evidence for Lovejoy's positive identity jurisprudence.

At least here:

`different label`
+
`minor terminological disagreement`
can coexist with
`essentially same theory / precisely same conception`.

The fixed object is not the word. It is the reconstructed content/mechanism:

`impersonal power / efficacy`
+
its claimed origin/content profile
+
its ethnographically reconstructed mode of operation.

This looks like a hybrid of:

- **M1 content/mechanism identity**, and
- **M2 common-core recognition across labels**.

It is direct evidence that Lovejoy's cross-context identity judgments could be explicitly **anti-lexical** well before the mature 1941 semasiological statement.

# IV. 1914 is not a retreat from `Primitive Energetics`; Lovejoy expands its claimed importance

The strongest surprise is the direction of Lovejoy's correction.

He does not say:

`Leuba has shown my 1906 category was too mechanical / too general / too strong`.

Instead he says Leuba makes **far less use of the hypothesis than the facts justify**.

Lovejoy adds that the idea of `mana` or `manitou` **profoundly interpenetrates other elements of early belief** and affects the development of religion in many significant ways Leuba does not take into account.

Thus the 1914 self-position is:

`1906 hypothesis retained`
+
`identity with Leuba's dynamism asserted`
+
`importance and downstream penetration defended/enlarged`.

This directly falsifies any current working hypothesis that the 1914 review would supply an early self-demotion of the 1906 macro-category.

# V. p.220 — three different meanings of `definition`

Lovejoy criticizes Leuba's attempts to reach general conclusions about religion `in all its phases`, saying that, like most such attempts, they are damaged by neglect of a point of `elementary logic`.

The key methodological passage distinguishes three possible senses of Leuba's definition of religion.

## (a) stipulative naming

A statement of what Leuba **prefers to designate by that name**.

This is analyst-owned nomenclature.

It does not by itself tell us what historical phenomena share or what religion historically is.

## (b) common-element definition across historical uses

A statement of **the common elements in all the enormously diverse historic applications of the term**.

This is a common-core / usage-history operation.

It differs from merely choosing a label but can still be hostage to the history and ambiguity of the word.

## (c) analytical description of complex observable phenomena

An **analytical description of certain complex observable phenomena occurring at various phases of individual and racial development**.

Lovejoy says:

> this third thing is what `we desiderate`.

This is the crucial result.

The object of historical/scientific analysis should not be whatever a stipulated or inherited word-boundary includes. It should be the analytically described complex of phenomena.

# VI. Inclusion/exclusion by definition is circular

Lovejoy says Leuba often slides among the three senses. When Leuba declares that religion `is` or `is not` something, he can end up reasoning in a circle because exclusion or inclusion of elements may mean only that they do or do not fit:

- Leuba's own definition; or
- a definition suggested by prevailing usage.

Lovejoy therefore suggests that it might have been better, at least at the beginning, to **dispense with a definition of religion altogether**.

### Governing rule

This yields a direct 1914 actor-level firewall:

`stipulative / prevailing lexical boundary`
≠ `analytical membership criterion`
≠ `empirical/historical description of the phenomenon`.

And:

`definition excludes x`
↛ `historical/observable phenomenon excludes x`.

This is extremely close to the current project's Gate-A concern, but it should be kept in Lovejoy's own 1914 jurisdiction: he is criticizing a psychology/history-of-religion definition, not formulating a universal mature historiography of unit-ideas.

# VII. 1914 supplies an actor-level precursor to aggregation-mode topology

The current project recently formalized six aggregation modes (M1–M6). The 1914 passage now prevents treating `aggregation-mode selection` as wholly retrospective analyst vocabulary.

Lovejoy himself explicitly separates at least three category-making operations:

`D-a stipulation`
≠ `D-b historical common core`
≠ `D-c analytical phenomenal description`.

These do not map one-to-one onto M1–M6, but they show that Lovejoy already recognized that the phrase `definition of X` hides distinct epistemic operations.

A safe crosswalk is:

- `D-a` resembles analyst-owned naming/class construction;
- `D-b` resembles M2 lexical/common-core aggregation;
- `D-c` resembles mechanism/configuration-based historical-empirical typing, closer to M4/M5 depending the scale of the claim.

The most important actor-level principle is not the crosswalk itself. It is:

> **the rule for including/excluding phenomena must not be smuggled in through a nominal definition when the object of inquiry is a historically complex observable phenomenon.**

The 1914 review clarifies 1906 in two directions at once.

Lovejoy explicitly claims authorship/priority of the `primitive energetics` hypothesis and its principal evidence. There is no need to infer a 1906→1914 continuity from lexical resemblance; he states it himself.

`1906 Primitive Energetics`

## 2. It sharpens rather than resolves the aggregation problem

Lovejoy defends the identity and importance of `primitive energetics`, while p.220 simultaneously warns against definitional circularity in broad generalizations about `religion in all its phases`.

So 1914 demonstrates coexistence of:

- bold cross-cultural mechanism/category reconstruction;
- explicit suspicion of nominal definitions and universal common-element definitions;
- preference for analytical description of complex observable phenomena.

The question is no longer whether Lovejoy knew the generic danger of over-definition. He plainly did.

The sharper historical question is:

> **why did `primitive energetics` count for him as an analytically warranted description of a real cross-cultural phenomenon rather than as another stipulative/common-element category vulnerable to the same circularity?**

This is precisely a Gate-A warrant question.

# X. New consequence for the logic-locus branch

The p.220 phrase `elementary logic` is particularly important.

This is **not** formal logic as an autonomous object (`O/F`). It is explicit logical criticism applied to category construction in religion/psychology/history.

Best current coding:

- locus: `M` — logical method used on another object;
- semantic sense: chiefly `C/E/A` — conceptual distinction, inclusion/exclusion jurisdiction, analytical self-consciousness;
- specific operation: distinguish definition types and block circular transfer from nominal definition to empirical/historical conclusion.

So 1914 belongs between:

- 1908–09 logical analysis of pragmatisms / historical compounds; and
- 1924/1941 explicit relation- and category-sensitive historiography.

It materially strengthens the person-level recurrence of **jurisdictional partition** without proving one formal calculus migrated unchanged.
