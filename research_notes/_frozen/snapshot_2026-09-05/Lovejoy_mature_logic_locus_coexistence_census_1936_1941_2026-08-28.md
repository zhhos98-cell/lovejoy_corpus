# Lovejoy mature `logic` locus census — coexistence rather than simple shift (1936 / 1941)

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: **RESTART-SAFE CHECKPOINT / DIRECT PRIMARY CONTROL / LOGIC-LOCUS REFINEMENT**

## Question

Does mature Lovejoy move simply from an object-immanent `logic of X` to analyst-owned logical analysis, or do these loci coexist?

## Result

They coexist, and 1941 makes their epistemic relation explicit.

The mature architecture is better represented as:

`candidate logical structure in ideas / intellectual complexes`
→ `historian-owned analytical reconstruction of implication/opposition/presupposition`
→ `return to historical evidence`
→ `test whether the reconstructed logical relation was historically recognized or operative`.

Thus the earlier two-axis coding remains useful, but mature cases often require a **sequence of loci** rather than one static L-code.

A useful notation is:

`M/A → I/C → Hx`

where:

- `M/A` = historian explicitly constructs a logical hypothesis analytically;
- `I/C` = the candidate relation is a conceptual/implicational relation attributed to the intellectual object itself;
- `Hx` = historical-operation test: whether actors recognized it or whether it functioned in the historical process.

`Hx` is not a new kind of logic. It records the promotion gate from analytical possibility to historical fact.

---

## 1. 1936 methodological opening: analyst decomposition, but the target is not merely analyst-invented

Primary: Arthur O. Lovejoy, *The Great Chain of Being* (1936), Lecture I, esp. pp.3–5.

Lovejoy describes the historian of ideas as breaking philosophical systems into component `unit-ideas`. He says the same elements can be hard to recognize in differing logical combinations and directs the historian toward common logical, pseudo-logical, or affective ingredients beneath surface dissimilarities.

Coding:

- decomposition itself: `M/A`;
- logical/pseudo-logical ingredients claimed to belong to the historical compound: `I/C` candidate;
- identity across differing expressions remains a Gate-A problem, not something logical analysis by itself proves.

This already prevents a simple analyst/object dichotomy. The historian constructs the decomposition, but claims that the resulting components and relations disclose something about the historical compound.

---

## 2. 1936 Aristotle: `logic` can name historically operative habits of classification

Primary: *Great Chain*, Lecture II, pp.55–58.

Lovejoy distinguishes two opposing Aristotelian inheritances. One favors sharply delimited class-concepts; the other stresses continuity, shading, and overlapping boundaries. He explicitly discusses their influence on `logical method` and describes them as rival conscious or unconscious `logics`.

This is not formal logic as an autonomous object (`O/F`). It is closer to:

- `I/K,C`: a historically operative classificatory/conceptual habit attributed to a tradition or intellectual formation.

The important point is that mature Lovejoy still speaks of an object's/tradition's `logic` while also practicing historian-owned analysis.

---

## 3. 1936 continuity from plenitude: immanent implication as historical object

Primary: *Great Chain*, Lecture II, pp.55–59.

Lovejoy says the Aristotelian conception of continuity later fused with plenitude and came to be regarded as logically implied by it: if an intermediate form is genuinely possible, plenitude seems to require its realization.

This relation is best coded `I/C` when Lovejoy reconstructs the conceptual consequence of the principles. But historical acceptance of that consequence is a separate proposition. Lovejoy's later treatment is especially revealing because the same principles can eventually be shown to conflict.

Therefore:

`logical implication reconstructible by historian`
≠ automatically
`actor recognized implication`
≠ automatically
`historically operative inference`.

The 1936 book already performs these distinctions in practice; 1941 states them programmatically.

---

## 4. 1936 Leibniz: principles as a structured historical complex

Primary: *Great Chain*, Lecture V, pp.144–145.

Lovejoy identifies plenitude, continuity, and linear gradation as essential characteristics of Leibniz's universe. Gradation is defined psychologically for monads by degrees of consciousness/clarity, yet the same three laws are also said to hold in the phenomenal/material world as guides to empirical inquiry.

This is another object-level structured complex. The historian is not merely assigning a convenient external taxonomy; he is reconstructing relations that he takes to organize Leibniz's system and its applications.

Coding: primarily `I/C,K`, reconstructed through `M/A`.

---

## 5. 1941 supplies the explicit promotion rule

Primary: Arthur O. Lovejoy, `The Meaning of Romanticism for the Historian of Ideas`, *Journal of the History of Ideas* 2.3 (1941), esp. pp.264–266.

After discriminating the relevant ruling ideas, Lovejoy says their relations must be examined as logical, psychological, and historical/genetic. He explicitly locates logical and psychological inquiry first in the historian's own analytical/constructional work and compares this stage to constructing tentative hypotheses. Logical relations include implication, opposition, tacit presupposition, and incompatibility. Crucially, whether historical actors recognized those relations is still open.

He then requires the historian to return to historical data and determine how far the logical relations were actually manifested as operative factors in the thought tendencies of the period.

This gives the cleanest mature sequence:

`M/A analytical construction`
→ `candidate I/C relation`
→ `historical-operation test`
→ `promote / reject / qualify as historical relation`.

The analytical relation can be historically significant even when actors did **not** draw an implication: non-recognition itself may be a historical fact requiring explanation.

## 6. Consequence for the locus coding

The mature evidence does **not** support a one-directional periodization:

`early object-immanent logic`
→ `late historian-owned logic`.

Instead at least three mature uses coexist:

1. **logic as object/tradition structure** — e.g. rival Aristotelian classificatory `logics` (`I/K,C`);
2. **logical relation as candidate relation among ideas** — implication/opposition/presupposition (`I/C`, often reconstructed by the historian);
3. **logical analysis as historian-owned method** — tentative construction prior to historical testing (`M/A,C`).

The key mature innovation/clarification is therefore not relocation of logic from object to analyst, but **jurisdictional staging**:

> analytical construction may disclose a possible logical relation; historical evidence alone licenses the stronger claim that this relation was recognized, operative, genetic, or causally consequential in the past.

This strengthens the broader project metarule that evidentiary force is relation-indexed.

---

## 7. Updated coding rule

When a passage contains both analytical reconstruction and an attributed relation, do not force one L-code. Use a directional compound where warranted:

- `M/A → I/C` = historian constructs a candidate implication/opposition in the object;
- `M/A → I/C → Hx+` = historical data supports operation/recognition;
- `M/A → I/C → Hx−` = logical relation is reconstructible but historical operation/recognition is absent or contradicted;
- `I/K` = actor/tradition is directly described as possessing a connected or classificatory `logic`, without evidence that formal logic is being applied.

This is a refinement of, not a replacement for, the existing two-axis locus/semantic coding.

> **Mature Lovejoy retains object/tradition `logic` and historian-owned logical analysis simultaneously. The proper model is coexistence plus jurisdictional staging, not a simple locus shift.**
