# What Was Arthur Lovejoy Doing with Buddhism?

Status: **SCENE DENSE LONG DRAFT / v3.9 / JHI BLOG**
Date: 2026-09-04
Target body length: **8,000 to 11,000 words**

Arthur O. Lovejoy’s bibliography gives him a strange beginning. The list appended to *Essays in the History of Ideas* opens with a Buddhist article from 1898. It concerns two Pāli technical terms. A reader meeting Lovejoy through that list could imagine a young orientalist who later moved elsewhere. The chronology is messier. Lovejoy had already published on Lord Monboddo in 1895. The Buddhist paper was therefore an early turn inside an active philosophical career. It was also the first item that his later bibliography chose to place before the reader.[^1]

The choice matters because the 1898 paper looks unusually small beside the books that made Lovejoy famous. Its title names *upādāna* and *upādisesa*. Its pages move through philology, doctrinal classification, textual usage, and disputes among European Buddhist scholars. There is no unit idea vocabulary. There is no program for a history of ideas. There is a young scholar trying to determine what two difficult terms can bear. The scale is narrow. The archive around the paper is much larger.

Lovejoy arrived at Harvard in September 1895. His graduate record places him in Philosophy. During his first year he took two Sanskrit half courses and received A grades in both. The same card records several philosophy courses. One surviving student essay from this period already shows him working inside technical philosophical disputes. The next year changed the linguistic level. His card lists Sanskrit 5, grade A. Harvard’s course announcement identifies course 5 as Charles Rockwell Lanman’s advanced Pāli course. The title was “The Sacred Books of Buddhism.” The assigned texts included the *Dhamma Pada*. The *Sutta Nipāta* appears beside it. The *Majjhima Nikāya* completed the listed Buddhist reading.[^2]

The same year Lovejoy took Crawford H. Toy’s Semitic 13. He received another A. Toy’s course treated the history of Hebrew religion through comparison with other Semitic religions. Philosophy 20c also appears on the card. A surviving paper from February 1897 carries that course number. Its title asks whether Kant’s characteristic contributions possess permanent value. The student moving through Pāli texts was therefore also writing on Kant. He was working in several offices at once. Harvard put those offices in neighboring rooms.

On 10 April 1897 the American Oriental Society issued its final circular for the Baltimore meeting. Item 24 listed Arthur O. Lovejoy of Harvard University. The announced paper was a “Critical summary of the argument of the Milinda-pañha.” The object is strikingly specific. The *Milinda-pañha* is a dialogic Pāli text. Lovejoy’s advanced Pāli course was still running. His name now appeared on the program of the major American orientalist society. A bibliography of later publications barely registers this intermediate scholarly object.

Twelve days later the object had changed. The meeting opened in Baltimore on 22 April. Lovejoy was absent. His communication was among the papers read by title. The final Proceedings listed it as No. 30. The recoverable title concerns the meaning of *upādāna* and *upādāna-kkhandhā*. The exact typography still requires direct visual closure. The content level is clear. The late April communication had moved from the argument of the *Milinda-pañha* to a pair of Buddhist technical terms.[^3]

The 1898 publication changes the pair again. *Upādāna* remains. *Upādāna-kkhandhā* gives way to *upādisesa*. This is a small bibliographical fact with unusual force. The April circular names one problem. The Baltimore record names another. The printed article names a third. Each state survives independently. None requires a story about one stable manuscript. The sequence alone places Lovejoy inside a live process of contraction, substitution, and redefinition.

The first half of the 1898 article explains why the late April title matters. Lovejoy takes up Émile Senart’s treatment of *upādāna* and the five *upādānakkhandhas*. Senart had argued that the former could function as an abbreviated designation for the latter. That equation affected the structure of dependent origination. It also affected the charge that the twelvefold formula contained repetition. Lovejoy spent several printed pages separating attachment from the aggregates to which attachment relates. The 1897 term pair therefore points directly into the problem that dominates the opening of the published paper.

A Harvard Cooperative Society notebook in the Johns Hopkins papers catches the argument before print. Lovejoy wrote “Sankhya + Buddhism” on the notebook. Its pages are crowded. Rhys Davids appears early. Warren follows. Childers fills lexical stretches. Oldenberg enters repeatedly. Senart occupies a large block. Burnouf appears in the later pages. Garbe belongs to the wider comparative field. Buddhist passages sit beside dictionary entries. Questions interrupt extracts. Tables and recast formulas change the shape of inherited material.[^4]

Henry Clarke Warren supplied one of the immediate problems. Warren had already warned readers against treating dependent origination as a simple causal chain. Different links expressed different forms of dependence. Warren nevertheless treated *taṇhā* and *upādāna* as equivalent at one point. Lovejoy copied the judgment, then interrupted it. His objection appeals to “the present text.” He compared the four forms of attachment with the six forms of desire. The categories failed to line up cleanly. A general warning about causal complexity became a local semantic problem.

The same movement appears elsewhere in the notebook. A source can establish the vocabulary and still leave a relation open. On one page Lovejoy adds to a remark from Rhys Davids. On another he rewrites a classification around *nāmarūpa*. The leaves carry many inherited propositions. Lovejoy’s own hand is most useful where he writes a connective sentence, a question, or a local judgment. These small clauses often determine what the surrounding evidence can do.

One page makes the problem visible almost spatially. Manuscript p. 123 places *viññāṇa* within the *nāma* side of a classification. Nearby prose gives it another position. Lovejoy writes that *viññāṇa* is “temporally (?) an antecedent of nāmarūpa, and logically a subdivision of it.” The question mark belongs to the temporal claim. The logical relation receives a different formulation. The page holds both. A reader can move from the diagram to the sentence without forcing them into one line.

This distinction mattered because European discussions of the twelve *nidānas* constantly moved between different kinds of sequence. A term could occur earlier in a formula. It could also belong inside a larger category. A scholar might explain one term as historically older. Another might call the same term logically prior. A third might treat it as causal. Lovejoy’s page does not solve the Buddhist doctrine in one stroke. It keeps several relations alive long enough for them to be tested separately.

Senart made that difficulty harder. His 1896 discussion treated the twelve *nidānas* as historically composite. He saw repetitions. He found heterogeneous categories. He argued that ready made scholastic elements had been fused into an order with a considerable accidental element. Lovejoy copied much of this diagnosis. The surrounding pages preserve Senart’s language and problem structure. Then a sentence marks a turn: “On Senart’s account of the matter there is simply this to be said.”

Lovejoy grants much of the historical case. He can accept inherited terms. He can allow heterogeneous materials. He can accept a series with a loose temporal shape. He can accept repeated elements. The inference about present intelligibility receives a harsher answer. Lovejoy writes of a “discoverable logical system” governing the arrangement. The repetitions become intentional within that reconstruction. Senart’s provenance claim survives. Senart’s conclusion about the resulting structure loses its force.

The next page tests the alleged repetition arithmetically. If *upādāna* simply names the aggregates, the formula creates more than a duplication. Lovejoy points toward a triple enumeration. He also treats *nāmarūpa* as a collective designation. Some elements stand earlier in time. Some stand prior by logic. The repetition charge therefore depends on what counts as the same occurrence. A word can recur while its role changes.

Page 51 returns explicitly to Senart. The page first digests a historical account of *avijjā* and *saṅkhāra*. Then Lovejoy writes a question and signs it with his initials. “But is there necessarily any ontological function involved? A.O.L.” The initials make the interruption unusually secure. The question is brief. It changes the burden of the surrounding history. A genealogy of terms no longer settles what ontological work a term must perform inside the system.

Page 52 carries Senart’s composite thesis forward. Lovejoy writes that the enumeration is secondary in origin and composite in character. That proposition belongs to Senart’s field. The next move belongs elsewhere. Lovejoy asks, “And what of nāmarūpa?” He then returns to the place and function of that term. A historical verdict about the whole series creates a fresh local problem. The notebook advances through these reopenings.

The pages around *upādāna* now begin to converge with the later article. Lovejoy asks what happens to the present group of aggregates. He distinguishes their present continuation from the production of another group after dissolution. Attachment matters to the second question. Karma enters the causal account. The current group runs its course. A future grouping depends on a different condition. The 1898 article develops this argument in cleaner prose.

A lexical problem follows. Childers supplied the ordinary semantic field for *āsava*. The root evokes flowing, oozing, leakage, or influx. Buddhist technical usage pushes toward a moral sense. Lovejoy tests the transition. He places ordinary verbal meaning against canonical examples. The question is how far a dictionary can carry a doctrinal noun. An etymological possibility provides a starting point. Repeated use decides much more.

Page 55 redraws the twelve *nidānas* against the aggregates. The basic definitions come from the literature. The cross map belongs to the notebook’s own labor. Near the end Lovejoy compares the *Milinda-pañha* with later Piṭaka material. He sketches a historical development around Nirvāṇa. The proposal remains tentative. The next page makes that tentativeness explicit with the phrase “I incline to suspect.” Lovejoy imagines more than one line of Buddhist development. One line follows philosophical reflection on impermanence. Another softens Nirvāṇa and later feeds forms of Buddhist mysticism.

The same page moves in the opposite direction at the level of terminology. *Upādhi*, *āsava*, and *saṁyojana* have different derivations. Their connotations differ. Lovejoy can still gather them around a shared systemic function. They express attachment to objects of physical experience. The notebook therefore contains acts of separation beside acts of convergence. The decision depends on the relation under examination.

Several leaves after this point are dominated by source extraction. Burnouf fills one. Rhys Davids fills another. Oldenberg shapes much of the Nirvāṇa evidence. The notebook carries quotations from canonical texts. It compares terminology around the Arhat. It follows *upādhi* into discussions of the aggregates that remain during life. These pages matter because Lovejoy’s later claims sit on a dense base of inherited philology.

Then page 62 stops. Lovejoy has worked through Nirvāṇa as extinction of the cause of rebirth. He has pushed toward a stronger philosophical conclusion about final cessation. At that point he writes, “This is all that is required for the purposes of my paper.” The sentence is almost administrative. It limits the job. A larger metaphysical issue can remain open because the present publication needs less.

The next cluster concerns *upādisesa*. Oldenberg had already assembled much of the semantic direction. He used canonical witnesses. He discussed the Anāgāmin and the Arhat. He connected *upādi* with ethical residue. Lovejoy takes these materials into a usage based argument. Page 66 contains the strongest formulation. The *Sutta Nipāta* examples make physical death a poor reading of *anupādisesa*. Ethical residue fits the contexts better. Almost the same wording enters the 1898 article.

Print adds one more change. The notebook’s confidence becomes a measured public claim. Lovejoy says the evidence is relevant. He leaves the matter open at the end. The article gives usage priority over etymological argument. It retains troublesome passages. It stops short of a final closure. The notebook had already shown a similar narrowing on page 62. The article turns that private economy into published prose.

Page 68 gives one last historical scheme. Lovejoy writes “Original Elements.” He places the conception of flux there. He adds the formula of dependent origination. Under “Derived Elements” he puts the theory of sense perception. Psychology follows. The aggregates come next. Historical stratification was common in the field. Senart had made it central. Oldenberg worked with layers in another way. Garbe and others supplied further historical comparisons. Lovejoy’s exact allocation remains his local synthesis.

The page is worth lingering over because it cuts across the later image of Lovejoy. A historian who knows *The Great Chain of Being* might see “Original Elements” and “Derived Elements” as an embryonic program. The manuscript gives a humbler scene. Lovejoy is trying to sort parts of Buddhism. The categories answer a local historical question. They sit after dozens of pages on Pāli terms. They follow source disputes. They precede no declaration about the history of ideas.

The American Oriental Society record now sits differently beside these pages. The late April 1897 title names *upādāna* and *upādāna-kkhandhā*. Pages 48 to 70 of the notebook circle exactly that relation. The first half of the 1898 paper does the same. The connection is strong at the level of object. The exact manuscript path remains invisible. That gap need not be filled. The three surviving title states already show a changing public research object.

The 1898 article begins by entering Senart’s dispute. Lovejoy can grant borrowing of terms. He can even grant borrowing of ideas. The “arrangement and application” can still carry another history. This sentence compresses many notebook pages. Provenance becomes one question. Present structure becomes another. The distinction allows Lovejoy to accept historical dependence while preserving a local logical reconstruction.

The article then works through *upādāna*. It rejects the reduction to the aggregates. It distinguishes attachment from what is grasped. It follows the aggregates through the current life. It asks about the production of a new group. The treatment of *upādisesa* turns to a different philological problem. Here Lovejoy gives actual use greater authority than an etymological route. The *Sutta Nipāta* becomes crucial. It will appear again eight years later.

By 1898 Lovejoy had also moved beyond Harvard. He was traveling in Europe. A group of letters survives through Daniel J. Wilson’s archival transcriptions. The letters place the Buddhist article inside a much wider year. On 9 October, from Auxerre, Lovejoy wrote to George Holmes Howison. Most of the letter concerned philosophy. He discussed Royce. He worried over pluralism. He asked how real relations among monads could work. He also asked what role God could play in a pluralistic universe.[^5]

The Paris lectures had yet to begin. Lovejoy closed the letter by saying that he expected an interesting year in “philosophy and comparative religion.” The phrase precedes his actual Paris course experience. Comparative religion was already part of the intended work. So was philosophy. The two were linked in his plan before he knew which lectures would materialize.

Eleven days later he wrote to W. W. Lovejoy. The surviving transcription reaches the Buddhism project in the middle of a discussion of biblical Wisdom literature. Lovejoy proposed “to elucidate the theoretical presuppositions and doctrines of Buddhism from a philosophical point of view.” His complaint was disciplinary. Philologists had expounded Buddhist literature while caring little for philosophy. Philosophers had reconstructed Buddhist conceptions from their own heads while neglecting textual technicalities.

Lovejoy wanted Sylvain Lévi for this purpose. The choice was practical. Lévi could provide access to the textual side at the level Lovejoy wanted. Lovejoy also connected Buddhism with Hebrew Wisdom through the problem of evil. Ecclesiastes could sound strikingly Buddhist to him. Yet his fellowship defined philosophy as the principal field. Comparative religion remained close enough to shape the project and far enough to require explanation.

December made the institutional limits concrete. Lovejoy reported that no course in Pāli Buddhism was being given that year. He expected to go to Lévi’s house and read a little of the “Petakas” with him occasionally. Wilson’s typescript has that form. The likely reference is to the Piṭakas. The sentence describes an improvised substitute for a missing course. The research line survives by changing its institutional form.

The same letter is crowded with neighboring projects. Maurice Vernes’s Psalms course served Lovejoy’s Wisdom work. Jean Réville’s course on the Fourth Gospel became a secondary interest. The Faculty of Theology library offered better commentary resources for Wisdom books than the Bibliothèque Nationale. Lovejoy also tried Sabatier on Acts. He dropped that course after one visit. It demanded too much patristic knowledge and sat outside the year’s main line of work.

This is the same scholar who had written “This is all that is required for the purposes of my paper.” In Paris he makes similar decisions at the level of courses. One line receives time. Another becomes a parergon. A third is abandoned. A missing Pāli course leads to private reading. The choices are mundane. They are also the material form of intellectual specialization.

The Paris letters contain no clean passage from Harvard Pāli into a mature method. They show a scholar carrying several problems at once. Pluralism remained alive. Buddhist philosophy remained alive. Hebrew Wisdom gained space. Comparative religion entered the year explicitly. French religious politics appears throughout the correspondence. Guyau impressed Lovejoy in January 1899. The archive looks more like an overloaded desk than a disciplinary conversion.

A second notebook in the Johns Hopkins papers belongs to this crowded period. Its strongest core comes from Paris in 1898 to 1899. Lovejoy reused it later. One page is dated 20 December 1905. The notebook is physically accretive. Inserted slips occur in clusters. Folded sheets interrupt the host pages. Lists become diagrams. Cancellations sit beside fresh wording. A small slip appears near the end. The object grew by addition and return.[^6]

The subject matter also changes scale. Buddhism recedes. Comparative religion becomes dominant. The pages move through survival after death, sacrifice, blood, magic, agrarian ritual, cannibalism, fetish, and social differentiation. Marillier stands close to much of the Paris layer. Robertson Smith supplies a major sacrifice theory. Frazer appears repeatedly. Tylor receives special attention. Trumbull carries blood covenant material. Mannhardt enters the agrarian pages. Cameron and Grout supply ethnographic cases.

The first pages on future life are already crowded with determinants. A person’s fate after death may turn on status. It may turn on the manner of death. Burial can matter. Ritual provision can matter. Social relation can matter. Moral desert occupies only part of the field. The notebook revises these categories locally. Its schemes try to decide which factor belongs where. The result is less a doctrine of the afterlife than a comparative grid under constant repair.

That grid becomes important once sacrifice enters. Page 49 contains an inserted slip on Tylor. Lovejoy calls him “the master” of the anthropological school. He gives a reason. Tylor, he says, stays closest to his evidence. Lovejoy adds a practical instruction to himself. Tylor’s books should remain at hand during study of the subject. The line ranks an authority by the distance between theory and proof.

Page 50 turns to sacrifice classes. The first three class families already belong to Robertson Smith’s field. Honorific sacrifice sits beside expiatory sacrifice. Mystical or sacramental sacrifice forms another group. The notebook adds a fourth class, magical sacrifice. It also subdivides the mystical class into divine nourishment and union. Marillier’s course was treating functional sacrifice types at the same time. Lovejoy’s sheet reorganizes this crowded field into a working taxonomy.

The next pages fill those classes with mechanisms. Frazer supplies the god man whose vitality passes to a vigorous successor. Robertson Smith supplies the common meal and blood bond. Expiation receives its own packet. Magical sacrifice becomes especially important. Frazer had already described magic as direct action on nature. Marillier treated sacrifice itself as a possible magical force. Lovejoy’s page brings these grammars together. A sacrifice can work like a charm. Divine will can become irrelevant to the operation.

Then an ethnographic case breaks the theoretical rhythm. Page 55 draws on Verney Lovett Cameron’s account of Manyéma cannibalism. Cameron supplies the descriptive material. Lovejoy’s local label changes its place in the scheme. He classifies the case as alimentary rather than ritual. One short judgment moves an inherited report across categories.

That move matters more than the exotic content. Nineteenth century comparative religion was full of vivid cases. Cannibalism invited evolutionary stories. Sacrifice invited universal genealogies. Lovejoy’s notebook often does something smaller. It asks what a case is doing before it lets the case stand for a stage. The surface practice may resemble ritual consumption. The motive can still be food.

Pages 56 to 60 return to Robertson Smith. Marillier had already challenged a universal communion genealogy. Hubert and Mauss would soon make related criticisms in print. Lovejoy’s pages assemble several mechanisms before restricting the union model. Honorific giving occupies one place. Expiation occupies another. Magical compulsion has its own logic. Cannibalism can move into an alimentary class. The theory narrows as the dossier grows.

Page 65 revisits mixed ritual forms. Lovejoy calls for “delicate analysis.” A current rite can contain elements that once belonged to different contexts. Their present fusion does not erase earlier distinctness. Survivance theory already existed. Tylor and Marillier provided much of the field. Lovejoy’s page uses the idea to separate a concrete complex into several possible rationales.

Page 66 adds another relation. Collective expiation can belong to a whole community. Magical technique can require a specialist. Lovejoy maps the mechanism to the competent performer. The question becomes social as well as causal. Who can do this thing? Who carries the efficacy? A rite that works through communal guilt has a different jurisdiction from a rite that works through technical magical skill.

The distinction helps explain why the notebook keeps returning to priests, groups, victims, and specialists. Mechanisms need bearers. A theory of sacrifice can classify a rite by function. Lovejoy often asks a second question. The relevant social actor may change with the function. The same visible act can therefore belong to different social arrangements.

A later packet uses Grout’s South African material. The source describes a rite involving ancestors, a cow, and the removal of evil. Lovejoy calls the transaction a “veritable bargain.” He then refuses a deferred payment reading. The bargain is immediate in a different sense. He also marks substitution or scapegoat logic. One source case receives more than one analytic relation.

The notebook does this repeatedly. A visible rite can carry a transaction. It can also carry substitution. A meal can be nourishment. It can also establish communion. Blood can transfer life. It can also mark incorporation. The work lies in deciding which relation has evidence in a given case. A general class earns its force one case at a time.

Trumbull’s blood covenant material fills another run of pages. Much of the content belongs directly to Trumbull. Blood carries life. It can heal. It can rejuvenate. It can transmit qualities. Circumcision enters the same broad dossier. Trumbull interprets such practices through covenant, fraternity, incorporation, and adoption. Lovejoy later uses these inherited options when he encounters a competing genealogy.

The pages on fetish and charms create a similar problem. Tylor and Lang had strict criteria for fetishism. West African reports complicated those criteria. Some objects seemed powerful because a spirit inhabited them. Others worked through a more intrinsic efficacy. Kingsley supplied much of the ethnographic material. Lovejoy’s notebook sharpens the possible division. A blood marked gateway can protect by one mechanism. A spirit bound to an object can protect by another.

The distinction between personal agency and direct efficacy will return in 1906. Its component ideas were already widespread. Frazer supplied a mechanical account of magic. Marillier could describe magical sacrifice. Trumbull treated transferable life in blood. Tylor and Lang debated spirit related objects. The Paris notebook puts these materials beside one another. Lovejoy’s contribution appears most clearly when a case is reassigned or a causal path is tested.

The Marillier inserts around pages 92 to 99 deserve a different rhythm. They carry lecture or source material on divinity classes, genealogy, sacrifice, and comparative development. They belong largely to the classroom field. Lovejoy’s own questions are harder to isolate there. The pages still matter. They show what kind of comparative problems were available in Paris. They also explain why the surrounding notebook can move so quickly among ritual functions.

Page 103 returns to a more secure Lovejoy intervention. A branching scheme links cultivated plants, domestic animals, and sacrifice. The genealogy runs into chronology. Agriculture without animal labor complicates one route. Domestication may come too late for another. The page raises the specter of circular reasoning. A proposed ritual history now has to pass an external chronological test.

Page 104 narrows the route. Lovejoy excludes a direct path from wild animal to domestic animal for the specific model under examination. He retains a route through human sacrifice and subsequent modification. The categories themselves were common in the literature. The act of path selection belongs to the notebook. Formal resemblance yields to chronology.

A related problem appears on page 112. Circumcision could be read as a residue of partial human sacrifice. Lovejoy rejects that genealogy. He prefers a line through initiation and blood covenant. Incorporation provides the stronger mechanism. Trumbull supplies much of the positive vocabulary. The notebook supplies the judgment between rival histories.

Near the end, page 119 preserves an explanatory fork. A selected body part can matter because that organ is thought to contain a specific property. Another explanation uses part for whole. The part carries the force of the person more generally. Lovejoy leaves the alternatives beside each other. The case remains open between two mechanisms.

The physical notebook intensifies this effect. A page can hold a stable paragraph beside a later slip. A numbered scheme can be crossed through. A folded insert can place a lecture packet inside a longer argument. The material surface resists a smooth narrative. Lovejoy’s research grew through interruption. The surviving object records those interruptions with unusual clarity.

By 1902 some of these small habits appear in print outside Buddhist philology. “Religion and the Time Process” discusses Aristotle and Vedānta. Lovejoy calls their affinity “obvious.” He then treats eastern influence as “entirely gratuitous” when the Greek sequence supplies an internal route. Similarity is strong enough for comparison. Transmission requires another kind of evidence.[^7]

The sentence is brief, almost incidental. It matters because the Paris notebook repeatedly faced the same problem in ritual genealogy. A similar form can invite a historical path. External chronology may break that path. An internal development can carry more weight. Lovejoy is using the same economy in a different field.

The year 1906 makes the range much wider. In February Lovejoy published “Democracy in the Twentieth Century.” The essay begins from a familiar contrast. The eighteenth century had appealed to Nature. The nineteenth century had made Evolution central. Enlightenment political reasoning had worked with “generic Man.” Evolutionary history fractured that generic figure.

Lovejoy describes the fracture in two directions. Humanity is split “longitudinally” by stage of culture. It is also split “cross wise” by race or nationality. The two axes do different work. Stage carries the stronger developmental hierarchy. Race and national genius mark population difference across the same historical moment. Lovejoy also warns against overworking the race category. The warning leaves the political use of classification fully active.[^8]

The essay moves directly into institutions. A political form suitable for one historical group may fail elsewhere. Colonial policy enters. Female suffrage enters. Black enfranchisement and disfranchisement enter. Lovejoy keeps evaluative ends such as welfare, liberty, order, culture, civic virtue, and a “standard of civilization.” Historical differentiation therefore qualifies institutional transfer while preserving a hierarchy of ends.

The political stakes make the earlier notebook habits look less innocent. Fine classification can restrain a claim. It can also furnish a more elaborate hierarchy. Separating stage from race produces greater analytic precision. The same precision can support judgments about political capacity. Local caution and broad ordering can coexist in one argument.

Two months later Lovejoy published “Kant’s Antithesis of Dogmatism and Criticism.” The scale changes again. He takes an accepted historical contrast and breaks it into propositions. Which doctrines belong to Kant’s predecessors? Where does Kant diverge? Which arguments are new? How large is the discontinuity? The macro category loses coherence under the local audit.

The result resembles the 1897 page on *viññāṇa*. A large ordering seems clear until several relations are separated. Historical succession differs from logical structure. Doctrinal possession differs from argumentative novelty. Once those distinctions become explicit, “dogmatism” and “criticism” can no longer carry the whole story.

April 1906 also brought Lovejoy’s review of Paul Elmer More. Buddhism returns directly. More had offered a positive reconstruction of Nirvāṇa. Lovejoy reached again for the *Sutta Nipāta*. He cited verses 1073 to 1076. The same canonical text had mattered in his 1898 discussion of *upādisesa*. Eight years later it still served as a check on a philosophical interpretation.[^9]

The review praises induction from evidence. It values attention to historical conditions. Antecedents matter. Comparison across literatures matters too. Lovejoy is comfortable moving widely, yet the Buddhist example pulls him back to a specific textual witness. A philosophical idea has to survive contact with the wording of the source.

July 1906 moves in the other direction. “The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy” opens a large comparative object. Lovejoy takes ethnographic and lexical materials from many settings. He searches for a recurring kind of efficacy. The force is impersonal. It can be communicated. It can act in a quasi mechanical fashion. Magic, fetish, taboo, blood, and related phenomena become evidence for a broad conceptual family.[^10]

Lovejoy disputes animist or anthropomorphic master explanations associated with Tylor and others. He still retains the aggregate address “primitive philosophy.” The phrase already had a long anthropological history. His novelty lies elsewhere. He reorganizes a large dossier around a different relation. Impersonal efficacy becomes the organizing principle.

The move resembles the Paris notebook and also exceeds it. In Paris a magical sacrifice could be separated from a sacramental one. A cannibalism case could move from ritual to nourishment. A blood practice could receive competing explanations. In 1906 one family of these local distinctions is gathered upward. A relation that once helped divide cases becomes a feature attributed to “primitive philosophy” at a much larger scale.

The article acknowledges evidential weakness. Lovejoy revises an initially categorical statement. He works through lexical material in search of support. Yet he still makes the aggregate move. “Savage thought” becomes an address. Populations enter through the analyst’s category. The same scholar who narrowed the Kantian category in April broadens the primitive category in July.

There is no contradiction that needs to be solved by a developmental story. The works are months apart. They answer different problems. One macro category fails because local propositions refuse to align. Another macro category is built because diverse cases can be aligned around one causal relation. The intellectual act is scalar. The warrant changes with the object.

The political essay from February makes that scalar choice consequential. “Primitive” could remain a technical term inside comparative religion. It could also join a developmental hierarchy of peoples. Lovejoy’s “Democracy” essay already organizes humanity by stage of culture. His July article supplies a cognitive description attached to the primitive level. The two texts belong to the same year. Their relation deserves historical attention even where direct textual migration remains uncertain.

Lovejoy’s early Buddhist work helps here because its scale is so small. *Upādāna* does not name a people. It names a difficult Buddhist relation. *Viññāṇa* occupies different places under temporal and logical descriptions. The Senart dispute concerns the structure and genealogy of a formula. The 1898 article reduces a large notebook dossier to two terms. By 1906 Lovejoy can move from equally fine distinctions toward a category addressed to whole populations.

This movement also clarifies what “primitive” is doing in Lovejoy. It does not simply mark an early chronological point. It can gather a family of causal expectations. The 1906 article gives those expectations an intellectual unity. Impersonal force, transferable efficacy, and quasi mechanical action become characteristic of a broad level of thought. The category is assembled through comparison across distance.

The Paris notebook had already placed distant cases on the same page. That fact alone creates no genealogy into 1906. Frazer, Tylor, Marillier, Robertson Smith, Trumbull, Mannhardt, and many others supplied the comparative field. Lovejoy worked inside it. The later article shows what happens when one relation is selected from that field and made representative at a larger scale.

A further article from 1907 makes Lovejoy’s concern with warrant explicit in another domain. “The Entangling Alliance of Religion and History” distinguishes cosmic propositions from contingent historical occurrence. Historical propositions carry probabilistic status. Extraordinary events require a heavier documentary burden. Politics appears there as practical and temporal. The article belongs to a later stage, yet it gives language to habits already visible in the notebooks.[^11]

The years 1908 to 1910 provide similar controls. Lovejoy’s pragmatism controversies break claims into propositions. His disputes over historical proof require cumulative evidence. A revision between his 1909 and 1910 Kant work records self correction at the level of categories. He moves from an umbrella classification toward more local distinctions. These later examples make the archival pages easier to read without turning the pages into prophecy.

The mature *Great Chain of Being* changes the geography of the story. Its preface defines the principal historical field through “Occidental philosophy” and “Western thought.” The center is explicit. Buddhism still appears. Vedānta still appears. “Primitive Buddhism” remains available as a comparative form. Asian materials have therefore moved rather than vanished.[^12]

In the 1890s, Buddhist materials were local research objects. Lovejoy worried over their terms. He compared canonical usages. He entered disputes among Indologists. He studied Pāli at Harvard. He planned further Buddhist work in Paris. He sought private reading with Lévi when a course failed to appear. The materials demanded direct technical labor.

In *Great Chain*, Asian materials more often help mark philosophical types or boundaries inside a Western genealogy. They can illuminate a contrast. They can show that a possibility has occurred elsewhere. They can delimit the historical object. The work they perform has changed because the narrative center has changed.

The difference can be seen through the fate of comparison. In 1902 Aristotle and Vedānta can resemble each other while remaining historically separate. In 1906 the *Sutta Nipāta* can check a philosophical account of Nirvāṇa. The primitive philosophy article can gather distant cases into one cognitive pattern. In *Great Chain*, comparative examples support an Occidental sequence. Comparison survives through all these settings. Its historical job shifts.

The archive therefore gives Lovejoy several scales at once. A course card places him in advanced Pāli. An AOS circular places him before orientalist specialists. A notebook page places a Buddhist term inside several relations. A Paris letter places Buddhism beside Hebrew Wisdom. Another notebook places ritual cases inside competing mechanisms. A 1906 essay places populations inside stages. The mature book places Western thought at the center of a long genealogy.

Those scales do not line up automatically. That is precisely why the early evidence is useful. The young Lovejoy often makes the mismatch visible. A temporal sequence can diverge from a logical classification. A borrowed term can acquire a different application. A ritual form can carry a new rationale. A similar practice can have a different genealogy. A source case can move between mechanisms. A broad historical category can survive or fail after local testing.

The same archive also blocks any easy portrait of Lovejoy as a permanent splitter. Page 56 of the Buddhist notebook gathers differently derived terms into one systemic function. Page 68 builds an original and derived scheme. The 1906 primitive article aggregates widely. *Great Chain* constructs one of the most famous large narratives in twentieth century intellectual history. Lovejoy’s precision at the local level could feed broad synthesis.

This is where the obscure Buddhist paper becomes especially revealing. Its problem is neither Buddhism alone nor Lovejoy’s later fame. It captures a scholar learning what can be carried from one relation into another. The process is visible because the material is difficult. Pāli terms resist casual paraphrase. Dependent origination mixes different kinds of order. European scholars disagree over textual history. The technicality forces Lovejoy to show his hand.

Warren teaches him one version of the problem. Senart sharpens another. Oldenberg supplies a massive counterweight. Childers furnishes lexical tools. Rhys Davids supplies translations and textual judgments. The notebook is saturated with other people’s work. Lovejoy’s local acts often occur in half a sentence. They are easy to miss when the whole page is read as authored prose.

The same is true in the Paris notebook. Robertson Smith already has a sacrifice taxonomy. Frazer already has impersonal magic. Marillier already has a differentiated sacrifice field. Tylor already has a theory of survivals. Trumbull already has blood covenant. The pages become historically useful when a concrete case is reclassified, when a path fails chronology, or when the competent social bearer changes with the mechanism.

These are small acts. They also explain why the later large acts require attention. A category such as “primitive philosophy” does not arrive from nowhere. It depends on decisions about equivalence. Cases from different places must be treated as comparable. Different words must be treated as carrying a shared relation. Mechanisms must be abstracted from local contexts. The resulting abstraction then returns to the cases as a description of a collective mentality.

The political consequence follows from the same movement. A stage of culture is also an abstraction from heterogeneous social evidence. Once attributed to a population, it can guide judgments about institutions. Lovejoy’s February 1906 essay says exactly that. Historical fit becomes a criterion for political form. The category can therefore travel from comparative history into policy.

There is no need to make every notebook page political. Most pages are doing something else. The connection appears through scale. A local distinction can remain local. It can also become part of a population level description. The historical question is when the shift occurs and what evidence survives the move.

Lovejoy himself rarely writes that question in abstract form during the 1890s. He writes the operations instead. “If we are to follow the present text.” “Temporally (?) an antecedent.” “Logically a subdivision.” “There is a discoverable logical system.” “I incline to suspect.” “This is all that is required for the purposes of my paper.” Each phrase belongs to a specific problem. Together they give a texture to the work.

The public title changes around the 1897 AOS meeting add another layer. The April circular begins with the *Milinda-pañha*. The Proceedings shift to *upādāna* and the aggregates. The 1898 article replaces the second term with *upādisesa*. The research object keeps being cut down and rearranged. The bibliographical record therefore preserves part of the intellectual labor that the lost paper itself no longer shows.

Harvard makes the chronology denser. Lovejoy began with Sanskrit in 1895. Advanced Pāli followed in 1896 to 1897. The AOS communication appeared during that same academic year. Anthropology 1 appears on his 1897 to 1898 card with an A. His surviving Philosophy 20d paper from June 1898 concerns ethics. By autumn 1898 he was in France, planning philosophy and comparative religion together. The Buddhist article appeared inside this sequence.

The sequence also gives a different picture of specialization. Lovejoy did not approach Buddhism as a philosopher who picked up a few translated examples. He had formal language training. He read Pāli texts in an advanced Harvard course. He entered an orientalist society’s program. He followed philological disputes closely enough to write on technical terms. His later complaint about philosophers reconstructing Buddhism from their own heads therefore had an immediate biographical edge.

At the same time, he never became a conventional Indologist. His Harvard division was Philosophy. His Kant work continued. His letters to Howison are saturated with metaphysics. His fellowship in Paris required philosophy to remain central. Buddhism sat inside a wider attempt to connect textual competence with philosophical reconstruction.

That combination helps explain the density of notebook 004. Lovejoy rarely treats a word as a mere lexical puzzle. *Upādāna* changes the logic of dependent origination. *Viññāṇa* changes the relation between temporal order and classification. *Upādisesa* changes the interpretation of Nirvāṇa. The textual detail has philosophical consequences. The philosophy remains answerable to the text.

The same combination explains his frustration in Paris. Philologists had the literature. Philosophers had large reconstructions. Lovejoy wanted enough technical control to adjudicate the reconstruction. Lévi offered one possible route. The failed Pāli course forced an improvised solution. Reading at Lévi’s house was a small institutional fact. It fits the intellectual problem exactly.

Notebook 005 then changes the kind of evidence. Here the sources are often ethnographic reports rather than canonical texts. The objects are rites rather than Pāli terms. The difficulty shifts from translation to comparison. Yet the pages repeatedly ask a familiar question. What relation does the evidence actually establish?

A Manyéma case can establish alimentary cannibalism. It need not carry a ritual genealogy. A blood rite can establish incorporation. It can also establish transferable life. The source must decide which mechanism is active. A sacrifice theory can fit one class and fail another. A proposed sequence can collapse under external chronology.

The 1902 Aristotle and Vedānta comparison makes this logic portable. Affinity can be obvious. Influence can still lack a route. The 1906 Kant essay makes the same point at the level of a historical category. A label can describe part of a philosophical shift and still fail as an aggregate contrast. The More review makes textual control active again. The primitive article gathers a successful abstraction upward.

Lovejoy’s mature reputation often begins from the last move. He is remembered for unit ideas, large genealogies, and the migration of concepts through Western intellectual history. The early archive complicates that image by showing how much splitting preceded synthesis. It also complicates any celebration of splitting. The same scholar could reaggregate quickly when he believed a relation traveled across cases.

The primitive article is the clearest early example. Lovejoy does not merely collect beliefs. He identifies a causal grammar. Force can be impersonal. It can pass between objects or persons. It can work through contact or possession. These relations allow phenomena from distant settings to enter one analytic field. The field then becomes a claim about “primitive philosophy.”

The move resembles the construction of a unit idea in only a limited sense. Both require abstraction from local expressions. Yet the historical address is different. In the 1906 article the abstraction is attributed to a stage or broad population category. In *Great Chain*, abstract ideas move through a Western textual genealogy. The early primitive paper therefore sits awkwardly beside Lovejoy’s later program. That awkwardness is useful.

It reveals that abstraction itself carries no fixed politics or scale. The same analytic habit can produce a narrow semantic distinction. It can produce a broad cognitive type. It can support a Western genealogy. The effect depends on where the abstraction is returned after extraction.

Lovejoy’s “Democracy” essay makes the return visible. “Generic Man” breaks apart under historical differentiation. The stage category then returns to living populations as a judgment about institutional suitability. Race or nationality supplies another axis. The essay uses these classifications to discuss colonial rule and franchise. The analytic scale reaches directly into politics.

The language of “stage” also links the political essay to the comparative study of religion. Tylor’s *Primitive Culture* had made stages of culture central. Jastrow and other scholars of religion worked inside developmental frameworks. Marillier’s classroom compared ritual forms across peoples and historical levels. Lovejoy inherited this environment. His own writing in 1906 shows both the attraction and danger of aggregate historical classification.

The danger is easier to see because the notebooks preserve the smaller acts. A stage is made from relations that once belonged to individual cases. If those relations are flattened, heterogeneity disappears. If they remain too local, the aggregate category loses force. Lovejoy’s career repeatedly moves along this tension.

That tension also changes how *Great Chain* looks. The book is famous for tracing a persistent set of ideas across centuries. Its Western center is explicit. Yet the comparative outside remains important. Buddhism and Vedānta can mark alternatives. They can clarify what belongs to the Occidental sequence by showing nearby philosophical possibilities. The outside helps shape the boundary of the inside.

The 1890s notebooks reverse that relation. Buddhism itself is inside the problem. Its terms need reconstruction. Its textual layers need sorting. Its doctrinal relations are uncertain. European authorities disagree. Lovejoy has no stable Western narrative in which Buddhism can function as a convenient contrast. He has to work through the material at ground level.

This change of function is more interesting than disappearance. It tells us how a scholar’s archive can be reorganized by later projects. A source once studied for its own technical problems can later serve as comparison. A comparative field can later become background. A local object can become a boundary case. The material stays. Its narrative address changes.

The 1948 bibliography performs a similar reorganization. It places the 1898 Buddhist paper at the front of a career now known for something else. The item looks like an origin because bibliographies create lines. The archive breaks that line apart. Monboddo comes earlier. Harvard Pāli surrounds the paper. The AOS title states multiply the object. Paris extends the project. Later Buddhism reappears in 1906. Mature work changes its function.

The resulting story has no single conversion scene. There is no moment when Lovejoy leaves philology and becomes an intellectual historian. There is no moment when comparison turns into abstraction once and for all. The operations overlap. Source control and aggregation coexist. Local textual work and broad historical classification can occur within the same year.

That overlap is visible already in 1898. Lovejoy’s article can say that terms were borrowed while arrangement remained characteristic. The sentence separates provenance from function. Yet the same article also makes a historical claim about Buddhist doctrine. It reconstructs a system from texts. It places technical terms inside a broader picture of Nirvāṇa and rebirth. Local philology already serves synthesis.

Notebook 005 expands the pattern. Lovejoy can distrust theory that exceeds evidence. He can still create a four class working scheme. He can reclassify one cannibalism case. He can also gather sacrifice materials across continents. He can reject one genealogy while keeping another. Comparative ambition and local restraint occupy the same pages.

The 1906 primitive paper pushes the ambition farther. One family of mechanisms becomes a fundamental concept. The category is broad enough to address “savage thought.” Lovejoy’s own source discipline remains visible in the article’s qualifications. The aggregate nevertheless survives. The paper therefore offers no clean victory of caution over synthesis.

That matters for the history of ideas because Lovejoy’s later method has often been criticized for abstraction. Unit ideas can seem detached from contexts. The early archive shows a scholar who knew very well that relations can change across contexts. It also shows how he could decide that one relation was portable. The problem lies in the warrant for portability, not in abstraction alone.

The Buddhist pages offer a fine example. *Viññāṇa* can be earlier in one sequence and included in another category. Lovejoy keeps both claims. When he later compares Aristotle with Vedānta, affinity can coexist with separate genealogy. When he writes about primitive philosophy, impersonal efficacy becomes portable across many cases. The decision about portability changes from object to object.

This perspective also prevents the archive from becoming a morality play about good localism and bad generalization. Lovejoy’s local judgments could be wrong. His “Original Elements” scheme may fail historically. His Nirvāṇa interpretation can be disputed. His political classifications carry grave consequences. Fine grained method does not guarantee sound history.

What the archive gives is visibility. We can see the moment when he chooses a relation. We can see when he stops. We can see when he crosses out. We can see when a source enters. We can see when a local case changes class. We can see when a public title changes. That visibility makes the later abstractions historically tractable.

The 1897 title sequence is especially valuable here. A historian usually encounters the 1898 article as a finished object. The AOS documents restore two earlier public states. First comes the *Milinda-pañha* argument. Then comes *upādāna* with the aggregates. Finally comes *upādāna* with *upādisesa*. The movement is concrete. It does more work than a retrospective claim about Lovejoy’s method.

The Paris letters do the same for intellectual formation. Lovejoy himself names philosophy and comparative religion before courses begin. He then defines the philology problem. He selects Lévi. A course disappears from the schedule. Private reading takes its place. Vernes serves the Wisdom project. Sabatier is tried and dropped. The year assembles itself through choices.

Notebook 005 does the same for comparison. A theory enters from Robertson Smith. Another enters from Frazer. Marillier’s course supplies a problem field. Tylor is ranked by evidential restraint. Cameron supplies a case. Lovejoy changes the case’s class. Grout supplies another. Lovejoy changes the causal reading. A ritual genealogy hits chronology. The route is revised.

By 1906 these acts can be found in public prose. The Kant category dissolves. The Nirvāṇa reconstruction is checked against the *Sutta Nipāta*. The primitive category expands. The democratic subject is divided by stage. One year contains several scales of classification. Any account that turns them into a simple ladder will miss the simultaneity.

The simultaneity also helps explain Lovejoy’s later confidence in large historical objects. He did not arrive at abstraction through ignorance of local difficulty. He arrived there while practicing local discrimination. That makes the broad claims more historically interesting. It raises the question of how much local uncertainty an aggregate can absorb before its shape changes.

His own writings provide different answers. The Kant category collapses under local pressure. The primitive category survives despite acknowledged gaps. The Great Chain survives as a Western historical sequence while Asian comparison remains at the margins. The object sets the threshold.

This threshold is visible in page 62 of the Buddhist notebook. “This is all that is required for the purposes of my paper.” Lovejoy does not need the strongest philosophical conclusion. A smaller proposition will support the publication. In 1906 he sometimes makes the reverse choice. A larger comparative claim becomes the purpose of the paper, so heterogeneous evidence is organized toward that scale.

The phrase therefore belongs beside the later works without becoming their secret key. It captures a practical habit of intellectual work. Papers have purposes. Evidence is selected for those purposes. Questions outside the purpose can remain open. Another paper may require a different closure.

The obscure Buddhist article gives us access to that practice before Lovejoy’s reputation hardened. Its technical vocabulary slowed him down. Its sources remain visible. Its notebook survives. Its prepublication title states survive. His course record survives. His Paris letters survive through Wilson’s transcriptions. Few early episodes offer such a dense chain of working evidence.

That chain changes how the 1898 paper should be read. It is neither an embarrassing orientalist juvenile nor a hidden blueprint for *Great Chain*. It is a compact public result cut from a much larger research field. The field included Pāli training, philosophical questions, and comparative religion. It included competing European authorities. It included decisions about which relation each kind of evidence could sustain.

The same field also belonged to an imperial scholarly world. Buddhist texts reached Harvard through philological institutions shaped by colonial knowledge. Comparative religion organized peoples through evolutionary categories. “Primitive” could become a technical label for living societies. Lovejoy’s later political writing used developmental distinctions in debates about colonial government and franchise. The archive therefore sits inside histories of power as well as histories of method.

Those histories need precision too. The 1898 Buddhist paper does not itself make a colonial policy argument. The 1906 “Democracy” essay does. Notebook 005 compares ethnographic reports from colonial settings. The primitive article turns such reports into a broad cognitive category. The political force increases as the scale changes. The connection runs through classification rather than through a single declared ideology.

This is why the small page level distinctions matter. If *upādāna* and the aggregates are collapsed, one structure appears. If they are separated, another appears. If a cannibalism report is classified as ritual, it can feed a history of religion. If it is classified as food, its evidentiary role changes. If a blood rite is read through spirit agency, it supports one account of religion. If it is read through direct efficacy, it supports another.

The analyst makes these allocations. Later categories inherit them. “Primitive philosophy” is one such inheritance. It gathers relations that have already been selected from local cases. “Stage of culture” is another. It organizes social differences along a historical axis. Lovejoy’s archive lets us watch the allocations before the aggregate becomes natural.

The archive also makes clear that selection can run both ways. Lovejoy sometimes pulls a case out of a large class. He sometimes puts several cases into a new class. He sometimes leaves alternatives unresolved. He sometimes closes the question for publication. The intellectual style lies in movement between these operations.

That movement is easy to lose in a polished article. Published prose hides discarded routes. Notebook pages preserve them. Bibliographies hide changing titles. Meeting records recover them. Course catalogues hide individual enrollment. Student cards recover it. Biographies paraphrase letters. Archival transcriptions recover the actor’s own wording more closely.

Seen together, these sources place the 1898 Buddhist paper in a dense institutional sequence. Harvard provided language training. The AOS provided a specialist public. Johns Hopkins preserves the working notebook. Paris supplied a new comparative environment. Lévi offered textual expertise. Marillier’s classroom supplied another comparative field. Lovejoy moved through all of them before thirty.

He carried different objects through each setting. The *Milinda-pañha* belonged to the 1897 communication. *Upādāna* and the aggregates defined the late April title. *Upādisesa* entered the published pair. Hebrew Wisdom joined Buddhism in Paris through the problem of evil. Sacrifice dominated the second notebook. Primitive efficacy became a public comparative abstraction in 1906.

The objects changed faster than the later career narrative suggests. Lovejoy’s method also changes with them. Pāli philology demands textual usage. Sacrifice demands functional classification. Ritual genealogy demands chronology. Political institutions demand historical population categories. A history of ideas demands another kind of continuity across texts.

The mature Lovejoy is therefore already difficult to isolate from the young one. Some habits persist. Some objects disappear. Some return in new roles. The *Sutta Nipāta* is a good example. It is a course text at Harvard. It becomes evidence in the 1898 article. It returns in the More review of 1906. Later mature work no longer centers that kind of Buddhist semantic dispute.

The repetition is modest and concrete. It gives us more than a claim about “influence.” The same canonical source is used for semantic control at two dates. The same scholar who learned advanced Pāli at Harvard can still reach for that text eight years later. The use is local each time. No grand genealogy is needed.

The same restraint helps with Lévi. Lovejoy wanted to study under him for a specific purpose. The formal course failed to appear. Private reading was planned instead. This makes Lévi part of the formation story. It gives no basis for assigning Lovejoy a Lévi method. The evidence is already rich without that shortcut.

Marillier should be handled similarly. His course and writings place Lovejoy inside a highly differentiated debate on sacrifice. Several notebook packets are clearly Marillier mediated. Lovejoy participated actively in the seminar environment. The local reclassifications remain important precisely because the larger theoretical field was shared.

This crowded authorship is a strength. It allows the archive to show intellectual work as a social process. Lovejoy’s pages are built from Warren, Senart, Oldenberg, Childers, Frazer, Tylor, Marillier, Trumbull, and many more. His own contribution often lies between their sentences. Intellectual history becomes visible at the seam.

The seam can be tiny. A question mark after “temporally” matters. Initials after an ontological question matter. A label on a cannibalism case matters. A note praising Tylor’s evidential restraint matters. A chronological objection beside a ritual genealogy matters. A title change in a meeting record matters.

Large claims emerge from such tiny decisions. The primitive article is built from decisions about which cases belong together. *Great Chain* is built from decisions about which formulations count as instances of an idea. Lovejoy’s later method therefore has a material prehistory in acts of selection, even where the early objects differ radically from the mature ones.

Yet the direction also runs backward. Knowing the later Lovejoy can distort the notebooks. “Original Elements” can look like a unit idea scheme. The temporal and logical distinction can look like a mature methodology in miniature. The primitive article can look like an inevitable bridge. The archive becomes more interesting when those retrospective labels are withheld.

Then the young Lovejoy appears at work amid uncertainty. He is learning Pāli. He is presenting or submitting specialist material to the AOS. He is copying large amounts of other scholarship. He is testing Senart. He is following Oldenberg. He is planning a Paris project. He is changing courses. He is building comparative notebooks. The later system has yet to define the scene.

This earlier scene also changes the emotional temperature of the evidence. The notebook is less grand than the mature book. It contains irritation. It contains hesitation. It contains first person suspicion. It contains provisional schemes. It contains page level stopping rules. The research feels unfinished because it was unfinished.

That unfinished quality is historically useful. It shows where a category had to be made. “Primitive philosophy” later appears as a finished analytic object. In 1898 to 1899 the notebook still contains separate mechanisms. Magical compulsion sits beside expiation. Alimentary consumption sits beside ritual. Social bearers differ. Historical routes compete. The later unity has yet to be assembled.

The same is true of Buddhism. “Buddhism” can appear as a philosophical system in later comparative writing. Notebook 004 presents something more fractured. Dependent origination has layers. Terms have disputed uses. Historical provenance remains open. Logical order differs from temporal sequence. Nirvāṇa requires textual adjudication. The object has to be built from relations.

Lovejoy’s early archive therefore sits at a productive distance from his mature reputation. It neither anticipates everything nor belongs to another career. It records the work through which scholarly objects are made. Some are made smaller. Some are made larger. Some are left open.

The 1948 bibliography accidentally preserves that distance. Its first item is too technical to look like the famous Lovejoy. That is why it deserves attention. The paper points backward to an intensive period of language study and forward to later comparative work. It also resists both directions. Its own argument remains about two Buddhist terms.

Read from the archive, the paper acquires a different density. Behind *upādāna* stand Lanman’s Pāli course, the *Milinda-pañha* communication, Warren’s classifications, Senart’s composite formula, and the notebook cross maps. Behind *upādisesa* stand Oldenberg’s semantic field, the *Sutta Nipāta*, and Lovejoy’s usage based argument. Behind the final ten pages stand months of selection.

Paris adds another density. Lovejoy’s complaint about philologists and philosophers sounds abstract until the Harvard record is placed beside it. He had already occupied both sides of the divide. He had philosophical training. He had Sanskrit and Pāli. His project was born from the difficulty of joining forms of expertise that institutions separated.

Notebook 005 then shows what happens when the textual object gives way to a comparative one. The problem becomes harder to contain. Ethnographic cases proliferate. Theories overlap. Mechanisms migrate. Sources disagree. Lovejoy’s solution is often to cut again. Which function? Which bearer? Which path? Which evidence? The cuts make comparison possible.

Comparison then makes aggregation possible. Once cases are separated by mechanism, one mechanism can be traced across many cases. The primitive article performs exactly that move with impersonal efficacy. Separation creates the conditions for a new totality. The archive makes both halves visible.

That pattern helps explain why Lovejoy could be skeptical about some total categories while constructing others. A totality survives when he believes a relation remains stable across instances. It fails when local inspection breaks that stability. The judgment is historical and evidentiary. It is also shaped by the categories available in his field.

Tylor’s importance in notebook 005 is suggestive here. Lovejoy praises his restraint. Yet Tylor himself built vast developmental schemes. Evidential caution and large scale anthropology belonged to the same scholarly persona. Lovejoy could admire one without abandoning the other. The apparent paradox disappears once scale is treated as a choice rather than a temperament.

The same applies to Lovejoy’s politics. His 1906 essay can warn against excessive use of race while still employing race or nationality as a cross wise category. He can distinguish that axis from stage. He can still rank stages of culture. Precision does not dissolve hierarchy. It refines its terms.

This matters for any account of intellectual history that treats method as politically self sufficient. A careful method can serve very different ends. Lovejoy’s archive proves this through proximity. The same year contains a source based Buddhist correction, a dismantled Kantian category, a developmental political essay, and a broad primitive philosophy claim. Method has no single political destination.

The archive also suggests a way to read Lovejoy’s later Western focus. The shift toward Occidental history did not require forgetting Asia. It required reallocating its function. Buddhism could become comparison. Vedānta could become analogy. Western texts could carry the main genealogy. The boundary of the object changed while the comparative repertoire remained available.

Such reallocations are already present on the notebook page. A term can move from lexical object to systemic function. A rite can move from religious to alimentary class. A source case can move from one genealogy to another. The mature historical field is another allocation, performed at a much larger scale.

There is a final irony in Lovejoy’s complaint from Auxerre. He feared philosophers who built Buddhism from their own heads. His later reputation would rest on a method often accused of abstracting ideas from their local settings. The early archive makes the irony sharper. Lovejoy knew the danger intimately. He had trained himself against it in one field. He also learned how to abstract when he believed the evidence allowed it.

The result was never a simple rule against abstraction. It was a practice of deciding when abstraction could travel. That practice could succeed. It could fail. It could become politically loaded. It could produce a narrow article or a grand book. The Buddhist notebook lets us see the decision before the abstraction has become natural.

That is enough reason to recover the odd first item in Lovejoy’s bibliography. The 1898 article belongs to a sequence of concrete acts. A student chooses advanced Pāli. A paper changes title. A notebook interrupts Warren. Senart is accepted on one point and rejected on another. A question receives the initials A.O.L. A stronger issue is closed because the paper requires less. A canonical passage is carried into print.

Then the scene changes. Paris rearranges the available disciplines. A private reading substitutes for a missing class. Marillier’s comparative world fills another notebook. Tylor is kept at hand. Cameron’s case changes category. A ritual genealogy meets chronology. Circumcision changes genealogy. Competing mechanisms survive to the end of a page.

The later publications amplify those operations. Aristotle and Vedānta resemble without a historical bridge. Kant’s macro category breaks under inspection. The *Sutta Nipāta* checks More’s Nirvāṇa. Primitive efficacy becomes a large comparative object. Generic Man becomes historically stratified. Western thought becomes the center of *Great Chain*.

None of these scenes needs a hidden master method to connect them. Their connection lies in the work itself. Lovejoy keeps deciding which relation can carry which claim. He also keeps deciding how far that relation can travel. The archive catches those decisions before later reputation smooths them into a career.

The strange bibliography can therefore remain strange. Its first Buddhist item does not have to become an origin. It can remain a technical article with an unusually rich archive behind it. That archive lets us watch Lovejoy at several scales before the history of ideas became his defining address. The value lies in the motion between those scales, visible one page at a time.
