# Batch 50 — United States × France: Lovejoy across two late-1890s infrastructures for the historical study of religion

Date: 2026-08-18  
Status: synced  
Scope: cross-national reconstruction joining the Harvard / American Orientalist-history-of-religions environment to the Paris EPHE / sciences religieuses environment, with notebooks `MS38_004_001_061_004` and `MS38_004_001_061_005` as the material hinge.

## Core result

The strongest current reconstruction is no longer two parallel stories, `American Orientalism` and `French comparative religion`. Lovejoy moved through a **transatlantic field whose institutional forms were asymmetrical**.

In the United States, the historical/comparative study of religion was distributed across Sanskrit and Semitic chairs, the American Oriental Society and `JAOS`, the Harvard Oriental Series, temporary lecture committees, clubs, museums, libraries and individual scholars. In Paris, the same broad aspiration had acquired a more concentrated institutional form through the Collège de France chair in history of religions, the Vᵉ Section of the EPHE (`sciences religieuses`), the *Revue de l'histoire des religions*, the Musée Guimet, and by 1900 an international congress of the history of religions.

Lovejoy's trajectory crosses these forms directly:

> **Harvard/Cambridge, 1895–1898 → notebook 004 + JAOS 1898 → Paris, 1898–1899 → Vernes / Jean Réville / Sylvain Lévi + documented Marillier EPHE participation → notebook 005 → return to the United States → 1900 formulation / 1906 `Primitive Philosophy`.**

This should be understood as **field transfer and recomposition**, not as a national influence story in which France replaces Harvard. The American environment supplied texts, philological controls, journals and scholarly networks; Paris supplied a denser institutional workshop for `sciences religieuses`, above all Marillier's comparative-religion seminar. Lovejoy carried operations, questions and source packets across the Atlantic.

## 1. The material objects themselves already register the move

Notebook 004 is materially Harvard. Its cover reads:

> `A. O. Lovejoy / Sankhya + Buddhism / Harvard Cooperative Society.`

The notebook is a working dossier on Buddhist technical terms, dependent origination, Sāṁkhya relations, khandhas and Nirvāṇa. Its source ecology includes Warren, Childers, Oldenberg, Rhys Davids, Buddhaghosa, Hardy, Hopkins and, crucially, Émile Senart. It culminates in the same philological problems published in Lovejoy's 1898 *Journal of the American Oriental Society* article, which identifies him as `Harvard University, Cambridge, Mass.`

Notebook 005 is materially Paris. The front pastedown advertises:

> `LIBRAIRIE / PAPETERIE ... CABANON ... 162, Rue Saint-Jacques, 162 / PARIS / En face de l'École de Droit.`

That shift in stationery is not itself intellectual evidence, but in combination with the EPHE records it becomes unusually useful material context. The two notebooks permit a visual opening for a short article: **Harvard Cooperative Society → Cabanon, rue Saint-Jacques**. They are physical traces of the institutional movement we are trying to reconstruct.

## 2. Wilson's biography gives the broad American-to-Paris itinerary but under-describes the EPHE side

Daniel J. Wilson's biography states that Lovejoy's French year ran from September 1898 to August 1899 and had initially been intended as research toward a dissertation. Instead he pursued his own interests, chiefly a historical approach to comparative religion.

Wilson's Paris reconstruction is important and specific:

- Lovejoy intended to study at the Sorbonne;
- he studied the Psalms with **Maurice Vernes**;
- he frequently attended **Jean Réville's** Saturday lectures on the Fourth Gospel;
- he hoped to take Pāli Buddhism with **Sylvain Lévi**; because Lévi was not offering the course, Lovejoy occasionally went to Lévi's home to read sacred texts with him;
- he worked extensively in the Bibliothèque Nationale and other Paris libraries on Jewish and other religious thought.

This remains correct as far as it goes. The new archival point is that a Sorbonne-centered summary misses Lovejoy's strongest documented regular comparative-religion context: **the EPHE Vᵉ Section under Léon Marillier**.

The correction should therefore be additive, not adversarial:

> **Wilson reconstructed Lovejoy's Paris year from correspondence and later evidence around Sorbonne/Vernes/Réville/Lévi; the EPHE annual report adds a second, institutionally sharper layer and names Lovejoy as an active auditor in Marillier's seminar.**

This is a substantial biographical upgrade because it changes `Paris as research trip` into `Paris as documented seminar participation`.

## 3. The EPHE report makes the French side unusually hard evidence

The 1898–1899 report of the EPHE, Section des sciences religieuses, p. 33, `Religions des peuples non civilisés`, lists:

> `Auditeurs ayant pris une part active aux travaux : MM. Lovejoy, W. S. Andrews et Lorette.`

The two conferences described on the same page reproduce the architecture of 005 with exceptional precision.

### Monday: survivance / amoral eschatology

Marillier's explicit task was to establish that no moral element enters primitive eschatology at its origin. The afterlife is `continuation` rather than compensation, repair or punishment of terrestrial life. New evidence came especially from the two Americas.

005 pp. 16–30 works through almost exactly this problem: manner of death, taboo/custom, suicide, ritual condition, social status, missionary contamination, happy hunting grounds, continuity of earthly occupations, and the late/infiltrated appearance of moral desert. The inserted slip on PDF p. 29 reads:

> `Marillier — Survivance — 12 June`.

### Tuesday: human sacrifice / ritual anthropophagy

Marillier distinguished expiatory, funerary, anthropophagic and magical human sacrifice; compared southern African/Bantu, Guinea-coast, North American and non-Aryan Indian materials; and examined bloody animal sacrifice with special emphasis on agrarian and fecundating rites.

005 pp. 46–119 develops precisely these separations and repeatedly refuses to infer mechanism from outward ritual form. Its later synthesis makes sacramental communion comparatively exceptional rather than the universal key to sacrifice.

This is the central French anchor. The notebook is not simply `French-influenced`; its core is now best read as a material trace of Lovejoy's active participation in a named EPHE problem field.

## 4. Paris was institutionally different from Harvard in exactly the way that matters here

Modern institutional histories of French religious studies emphasize the transformation produced by the new Third Republic infrastructure:

- a chair of history of religions at the Collège de France, first occupied by Albert Réville in 1880;
- creation of the Vᵉ Section of the EPHE, `sciences religieuses`, in 1886;
- separation of confessional theology from a public, comparative and historical study of religious phenomena;
- combination of philology, history, psychology, ethnology and, increasingly, sociology;
- *Revue de l'histoire des religions*, founded in 1880 by Maurice Vernes.

Marillier sits squarely inside this institutional concentration. The EPHE prosopographical dictionary records his `Religions des peuples non civilisés` teaching from 1890 to 1901, preceded by an 1888 free course. That 1888 course is methodologically revealing: its first semester involved a **critical study of different orders of testimony** concerning the religion of peoples then called `non civilisés`, followed by psychological analysis; the second semester treated cult of the dead and animism in Africa and Oceania.

From 1896 Marillier co-directed the *Revue de l'histoire des religions* with Jean Réville; the two were also secretaries of the organizing commission for the 1900 Paris International Congress of the History of Religions.

This makes the French setting more than a list of teachers. It was a **research infrastructure for criticism, classification, comparison and publication**.

## 5. The American field was real, but more distributed

The American side should not be reduced to `Harvard philosophy`. By the time Lovejoy entered Harvard in 1895, a substantial if institutionally dispersed history-of-religions / Oriental-studies ecology already existed.

### Harvard Sanskrit / Buddhist infrastructure

Charles Rockwell Lanman was Harvard professor of Sanskrit from 1880 to 1926. Harvard's archival finding aid states that Lanman and his former student **Henry Clarke Warren** founded the Harvard Oriental Series in 1891 and that Lanman edited it until 1934. Lanman's papers preserve correspondence, teaching material, student records and Harvard Oriental Series files. The same finding aid points to the separate **History of Religions Club records, 1892–1952 (HUD 3454.xx)**.

004's use of Warren is therefore not just a bibliographic accident. Warren was part of a local Harvard Buddhist/Sanskrit production infrastructure, even though we still need direct evidence for Lovejoy's personal relation to Warren or Lanman.

### American Lectures on the History of Religions

The front matter to Daniel G. Brinton's *Religions of Primitive Peoples* records that an American committee had been created in 1891 to organize public courses in the history of religions in multiple American cities. **Crawford H. Toy of Harvard chaired the committee; Morris Jastrow Jr. served as secretary.**

Its first lecturer was T. W. Rhys Davids, whose 1894–95 course on the history and literature of Buddhism was delivered in Baltimore, Boston at the Lowell Institute, Brooklyn, Ithaca and New York. The next courses were:

- 1896–97: Daniel G. Brinton, *Religions of Primitive Peoples*;
- 1897–98: T. K. Cheyne, Jewish religious life after the Exile;
- 1898–99: Karl Budde, religion of Israel to the Exile.

The chronology is striking against Lovejoy's notebooks: Buddhism, `primitive peoples`, Jewish religion. **No evidence has yet been found that Lovejoy attended these lecture courses.** They should be used as evidence of an American problem ecology and an availability structure, not as direct influence.

005 does independently contain a bibliographic slip for Brinton's *The American Race*; 004 is saturated with Rhys Davids. That makes the lecture network a priority archival lead, but it does not license an attendance claim.

## 6. Morris Jastrow is the best control for the transatlantic corridor

Morris Jastrow is useful because his career demonstrates that an American scholar could move through the French institutions and then attempt to build an American analogue.

Persée's authority record states that Jastrow studied in Europe, attended the **Collège de France and EPHE**, and moved in the circles of Renan, Oppert, Derenbourg and Halévy before returning to the University of Pennsylvania, where he taught Semitic languages and comparative history of religions.

A recent Collège de France colloquium on `American Students of Near Eastern Languages in Paris, 1875–1900` makes the larger structural point: Paris offered an unusually rich but institutionally complex constellation—Collège de France, EPHE, Langues Orientales, Louvre, Sorbonne, libraries and learned societies—yet relatively few Americans chose it compared with German universities. Jastrow is one of the specifically identified American Paris cases. Lovejoy therefore belongs to a small but historically intelligible American route into this French scholarly ecology.

Even more useful is Jastrow's own contemporary institutional language. In his prefatory note to Edward Washburn Hopkins's *Religions of India* (1895), he juxtaposes:

- the American lectures committee;
- a special department for the subject at the University of Paris;
- Musée Guimet;
- *Revue de l'histoire des religions*;
- European and American chairs in the historical study of religion.

By 1899, however, Jastrow still complained in `The Historical Study of Religions in Universities and Colleges` that even Harvard lacked a dedicated chair for the historical/scientific study of religion. A recent historiographical reconstruction shows that he explicitly looked to European institutionalization, and soon to the Paris 1900 congress, as a possible stimulus for American development.

This gives us the most important institutional comparison:

> **Paris had a concentrated `sciences religieuses` apparatus; the United States had an increasingly active but scattered network.**

Lovejoy's Paris year can therefore be read as movement from one organizational regime of knowledge to another.

## 7. JAOS 20 (1899) shows how quickly the problem field reappeared on the American side

Volume 20 of the *Journal of the American Oriental Society* (1899), immediately after Lovejoy's own 1898 JAOS intervention and his Paris year, contains an almost unnervingly compact snapshot of the American field:

- Charles Carroll Everett, `The Psychology of the Vedanta and Sankhya Philosophies`, pp. 309–316;
- Morris Jastrow Jr., `The Historical Study of Religions in Universities and Colleges`, pp. 317–325;
- Crawford H. Toy, `Relation between Magic and Religion`, pp. 327–331;
- Charles R. Lanman, obituary notice of Henry Clarke Warren, pp. 332–337.

Earlier in the same volume Toy also published `Taboo and Morality`, pp. 151–156.

This does not prove that JAOS 20 responded to Lovejoy. What it demonstrates is that **the exact categories we now encounter across 004 and 005—Sāṁkhya, scientific history of religion, magic, taboo/morality, Warren's Buddhist philology—were co-present in the American learned journal ecology at the turn of 1898–99.**

That matters historiographically because Lovejoy's Paris training did not return into an empty American space. It returned into a field already trying to institutionalize many of the same problems.

## 8. The Senart and Marillier lines should now be separated but joined

France enters the two notebooks by two different evidentiary routes.

### 004: France as textual interlocutor

Émile Senart is inside the live 1896–98 European Indological controversy. 004 directly records his `À propos de la théorie bouddhique des douze nidānas`; Lovejoy's 1898 JAOS article attacks Senart's `upādāna = upādānaskandha` inference. Our recovered Senart/Jacobi/Oldenberg primaries show that this was an international technical controversy already committed to term decomposition, borrowing, filiation and historical stratification.

The French role here is largely **printed philological argument**.

### 005: France as pedagogical and institutional workshop

Marillier is not merely cited. Lovejoy is documented in the room, as an active auditor; the course sequence maps onto the notebook; the notebook contains a dated Marillier slip; Lovejoy later attributes his 1900 conclusions strongly to his studies under Marillier.

The French role here is **pedagogy, research practice and institutional participation**.

Keeping these two vectors distinct prevents `France` from becoming a vague influence category.

## 9. Mauss supplies a second French relay, but through Marillier rather than direct Lovejoy contact

The newest batch-49 audit sharpens the French side further. Marcel Mauss was an earlier/adjacent Marillier student. Mauss explicitly acknowledged that Marillier communicated bibliographies and course notes to him for his 1896–97 work; later Mauss remembered that Marillier taught them above all `la critique des faits`.

Current evidence still gives **no direct Lovejoy–Mauss meeting, correspondence, citation or textual dependence**. The historically defensible relation is Marillier-mediated and infrastructural:

> **Marillier bibliography / course apparatus → Mauss**
>
> **Marillier seminar / problem field → Lovejoy 005**

The comparative payoff is strong precisely because the outcomes diverge. Hubert/Mauss increasingly socialized magical efficacy; Lovejoy's 1906 `Primitive Philosophy` increasingly causalized it as impersonal, transferable, quasi-mechanical efficacy. This is a useful example of **one training/problem environment yielding different explanatory abstractions**.

## 10. A second American control: Laetitia Moon Conard

The existing Paris lead on Elizabeth Laetitia Moon (later Conard) should now be folded into the transatlantic frame. Secure facts currently establish that she studied in Paris in 1897–98 in a Sorbonne / École des Hautes Études environment, completed a Chicago doctorate in 1899 on Algonkin ideas of future life, and had that dissertation translated into French by Marillier.

Her formal enrollment in Marillier's course remains unproved. Even so, she is valuable because she demonstrates another American route into almost exactly the same Paris problem field immediately before Lovejoy: **American future-life research → Paris sciences religieuses → Marillier mediation → American doctoral production.**

If enrollment records, correspondence or a notebook can tie her directly to Marillier, she becomes a first-rate control witness for Lovejoy 005.

## 11. Revised paper-level thesis

The transatlantic reconstruction permits a stronger claim than either `Lovejoy as American Orientalist` or `Lovejoy was influenced by French comparative religion`:

> **Lovejoy's late-1890s notebooks record movement between two different infrastructures for making religion into a historical-comparative object. At Harvard, Buddhist and Oriental scholarship reached him through a dispersed American apparatus of Sanskrit philology, the Harvard Oriental Series, JAOS, public lectures and imported European controversy. In Paris, the same broad comparative ambition was more institutionally concentrated in the EPHE's sciences religieuses, where Lovejoy is documented as an active participant in Léon Marillier's seminar. Notebook 004 shows him working inside an international philological controversy; notebook 005 shows him inside a comparative-religion workshop that classified testimony, ritual mechanism and ethnographic cases. The historical significance lies less in identifying a single national source for a later `method` than in following how Lovejoy carried evidentiary operations across institutions and recomposed them after his return to the United States.**

A shorter formula for the JHI Blog:

> **Harvard gave Lovejoy an international textual argument; Paris gave him an institutional comparative-religion workshop. The notebooks preserve the crossing.**

## 12. What this changes in the JHI Blog architecture

The blog no longer needs to begin with an abstract claim about the origin of `history of ideas`. The two notebook covers can do the historical work.

Possible opening sequence:

1. 004: Harvard Cooperative Society label, `Sankhya + Buddhism`.

3. Cross the Atlantic.
4. 005: Paris stationer's pastedown, rue Saint-Jacques.

This gives the short piece a documentary narrative rather than a retrospective methodological teleology.

## 13. Evidentiary status

### Secure

- 004 is a Harvard Cooperative Society notebook.
- Lovejoy's 1898 Buddhist article appeared in JAOS under Harvard affiliation.
- 004 directly engages Senart and a broader international Buddhist philological controversy.
- Lovejoy was in France from September 1898 to August 1899; Wilson documents Vernes, Jean Réville, Sylvain Lévi and Paris library work.
- 005 is materially a Paris notebook from Cabanon, 162 rue Saint-Jacques.
- EPHE 1898–99 explicitly names Lovejoy as an active auditor in Marillier's `Religions des peuples non civilisés`.
- Marillier's two 1898–99 conference sequences map closely onto the main structural blocks of 005.
- 005 contains `Marillier — Survivance — 12 June`.
- Harvard's Lanman/Warren infrastructure and the American history-of-religions lecture committee existed before and during Lovejoy's Harvard years.
- Jastrow explicitly moved through Paris institutions and then advocated institutional historical study of religions in the United States.

### Strong interpretation

- 004 and 005 can be treated as material traces of Lovejoy's passage between two differently organized scholarly infrastructures.
- Paris supplied a more concentrated institutional experience of `sciences religieuses` than Lovejoy had encountered at Harvard.
- The most useful transatlantic object is not doctrine but a set of evidentiary operations: criticism of testimony, classification, source comparison, separation of mechanism from outward form, and distinction of historical provenance from present function.

### Not established

- that Lovejoy attended the American Lectures on the History of Religions in Boston or elsewhere;
- that Lovejoy was a member of the Harvard History of Religions Club;
- a direct personal relation between Lovejoy and Lanman or Warren;
- direct correspondence between Harvard figures and Marillier concerning Lovejoy;
- direct Lovejoy–Mauss contact;
- that Marillier supplied Lovejoy with the same physical bibliographies/notes he supplied Mauss;
- exact publication month/order of Lovejoy's 1898 JAOS paper relative to his September move to France;
- that every later insert in 005 belongs to the 1898–99 seminar itself.
