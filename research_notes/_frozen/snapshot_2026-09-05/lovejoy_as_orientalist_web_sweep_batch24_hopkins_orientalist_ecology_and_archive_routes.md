# Batch 24 — Johns Hopkins after 1910: Orientalist expertise around Lovejoy and the archival routes into mature work

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced

## Research move

The strict-core sequence through 1907 raises a second question for the `Lovejoy as Orientalist` project: **what happened after Lovejoy entered Johns Hopkins in 1910?** The answer should not be sought only by scanning his later published books for Asian references. Johns Hopkins archival metadata now exposes a much more concrete route: Lovejoy's mature intellectual environment and papers connect him to major specialists in Semitic/Near Eastern studies, Indology and Inner/East Asia.

The evidentiary model changes by period. Early Lovejoy performs specialist work himself: Sanskrit/Pāli/Buddhist terminology, Indian philosophical comparison, comparative religion. Mature Lovejoy increasingly appears to work in an environment of **distributed area and linguistic expertise**, some of which enters projects he organized directly.

## 1. MS-0038 is now a central Orientalist archive target, not just a general Lovejoy fonds

Johns Hopkins Special Collections describes the **Arthur Oncken Lovejoy papers, MS-0038** as 39.4 cubic feet, spanning 1872–1963/1973. The collection includes lecture notes, notebooks, diaries, writings, annotated books and extensive correspondence.

Most relevant arrangement facts:

- Boxes 1–59: lecture notes, notebooks and writings;
- Boxes 72–84: correspondence;
- collection open for use, off-site, with advance retrieval notice;
- Daniel J. Wilson prepared a detailed item index and a complete correspondence index in 1974–75; current finding aid preserves that intellectual arrangement.

The official finding aid's `Related Names` already includes several figures directly relevant to Orientalist/intellectual-history reconstruction:

- **William Foxwell Albright**;
- **Paul Haupt**;
- **Owen Lattimore**.

This does not by itself identify the content or form of every relation, but it is enough to stop treating the Hopkins years as an archive-free gap.

## 2. Owen Lattimore: one exact correspondence location is already exposed

The Hopkins finding aid gives a precise Lovejoy-paper item:

> **Owen Lattimore correspondence — Box 77, Folder 11, item 10.**

Lattimore was a major scholar of Inner Asia and later taught Chinese history at Hopkins. The existence of a discrete correspondence item is direct evidence of contact, although the date, direction, subject and intellectual content of that item are not exposed in the public metadata.

This is therefore an immediate archival target, especially for the mature China/transmission strand. It must not yet be used to claim that Lattimore informed Lovejoy's 1933 China essay.

## 3. Paul Haupt: Semitic philology is present in Lovejoy's archival environment

The Hopkins authority record for **Paul Haupt (1858–1926)** identifies him as a philologist and Professor of Semitic Languages at Johns Hopkins. His own papers include a volume titled `Notes from the Oriental Seminary`.

Haupt also appears as a related person in the Arthur Oncken Lovejoy papers. Public metadata does not yet expose an exact Haupt–Lovejoy item, so the relation remains an archival lead rather than demonstrated correspondence.

This nevertheless matters because Lovejoy arrived at Hopkins in 1910 into an institution with an established Semitic/Oriental philological infrastructure. The project should test whether his 1907 Jewish-thought work, later primitivism project, or history-of-ideas activities intersected with Haupt's circle or materials.

## 4. William Foxwell Albright: from archive relation to History of Ideas Club participation

Albright appears as a related name in MS-0038. More significantly, Hopkins's **History of Ideas Club records, RG-15-120**, establish an institutional point of contact.

The club was founded in 1923 by Lovejoy, George Boas and H. C. Lancaster. Its records include minutes, abstracts of papers, correspondence, speaker/topic lists and handwritten Lovejoy letters. The official finding aid names **W. F. Albright** among notable speakers.

This provides direct evidence that Lovejoy's mature history-of-ideas institution did not operate only within philosophy/literature. A leading biblical archaeologist and ancient Near Eastern specialist participated in its discussion ecology.

Evidence limit: the public summary does not identify Albright's paper title/date. The minutes and speaker/topic lists should be checked before drawing a substantive connection.

## 5. P.-E. Dumont: mature Indian expertise becomes a direct collaborative component

Paul-Émile Dumont is especially important because he links the early Indian-philosophy strand to Lovejoy's mature documentary history.

Karttunen's *Persons of Indian Studies* records:

- Sanskrit study under Pullé;
- further study under Oldenberg and Deussen;
- later work with Macdonell and Caland;
- Visiting Professor at Johns Hopkins in 1929;
- from 1931, Professor of Sanskrit and Indology at Hopkins.

Bibliographic records for Lovejoy and Boas, *Primitivism and Related Ideas in Antiquity* (1935), explicitly identify **supplementary essays by W. F. Albright and P.-E. Dumont**. CiNii, Persée, Cambridge review metadata and later Hopkins Press records all preserve this authorship structure.

This is more than intellectual proximity. By 1935 Lovejoy's documentary-history project visibly allocates area expertise to specialists in **Ancient Western Asia (Albright)** and **Indian literature/Indology (Dumont)**.

The mature Orientalist strand is therefore best described as **distributed expertise** rather than continued personal philology at the 1898 level.

## 6. A periodized model of Lovejoy's Orientalist practice

The emerging chronology can now be stated more precisely:

### 1895–1907 — personal specialist competence and direct practice

- Sanskrit/Buddhist textual training;
- 1898 Pāli/Buddhist philological publication;
- Paris reading with Lévi and comparative religion with Marillier;
- 1901 Buddhism teaching;
- 1902 Vedānta/Buddhism category-history;
- 1907 current Vedānta review.

### 1910s–1930s — institutional embedding and distributed expertise

- Hopkins brings Lovejoy into proximity with an established Semitic/Oriental philological ecology;
- Haupt is a concrete archival/institutional node;
- Albright participates in the History of Ideas Club and later supplies a specialist essay;
- Dumont becomes Hopkins Professor of Sanskrit/Indology and supplies the Indian supplementary essay to the primitivism project;
- Lattimore appears in Lovejoy's correspondence and offers a later Inner/East Asian network node.

This does **not** mean that Lovejoy remained an Orientalist in the same professional sense throughout his career. It shows that Orientalist knowledge did not simply vanish from his scholarly world. Its mode changed from **personal textual practice** to **comparative deployment, institutional circulation and division of linguistic/area expertise**.

## 7. New archival priority: Wilson transcriptions, MS-0873

Hopkins separately holds:

> **Daniel J. Wilson transcriptions of Arthur O. Lovejoy letters, MS-0873 — Box 1.**

The collection consists of mostly typewritten (some handwritten) transcriptions of Lovejoy letters and spans 1872–1962.

This may be the most efficient route to the early biographical evidence already cited by Wilson, especially the 1898 Paris letters underlying claims about Sylvain Lévi, Pāli Buddhism and sacred-text reading. The public collection description does not state that those particular letters are definitely present, so this remains a high-priority retrieval hypothesis rather than a confirmed item list.

Request/search terms should include:

- `Lanman`, `Everett`, `Toy`;
- `Sanskrit`, `Pali/Pāli`, `Buddhism`, `Buddhist`;
- `Sylvain Lévi`, `Levi`, `Paris`, `EPHE`, `Marillier`;
- `Vedanta/Vedānta`, `Shankara/Śaṅkara`, `Ramanuja/Rāmānuja`, `Deussen`, `Woods`;
- `Dumont`, `Albright`, `Haupt`, `Lattimore`;
- `India`, `China`, `Oriental`.
