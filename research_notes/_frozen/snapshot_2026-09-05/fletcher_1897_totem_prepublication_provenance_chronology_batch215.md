# Batch 215 — Fletcher 1897 `Import of the Totem`: prepublication provenance chronology and the limits of proposition-level attribution

Date: 2026-08-26
Status: synced / provenance chronology control
Scope: tighten Batch214's distinction between formal print authorship and collaborative knowledge-production infrastructure. This batch does **not** claim hidden coauthorship. It asks whether the collaboration with Francis La Flesche demonstrably predates Fletcher's August 1897 paper strongly enough that the paper's ethnographic substrate cannot be treated as a purely individual field product.

## Core result

Yes. The collaborative substrate clearly predates `The Import of the Totem` by many years, even though proposition-level authorship of the paper remains Fletcher's unless new manuscript evidence changes that verdict.

Three chronological controls now matter together:

1. **1884:** in her published Omaha pipe-dance paper Fletcher explicitly acknowledged `Mr. Frank La Flesche` for `most valuable assistance in the preparation of this article`.
2. **January 1896:** Fletcher wrote F. W. Putnam that Francis had been working `night and day` with her on Omaha ritual/song material and that it would have been `absolutely impossible` for anyone to obtain that material without him; his uncle's trust had opened otherwise inaccessible ritual knowledge.
3. **August 1897:** `The Import of the Totem` was delivered at the AAAS Detroit meeting of 9–13 August. Fletcher's next documented Omaha field trip **with La Flesche** began only on 23 August and lasted to 9 September. Therefore that particular 1897 field trip cannot be the empirical source of a paper already delivered ten days earlier.

This chronology closes one ambiguity left in Batch214. The relevant alternative is not:

`Fletcher independently formulates 1897 paper → later begins collaborative Omaha research`.

The collaboration was already structurally established long before the paper.

The safe formulation is:

> **Fletcher's 1897 paper is formally a sole-authored print object produced inside a mature collaborative Omaha research infrastructure that had already involved La Flesche in data gathering, ritual access, linguistic/interpretive work and publication preparation for more than a decade.**

What remains unproved is whether La Flesche authored, proposed, translated, or materially shaped any **specific sentence** in the 1897 `common life / will-power / totemic efficacy` argument.

## 1. 1884 direct publication precedent: Fletcher could name La Flesche's article-level contribution

Joanna Cohan Scherer's archival reconstruction cites Fletcher's 1884 publication on the Omaha pipe ceremony and reproduces its first-note acknowledgment:

> `To Mr. Frank La Flesche I desire to acknowledge my indebtedness for most valuable assistance in the preparation of this article.`

This is important for two reasons.

First, it is a **pre-1897 publication-level precedent** for La Flesche's material contribution to Fletcher's Omaha writing.

Second, it shows Fletcher had an established documentary vocabulary for acknowledging La Flesche as an assistant/contributor inside a sole-authored paper.

It therefore strengthens, rather than weakens, the distinction used in Batch214:

- `formal author of 1897 paper = Fletcher`;
- `collaborative research substrate = demonstrably broader`.

It does **not** license automatic transfer of the 1884 acknowledgment to the 1897 article.

Secondary archival reconstruction:

- Joanna Cohan Scherer, `Partnership with a Native American Family`, in *Ethnographers Before Malinowski* (2022), notes 25–26 and surrounding text; based on Peabody Museum records and Fletcher 1884.

Direct-publication control still desirable:

- Alice C. Fletcher, Omaha pipe-dance paper, Peabody Museum report, 1884, opening footnote.

## 2. 1893 credit negotiation: La Flesche actively sought recognition before the 1897 paper

Scherer's archival reconstruction also quotes Fletcher's 13 April 1893 letter to Putnam concerning *A Study of Omaha Indian Music*.

Fletcher says Francis had `a great deal of feeling concerning the recognition of his share in the work involved in this monograph`. He wanted his name on the title page as `aided by Francis La Flesche`; Fletcher says he had spent money, time and labor and had first had the idea of writing out the music. She told Putnam she would be glad to have his name added.

This establishes an actor-level credit problem **before** the 1897 paper.

It should not be overread as evidence that every later Fletcher paper concealed La Flesche's authorship. It does show that publication attribution versus research contribution was a live negotiated issue in their own working relationship.

Role code:

`CREDIT NEGOTIATION / ACTOR-LEVEL = SECURE`.

---

## 3. January 1896: collaboration was epistemically indispensable, in Fletcher's own account

The strongest prepublication letter currently recovered is Fletcher to Putnam, 22 January 1896.

According to Scherer's archival transcription, Fletcher reports that Francis's uncle had spent about ten days with her; she had recorded more than eighty songs belonging to rituals and societies and recorded the `Counting of Honors`; Francis had worked `night and day` and would need to continue doing so. Fletcher then says it would have been **absolutely impossible for anyone to have gained this material without him**, because his uncle's regard for him opened many secrets.

This is not merely a later historian's statement that La Flesche `helped` Fletcher.

It is Fletcher's own contemporary description of a knowledge-access mechanism:

`La Flesche kin relation / trust`
→ `access to ritual specialist / restricted knowledge`
→ `recorded ethnographic material`
→ `Fletcher's research corpus`.

The collaboration is therefore epistemic and social, not clerical.

Primary archive locus via Scherer:

- Fletcher to Putnam, 22 January 1896, Harvard/Peabody Museum records, cited in Scherer 2022, note 38.

Publication-grade use should eventually be checked against the archival image or authorized transcription.

## 4. The 1897 paper predates the August 1897 Omaha field trip

The AAAS Detroit meeting chronology is independently fixed.

Contemporary *Nature* announcements/reports give:

- scheduled Detroit meeting: 9–14 August 1897;
- retrospective report: meeting held 9–13 August 1897.

The NAA reprint record identifies `The Import of the Totem` as a paper read at the Detroit meeting in August 1897.

Scherer's archival reconstruction then gives Fletcher's next Omaha trip:

- 23 August 1897: Fletcher returned to the Omaha Reservation with La Flesche;
- stayed through 9 September;
- Fletcher later told Putnam: `Francis and I took between seventy and eighty photographs & a large number of graphophone records. We secured some valuable material & paved the way for securing a ritual in a year or so.`

Library of Congress field-recording metadata supplies an independent material control: a Hethu'shka Society recording dated **26 August 1897** lists Fletcher and La Flesche as collectors.

Therefore:

`Detroit paper delivered by 13 Aug`

precedes

`documented Fletcher–La Flesche Omaha fieldwork beginning 23 Aug`.

This matters because the 1897 trip cannot be used retrospectively as the source for claims already in the paper. The paper must draw on earlier materials, earlier work, or knowledge already in Fletcher's possession before Detroit.

This makes the pre-1897 collaboration documented in sections 1–3 more relevant, not less.

Controls:

- *Nature* 56 (1 July 1897): Detroit meeting announced for 9–14 Aug.
- *Nature* 56 (26 Aug 1897): reports meeting held 9–13 Aug.
- NAA MS4821 reprint catalogue: paper read at Detroit meeting, Aug 1897.
- Scherer 2022: 23 Aug–9 Sep 1897 field trip, based on Fletcher-to-Putnam correspondence.
- Library of Congress, Hethu'shka Society Song, created 26 Aug 1897; collectors Fletcher and La Flesche.

## 5. This narrows the meaning of `provenance compression`

Batch214 introduced `provenance compression` at a broad level:

`collaborative Omaha knowledge ecology`
→ `sole-author Fletcher print object`
→ `Lovejoy citation = Fletcher`.

Batch215 makes the chronology more exact.

The compression is not inferred from the famous 1911 joint book backward in time. It is already visible from **pre-1897** evidence:

- article-preparation assistance acknowledged in 1884;
- explicit credit negotiation in 1893;
- indispensable ritual-access and recording work described in 1896;
- mature collaboration already in place before the 1897 AAAS paper;
- further joint fieldwork resumes immediately after Detroit.

Thus the safe historical claim is stronger:

> **The print object that entered Lovejoy's bibliography under Fletcher's name rested in a research environment where La Flesche's contribution was already longstanding, recognized, negotiated and at times described by Fletcher herself as indispensable.**

But the claim remains at the level of **research infrastructure and provenance**.

It still does not prove:

`La Flesche → specific 1897 common-life proposition`.

## 6. What the 1897 article itself can currently be shown to do

The complete 1897 Smithsonian volume is now securely located:

- *Annual Report of the Board of Regents of the Smithsonian Institution for 1897*, pp.577–586;
- Internet Archive identifier `annualreportofbo1897smit`;
- Smithsonian Libraries digital-book route;
- Wikimedia Commons public-domain PDF, 960 scanned pages;
- note in Commons metadata that printed p.583 has some text obscured.

The current web interface cannot reliably page through the 66 MB scan, so a complete first-person/name census has **not yet** been made from the facsimile.

However, multiple near-contemporary/public-text witnesses securely recover several article-level propositions:

- belief in a `common life` permeating animate/inanimate things;
- continuity/indivisibility of that life;
- personal totem obtained through vision/fasting;
- efficacy mediated by relation to natural agencies;
- will/directive-power analysis;
- proposed derivation of social/gens totem from an ancestor's personal vision.

Later writers quote these propositions as Fletcher's and cite pp.580–586.

A 16 September 1897 *Nature* report of Fletcher's British Association presentation—just weeks after Detroit—also attributes the Omaha interpretation to her and transmits an Omaha Sacred Pole legend through the formula `we are told`, without naming a particular Indigenous narrator in the journal report.

This is useful for public attribution, but **not a substitute for the full 1897 text's acknowledgment apparatus**.

## 7. New distinction: provenance compression can occur without credit falsification

The case now permits a precise distinction.

### Credit falsification

Would require evidence that a proposition/text authored by La Flesche was knowingly published or cited as Fletcher's alone.

Current evidence: **NOT ESTABLISHED**.

### Provenance compression

A formally legitimate sole-author article can nevertheless circulate as if its named author exhausts the knowledge-production chain, while the article rests on a documented collaborative infrastructure.

Current evidence: **STRONG**.

This distinction matters for Lovejoy because his citation practice is bibliographically ordinary:

`Fletcher paper`
→ cited as `Fletcher`.

The historiographic task is not to accuse that citation of false attribution but to reconstruct what the citation label no longer shows.

## 8. Comparison with Jones: two different forms of scale/credit asymmetry

### William Jones 1905

- Indigenous professional scholar is the named print author;
- local evidentiary scope is explicitly bounded;
- Lovejoy retains the source but expands its scope.

Problem:

`receiver scale strengthening`.

### Fletcher 1897 / La Flesche infrastructure

- sole print author is Fletcher;
- collaborative infrastructure predates publication and is directly documented;
- Lovejoy follows formal bibliographic authorship.

Problem:

`provenance compression`.

These should remain analytically separate.

Together they show that `primitive philosophy` is assembled through heterogeneous mediation regimes rather than one uniform colonial source chain.

## 9. Strongest current article formulation

> **The North-American materials behind Lovejoy's 1906 abstraction reached print through different regimes of authority. William Jones entered the proof as a Native professional scholar whose locally bounded analysis Lovejoy subsequently scaled upward. Alice Fletcher's Omaha evidence entered under a legitimate sole-author citation, yet the collaborative infrastructure behind that print object was already mature before 1897. Fletcher had publicly acknowledged Francis La Flesche's assistance in preparing an Omaha article in 1884, negotiated his demand for credit in the 1893 music monograph, and in January 1896 told Putnam that access to a major ritual-song corpus would have been impossible without La Flesche's labor and kin-based trust. `The Import of the Totem` was delivered at Detroit before their August 1897 return to the Omaha reservation, so its empirical substrate necessarily reaches further back into this earlier working environment. The issue here is not demonstrated hidden coauthorship but the compression of a collaborative knowledge infrastructure into a single-author citation object.**

## 10. Evidence limits and next action

### Secure

- Detroit meeting precedes 23 Aug field trip.
- pre-1897 Fletcher–La Flesche collaboration is direct and extensive.
- Fletcher explicitly recognized La Flesche's article-preparation assistance in 1884.
- credit negotiation occurred in 1893.
- 1896 letter describes La Flesche's contribution as epistemically indispensable for a ritual/song corpus.
- 1897 paper remains formally Fletcher sole-authored.

### HOLD

- exact role of La Flesche in drafting/revising `The Import of the Totem`;
- proposition-level origin of `common life`, `will-power`, `directive energy`, or the evolutionary relation between personal and gens totems;
- whether Fletcher names or acknowledges La Flesche inside the full 1897 paper;
- exact Omaha narrator(s) behind quoted/paraphrased ritual speech in that paper.

### Highest next move

Use the now-located Smithsonian/IA facsimile to make a **page-level internal provenance census of pp.577–586**:

- all personal names;
- all `I / we / my / our` markers;
- all `the Indians say / old men say / they say` constructions;
- all quotations attributed to an Omaha speaker versus unattributed collective speech;
- all footnotes / source references;
- any mention of La Flesche, Dorsey, Putnam, family members or named ritual authorities.

If the large-volume route remains technically awkward, locate a standalone 1897 Salem Press reprint (8–12 pp.) or a page-separated Smithsonian derivative.
