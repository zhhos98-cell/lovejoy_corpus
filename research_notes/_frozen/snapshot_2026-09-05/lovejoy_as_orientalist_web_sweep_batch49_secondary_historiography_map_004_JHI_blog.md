## Correction after repository cross-check: the French line is structural, not peripheral

The first version of this note over-weighted an `American/Harvard` frame and thereby missed a major result already established elsewhere in the repository. There are **two French vectors** that need to be built into the historiographical architecture from the start.

### A. 004: Émile Senart is a French node inside the live Buddhist-philological controversy

The 004 notebook is not simply Harvard uptake of British/German Indology. Lovejoy is working inside a multilingual European controversy in which **Émile Senart's 1896 French intervention** is central. Direct recovery of Senart's `À propos de la théorie bouddhique des douze nidânas` shows that Senart already had an explicit programme of term analysis, filiation, stratification and reconstruction of historical accretions. The later Jacobi–Oldenberg exchange and Lovejoy's 1898 article are best read as interventions in this already coupled field.

The strongest current distinction is therefore not `European philology → American Lovejoy`, but a disagreement internal to a transnational philological field over **what genealogical evidence is allowed to prove**. Senart can use filiation and compositeness to reconstruct layered formation and distrust apparent systematic unity; Lovejoy can grant the same compositeness while demanding independent semantic and causal evidence before concluding that recurrent or borrowed elements are functionally identical or that the arrangement is unintelligible.

### B. 005: Paris / EPHE / Léon Marillier is a documented pedagogical context

Notebook 005 makes France even more concrete. The 1898–1899 report of the **École pratique des hautes études, Section des sciences religieuses** explicitly lists **Lovejoy** among the auditors who `took an active part in the work` of Léon Marillier's conferences on *Religions des peuples non civilisés*. The two reported conference sequences correspond strikingly to the two dominant blocks of notebook 005: survivance/amoral eschatology and sacrifice/ritual anthropophagy. Lovejoy's 1906 *Monist* article later says that the conclusions reached in 1900 were strongly influenced by his studies under Marillier.

The French route therefore is not a generic biographical stop. It is a directly documented **seminar and research-production environment**:

> **EPHE / Marillier comparative-religion programme (Lovejoy documented as active auditor, 1898–99) → notebook 005 comparative ritual/eschatology workshop → Lovejoy's conceptual formulation by 1900 → 1906 `Primitive Philosophy`.**

The repository has also reconstructed the immediate French conceptual ecology around Marillier: taboo, ritual sanction, moral obligation, mana, direct versus spirit-mediated efficacy, and the distinction between social/religious sanction and moral guilt. Mauss explicitly used Marillier's 1896 taboo paper in a controversy that maps closely onto 005's distinctions. The working French chain currently stands:

> **Codrington 1891 → Marillier uptake/review 1892 → Marillier taboo/sanction analysis 1896 → Marillier EPHE seminars attended by Lovejoy 1898–99 → Marillier `Religion` / mana 1900 and `coutume, tabou, obligation morale` 1901 → Lovejoy 005 / 1900 formulation → Lovejoy 1906.**

### Consequence for the JHI Blog architecture

The most accurate spatial/intellectual frame is now **Harvard + European Indology + Paris/EPHE**, not `Harvard as the main institutional origin with Europe as reading matter`.

For 004 itself, the French line enters through Senart and the multilingual controversy over nidāna genealogy, semantics and system. For the broader early-Lovejoy story, 005 proves that Lovejoy was also physically embedded in the French comparative-religion world. This gives the blog a stronger transnational story without requiring the overclaim that the 004 notebook itself was produced in Paris.

A better working formulation is:

> **The notebooks show Lovejoy's early historical-philosophical practice being made across connected scholarly environments: Harvard's Sanskrit/Pāli infrastructure; a live German–French–British Indological controversy; and, in the adjacent 005 notebook, direct participation in Marillier's Paris seminar at the EPHE.**

This also changes the next historiographical search. We need secondary histories of **French sciences religieuses / EPHE / Revue de l'histoire des religions / Marillier–Mauss–Réville–Lévi networks**, not only American Oriental studies.

## Core result

The secondary literature now falls into four useful but only partly overlapping historiographies:

1. **Lovejoy’s intellectual development and historiographical practice**: Daniel J. Wilson, Leo Catana, Daniel Wickberg, Darrin McMahon, Anthony Grafton, and Nico Mouton already make it impossible to claim simply that Lovejoy’s mature history of ideas had an unnoticed early prehistory. Wilson explicitly links his early religion/history-of-philosophy writings to later history-of-ideas practice; Catana shows that Lovejoy’s actual historical practice can differ from his mature methodological rhetoric; Wickberg/McMahon/Mouton reopen the mature method against older caricatures.
2. **The nineteenth-century Western/American making of “Buddhism” as an object of knowledge**: Philip C. Almond and Thomas A. Tweed provide indispensable large frames. Almond emphasizes the nineteenth-century textualization/reification of Buddhism in European scholarship; Tweed reconstructs the American encounter with Buddhism from 1844 to 1912 and the ways Victorian American categories shaped interpretation. Sebastian Lecourt's recent work on reading `world religions` in the 1890s adds a useful fin-de-siècle frame and cautions against reducing all such reading to a single Orientalist logic.
3. **French / European philological controversy around Buddhism**: Senart, Jacobi and Oldenberg show that the 004 problem was already transnational and methodologically sophisticated. This needs its own historiography: histories of French Indology, Buddhist studies, philology and the circulation of comparative categories between Paris, German universities, Britain and the United States.
4. **French institutional history of comparative religion**: EPHE's Section des sciences religieuses and Marillier's seminar are a directly documented context for 005 and for Lovejoy's early formation. This opens a second, partially separate historiographical literature on the institutionalization of `sciences religieuses`, the *Revue de l'histoire des religions*, Marillier, Mauss, Réville, Sylvain Lévi and adjacent networks.

The productive intersection is therefore narrower than `Buddhism influenced Lovejoy's history of ideas`:

> **The notebooks let us watch a young American philosopher learning to perform historical-philosophical discrimination inside transnational textual, philological and comparative-religion infrastructures that were themselves producing Buddhism and `primitive religion` as stable comparative objects.**

This is a documentary/workshop argument, not a claim that Buddhism or French comparative religion caused the later unit-idea method.

## 1. Lovejoy historiography: what is already occupied

### Daniel J. Wilson

Wilson remains the baseline developmental account. His biography and annotated bibliography already treat Lovejoy’s early work in religion and history of philosophy as part of the genealogy of his mature historical practice. `Religion and the Time-Process` (1902) is especially important in Wilson’s narrative because it joins religious history, temporality, value and the later historical study of large conceptual complexes.

**Consequence:** the article/blog should not announce the discovery of an `early Lovejoy` doing history of ideas before the history of ideas. That bridge is already in the literature.

### Leo Catana, 2010

Catana's `Lovejoy's Readings of Bruno: Or How Nineteenth-Century History of Philosophy Was “Transformed” into the History of Ideas` (_JHI_ 71.1, 91–112; DOI 10.1353/jhi.0.0068) argues that Lovejoy's grand methodological declarations in _The Great Chain of Being_ were partly disciplinary rhetoric and do not fully describe his actual historical practice. Catana places the 1904 and 1936 Bruno studies in continuity with nineteenth-century general histories of philosophy.

**Use for 004:** method should be reconstructed from practice rather than read backwards from the 1936 manifesto. 004 adds something Catana does not have: a pre-publication notebook in which copied authorities, supplementation, reclassification and eventual publication can be separated.

### Wickberg / McMahon / Grafton / Mouton

Daniel Wickberg's `In the Environment of Ideas` (_Modern Intellectual History_ 11.2, 2014, 439–464; DOI 10.1017/S1479244314000080) repositions Lovejoy as a form of cultural historian rather than a simple foil for contextualism. Anthony Grafton's 2006 JHI survey is a key disciplinary history of the rise, criticism and transformations of history of ideas. Darrin McMahon likewise argues that standard dismissals of Lovejoy often flatten what he actually did. Nico Mouton's 2023 `Apologia` (_History and Theory_ 62.2, 272–295; DOI 10.1111/hith.12298) directly challenges the canonical list of accusations that Lovejoy treated ideas as timeless, contextless essences incapable of change.

**Consequence:** avoid a straw-Lovejoy setup. The notebook's value is positive and archival: it shows operations, not merely that mature Lovejoy was `more contextual` than Skinnerian caricatures suggest.

## 2. Buddhism / Orientalism historiography: the large frame

### Philip C. Almond

_The British Discovery of Buddhism_ (1988) argues that nineteenth-century European scholarship made Buddhism into an increasingly textualized and delimited object. By the middle Victorian period, an `ideal Buddhism` reconstructed through texts and Western scholarly institutions could function as the standard against which lived Asian forms were judged. Almond's conclusion describes the reification of Buddhism as a textual object and links this process to the emergence of the historical Buddha.

**Use:** 004 is full of precisely the kind of mediated textual Buddhism Almond makes visible: translated canons, philological authorities, comparative classifications, disputed technical terms, attempts to reconstruct an original system from layered textual evidence.

**Limit:** Almond's principal object is Britain/Europe. Lovejoy's trajectory requires us to map the actual routes among Harvard, French and German scholarship rather than treat Britain as the automatic center.

### Thomas A. Tweed

_The American Encounter with Buddhism, 1844–1912_ (first published 1992; later UNC edition) is the strongest American-scale contextual history. Tweed starts the American encounter in 1844 and studies the interpretive debate through which Americans made Buddhism intelligible within Victorian cultural constraints. The book is especially useful for distinguishing scholarly, religious, dissenting and popular forms of reception without assuming that interest in Buddhism amounted to conversion or doctrinal sympathy.

**Use:** Lovejoy's 1898 technical article and notebook sit chronologically inside Tweed's American encounter, but at the specialist/academic end of the spectrum. A targeted search has not yet surfaced evidence that Tweed treats Lovejoy directly. Until the book/index is checked, use Tweed as contextual historiography, not as a Lovejoy secondary source.

### Sebastian Lecourt, 2023

`Reading World Religions in the 1890s` in _Nineteenth-Century Literature in Transition: The 1890s_ (pp. 97–111; DOI 10.1017/9781009063852.006) surveys the expanding 1890s market for works on Buddhism, Islam, Zoroastrianism and Hinduism. Lecourt notes that scholarship on figures such as Max Müller often emphasizes Orientalism and imperial power-knowledge, while liberal, missionary, middlebrow and occult readers pursued heterogeneous projects that cannot always be reduced to a single imperial logic.

**Use:** good safeguard against making the JHI Blog an automatic `Lovejoy as Orientalist` indictment. The archive is more interesting if we reconstruct the specific scholarly operations and institutional routes first.

## 3. The French line changes the historiographical gap

After the repository cross-check, the gap is not merely between `Lovejoy historiography` and `history of Buddhism`. It lies at the intersection of at least three literatures:

- Lovejoy scholarship already knows that early religion/history-of-philosophy mattered to his development.
- histories of Buddhist studies know that nineteenth-century `Buddhism` was built through textual, philological and comparative institutions.
- histories of French comparative religion/sciences religieuses can potentially explain the Paris environment in which Lovejoy was an active participant, but this secondary literature has not yet been systematically mapped against the surviving notebooks.

I have still **not located a modern secondary study that uses MS38 notebook 004 and notebook 005 together to reconstruct Lovejoy's 1890s working practice across Harvard, European Indological controversy, and Marillier's EPHE seminar**. This remains a provisional negative search result, not an exhaustive novelty claim.

The strongest novelty prospect is therefore not `Lovejoy had French influences`. That is too generic. It is the unusually documentable **movement of inferential practices across institutional settings**:

> `Harvard Sanskrit/Pāli infrastructure ↔ Senart/Jacobi/Oldenberg controversy ↔ Paris EPHE comparative-religion pedagogy → notebooks 004/005 → Lovejoy's 1898/1906 publications and 1902 category-history reuse.`

## 4. What the JHI Blog can safely claim

A stronger first-form claim after the correction:

> Existing Lovejoy scholarship already recognizes his early work on religion as part of the prehistory of his history of ideas, while histories of nineteenth-century Buddhism have shown how `Buddhism` became a textual and comparative scholarly object. Lovejoy's notebooks bring those histories together at the level of practice and reveal that the relevant workshop was transnational: Harvard philology, a French–German–British controversy over Buddhist genealogy and semantics, and direct Paris training in comparative religion under Léon Marillier.

The blog can therefore be about **the workshop and its routes**, not about proving a final genealogy from Buddhism to _The Great Chain of Being_. The later journal article can ask the larger question once the French secondary historiography, Harvard courses/networks, bibliography, teaching and later reuse are more completely reconstructed.

## 5. Claims to avoid

- `Lovejoy invented the history-of-ideas method while studying Buddhism.`
- `Historians have overlooked the connection between Lovejoy's early religion work and his later method.` Wilson already occupies this ground.
- `The notebook proves that Buddhism caused the unit-idea method.`
- `Lovejoy was a Buddhologist throughout his career.` Better: specialist Buddhology was brief, but Buddhist/Indian-philosophy work continued through teaching and comparative reuse.
- `Lovejoy's notebook simply reproduces Orientalist ideology.` The stronger historical question is what particular textual and inferential operations he inherited, modified and used.
- `The intellectual route is basically Harvard/American.` Repository evidence now directly disproves that simplification.
- `Marillier supplied every category in 005.` The secure claim is seminar context and strong methodological/content overlap; page-level transmission still varies by item.
- `004 was produced in Paris.` The French Senart line is secure at the level of controversy/source field; the physical production context of 004 should remain separately established.
