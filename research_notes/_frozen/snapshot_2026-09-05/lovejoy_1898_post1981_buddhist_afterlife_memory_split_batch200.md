# Batch 200 — Post-1981 afterlife of Lovejoy's 1898 Buddhist article: technical survival, biographical drift, and split reception ecology

Date: 2026-08-26
Status: synced / POST-WILSON RECEPTION CONTROL
Scope: follows Batch199's finding that Wilson's 1982 title index records no secondary commentary node for entry19, entry243, or entry176 through his June 1981 cutoff. This pass moves outside Wilson and asks what happened afterward, prioritizing the exact 1898 article `The Buddhistic Technical Terms upādāna and upādisesa` and then checking whether the 1901 Buddhism syllabus (243) and 1907 Deussen/Vedānta review (176) were similarly recovered.

## Core result

The post-1981 evidence breaks the apparent `Indic disappearance` into a **split reception ecology**.

Lovejoy's 1898 article did not vanish from Buddhist scholarship. It survived independently of the canonical Lovejoy / history-of-ideas literature and continued to be used as a substantive intervention in the philology and doctrinal history of **upādi / upādisesa / nirvāṇa**.

By contrast, modern biographical or institutional treatments of Lovejoy tend to remember the 1898 article as an anomalous early publication or a marker of breadth, while failing to reconstruct the 1901–1907 recurrence of Buddhist/Indic work already visible in Wilson's primary bibliography.

Two different memories therefore coexist:

1. **Buddhist-studies memory** — remembers the *argument* of 1898 and places Lovejoy in an ongoing technical dispute;
2. **Lovejoy-biographical memory** — remembers the *existence/title* of the 1898 paper but often disconnects it from both its Buddhist afterlife and Lovejoy's subsequent Indic engagements.

This is not disappearance. It is **disciplinary bifurcation of reception**.

---

## 1. Soonil Hwang (2006): Lovejoy's argument remains technically live

Soonil Hwang's *Metaphor and Literalism in Buddhism: The Doctrinal History of Nirvana* (Routledge, 2006) is a direct positive control.

Hwang discusses the interpretation of the two nirvāṇa elements and the meaning of `upādi`. In that argument he explicitly returns to Lovejoy's 1898 paper and summarizes Lovejoy's position: the oldest and numerous texts support the ethical/subjective interpretation associated with attachment or defilement.

Hwang does not cite Lovejoy merely as historical decoration. Lovejoy is inserted into a live chain of scholarship alongside figures such as Oldenberg, Masefield, Harvey, and later Buddhist textual scholarship.

The book's index independently lists:

`Lovejoy, A. O. 14, 28`.

Thus Lovejoy has at least two substantive indexed appearances in a 2006 specialist monograph on nirvāṇa.

### Reception code

`B-TECH-REC` — Buddhist-studies technical reception in which the content of Lovejoy's 1898 argument is reused or assessed.

For Hwang:

`1898 Lovejoy → Hwang 2006 [B-TECH-REC]`.

## 2. Amrita Nanda (2017 → 2024): the 1898 argument remains part of current Pāli debate

Amrita Nanda's 2017 University of Hong Kong PhD thesis, later revised as *The Theory of the Four Stages of Liberation in Pāli Literature* (Palgrave Macmillan / Springer Nature, 2024), provides an even later control.

In the discussion of non-returners and `upādi`, Nanda groups:

- Oldenberg (1882),
- Lovejoy (1898),
- Masefield (1979),
- Huzita (1988),
- Hwang (2006)

as scholars who challenged the interpretation of `upādi` as the aggregates and argued for attachment/clinging or related readings.

Nanda specifically attributes to Lovejoy the demonstration that `upādi`, `upādhi`, and `upādāna` are often used synonymously in the Pāli Nikāyas, and characterizes his case against the aggregate interpretation as convincing.

The important chronological fact is:

`1898 → 2006 → 2017 → 2024`.

Lovejoy's technical claim remains legible in specialist Buddhist scholarship more than a century after publication.

The 2024 book is not simply a reprint of an old citation culture; it is a current monograph in Pāli / Buddhist soteriological studies.

## 3. Additional specialist preservation

The web survey recovered several other forms of post-Wilson preservation.

### Buddhist Digital Library, National Taiwan University

The NTU Buddhist Digital Library has an author record for Lovejoy and records the 1898 article as his Buddhist-studies publication, with full-text availability.

This is **infrastructure-level preservation**: the paper remains discoverable inside a Buddhist bibliographic environment rather than only through generic philosophy databases.

### Bibliography of Indian Philosophies

A modern bibliography of Indian philosophies places Lovejoy's paper inside the `Abhidharma, especially Theravada Buddhism` secondary-literature sequence.

Again the work is classified by its Buddhist technical subject, not by Lovejoy's later identity as historian of ideas.

### Later theses and Buddhist textual studies

A recent Buddhist-studies dissertation bibliography and Korean-language scholarship also recover Lovejoy's 1898 paper among works on early Buddhist textual problems, nirvāṇa, or related canonical interpretation.

These are weaker controls than Hwang/Nanda for proposition-level reuse, but they establish continued disciplinary circulation.

## 4. The 1898 paper survived outside the canonical Lovejoy reception channel

Yet the post-1981 Buddhist-studies evidence shows that this absence cannot be read as the end of reception.

The article's later life took place in a **different disciplinary citation network**.

## 5. Klaus Karttunen's Indology prosopography exposes the memory gap

Klaus Karttunen's *Persons of Indian Studies* entry for Arthur Oncken Lovejoy, first posted in 2017 and updated in 2026, records the 1898 JAOS paper but states that Lovejoy `apparently never returned to Buddhist studies`.

That statement is understandable if one knows only the 1898 journal publication and Lovejoy's later canonical career. It is difficult to sustain once Wilson's primary bibliography is reopened.

At minimum Wilson preserves:

- **1898** — `The Buddhistic Technical Terms upādāna and upādisesa`;
- **1901** — `Syllabus: The Philosophy of Buddhism`, a four-lecture course explicitly covering Buddhism among Indian philosophies, essential formulas, ethics, and goal.

Therefore the strong literal claim that Lovejoy never returned to Buddhism after 1898 conflicts with a documented 1901 Buddhism teaching object.

Additional Indic recurrence follows:

- 1902 — Indian civilization appears in `Religion and the Time-Process`;
- 1904 — Batch192 established Indic/Vedānta material inside `The Dialectic of Bruno and Spinoza`;
- 1907 — entry176 reviews Deussen on Vedānta and recommends greater scholarly attention to Rāmānuja.

The cautious correction is:

> Lovejoy did not develop a clearly continuous sequence of published specialist Buddhist articles after 1898, but he demonstrably returned to Buddhism in teaching by 1901 and continued to engage Indian philosophical material through at least 1907.

This is more precise than either `he abandoned Buddhism after 1898` or `he maintained a continuous Buddhist research programme`.

## 6. Daniel Gordon (2023): the article survives as a biographical marker, but with semantic drift

Daniel Gordon's open-access 2023 book *What Is Academic Freedom? A Century of Debate, 1915–Present* includes a compact intellectual-biographical sketch of Lovejoy.

Gordon names the 1898 Buddhist article as one of Lovejoy's first publications and places it beside `Religion and the Time Process` (1902) and `The Origins of Ethical Inwardness in Jewish Thought` (1907) as evidence of Lovejoy's early range and his capacity to map unfamiliar systems.

But the title is rendered as:

`The Buddhistic Technical Terms Upādāna and Upadeśa`.

The second technical term should be `upādisesa`, not `upadeśa`.

This tiny error is historiographically revealing. The work remains visible as **biographical evidence of breadth**, while its specialist philological content has become opaque enough for the technical title itself to drift.

Gordon also moves directly from 1898 to 1902/1907 in the brief sketch; the 1901 Buddhism syllabus and 1907 Vedānta review are not used to construct an Indic sequence.

This yields a second reception code:

`B-BIO-REC` — biographical reception that uses the existence of the work as evidence about Lovejoy but does not necessarily engage its argument.

For Gordon:

`1898 Lovejoy → Gordon 2023 [B-BIO-REC]`.

## 7. Argument memory versus title memory

The post-1981 reception can now be divided by what is actually remembered.

### A. Argument memory

Hwang 2006 and Nanda 2017/2024 recover:

- the semantic problem of `upādi`;
- Lovejoy's relation among `upādi / upādhi / upādāna`;
- the implications for `saupādisesa / anupādisesa nibbānadhātu`;
- the dispute over attachment versus aggregates / residue.

This is **proposition-level survival**.

### B. Title / career memory

Karttunen and Gordon preserve:

- the fact that Lovejoy wrote a Buddhist paper in 1898;
- its position early in his career.

But the larger 1898–1907 Indic cluster is not reconstructed, and in Gordon the technical title itself shifts.

This is **metadata-level survival**.

The same primary work therefore has two afterlives that carry different information.

## 9. The apparent one-off episode is partly an archival/retrieval effect

A later reader encountering Lovejoy through modern searches is likely to find:

- the famous history-of-ideas corpus;
- the 1898 Buddhist article through Buddhist digital libraries or citations;
- little or nothing immediately connecting it to the 1901 syllabus or 1907 Vedānta review.

The resulting narrative almost writes itself:

`young Lovejoy briefly did Buddhism → abandoned it → became Western intellectual historian`.

But the Wilson + primary-text reconstruction produces a different chronology:

```text
1898 Buddhist philology
   ↓
1901 Buddhism teaching
   ↓
1902 comparative India / otherworldliness
   ↓
1904 Indic material operational inside Bruno–Spinoza
   ↓
1907 Vedānta review / Rāmānuja recommendation
```

This does not yet prove one continuous project. It does prove that the `single youthful detour` model is structurally inadequate.

---

## 10. Historiographical consequence: disciplinary bifurcation can preserve content while erasing trajectory

The crucial paradox is now source-grounded:

- Buddhist studies preserved **what Lovejoy argued** in 1898;
- general Lovejoy memory preserved **that Lovejoy once wrote** about Buddhism;
- neither reception ecology, by itself, reconstructs the recurrent early Indic trajectory visible when Wilson's bibliography and the primary corpus are joined.

So a work can remain cited for 125 years while its role in an author's intellectual development becomes harder to see.

This is not ordinary `neglect`. It is a mismatch between **citation survival** and **genealogical survival**.

The article survived propositionally in one discipline and biographically in another, while the bridge between those meanings largely disappeared.

---

## 11. Relation to the larger Lovejoy project

This result matters beyond biography because it changes the evidentiary baseline for the early history-of-ideas problem.

If the 1898 paper remained technically respectable enough to be reused in twenty-first-century Buddhist scholarship, then it should not be treated merely as a youthful curiosity preceding Lovejoy's `real` work.

At the same time, modern Buddhist reuse does not prove that the 1898 semantic method directly caused his mature history-of-ideas method.

The stronger research programme is comparative:

- 1898: semantic/philological distinction inside Pāli materials;
- 1904: role-sensitive distinction among source, affinity, transmission, and dialectical structure;
- 1909–1911 evolution essays: contextual control against false precursor construction;
- later history of ideas: unit separation, semantic mutation, historical recurrence.

The question is whether these are historically connected practices or recurrent methodological habits. That requires primary-text comparison, not retrospective naming.
