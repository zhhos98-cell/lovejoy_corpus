## 1. Chapter 3, p. 62 — Lovejoy inside the realist revolt, but outside the six New Realists

Section 3.3 is explicitly titled **`Logic and Analysis`**.

Verhaegh writes that a fuller account of the revolt against idealism would include Morris Cohen's logical realism, E. B. McGilvary's perspectivist realism, Woodbridge's Aristotelian realism, and the early writings of philosophers later known as the `critical realists`, explicitly naming **Arthur Lovejoy, J. B. Pratt, and A. K. Rogers**.

This matters because the surrounding chapter is reconstructing how realism, analysis, symbolic logic, and Russell's logic of relations became entangled in the United States.

Coding:

- `boundary critic` / `field participant`;
- not `central/member` of the six New Realists;
- direct evidence that Verhaegh locates Lovejoy within the broader anti-idealist realist field that forms the background to the American logical-analytic shift.

This is compatible with the repo firewall:

`Lovejoy participated in the wider realist/logical problem-space`

≠

`Lovejoy belonged to the later analytic canon`.

## 2. Chapter 3, pp. 64–65 — Lovejoy as critic of pseudo-formalization inside New Realism

Verhaegh's argument on pp. 64–65 is unusually useful for the active `historicalize the knife` branch.

He argues that the American New Realists needed Russell partly because none of the six had themselves made major contributions to logic and because some of their symbolic/logical display could be superficial. Perry is used as one case. Holt is the second.

Verhaegh then cites **Lovejoy's 1914 review of E. B. Holt, *The Concept of Consciousness*** as a contemporary criticism of Holt's attempt to present his theory as a consistent deductive system. Lovejoy suspects that Holt is effectively producing a caricature of abuses of dialectical method; Kallen supplies a parallel criticism that Holt's system is not actually consistent.

Immediately afterward Verhaegh contrasts the New Realists with **Royce**, whom he identifies as Harvard's real symbolic-logical authority and the teacher/supervisor of Sheffer and C. I. Lewis. The next page reconstructs Royce's post-1898 engagement with symbolic logic and his respect for Russell.

Coding:

- `interlocutor`;
- `boundary critic`;
- direct contemporary criticism of the **validity of another realist's formalization**, not generic anti-analysis;
- indirect field control for Lovejoy's own documented 1907–1911 concern with precision, implication, opposition, classification, and formal-logical jurisdiction.

Important inference:

> Lovejoy's 1914 position is not well described as simply `old American holism versus new analysis`. He can criticize a purportedly logical/deductive realist system **from within a field in which he himself already practiced formal-logical scrutiny**.

This sharpens the repo's New Realism control. The relevant contrast is not analysis/non-analysis but competing standards for what counts as valid analysis or formalization.

## 3. Harvard chronology control — the logic shift postdates Lovejoy's student years but Royce bridges the periods

Verhaegh's Harvard reconstruction provides a valuable temporal control.

The dramatic institutional consolidation of Harvard as a center of formal logic occurs after Lovejoy's 1895–99 student period: Russell visits in 1914; Sheffer is hired in 1917; C. I. Lewis in 1921; Whitehead in 1924. Verhaegh describes a younger generation increasingly oriented toward logic and semantics.

Yet the story does not begin in 1914. Royce is the bridge. Verhaegh emphasizes that symbolic logic already had a Harvard foothold through Royce and the indirect influence of Peirce; Royce taught most Harvard logic courses and developed substantial expertise after attending Peirce's 1898 lectures.

This strengthens, but also bounds, the repo's formation model:

- the **later Harvard School of Analysis cannot be projected backward wholesale into Lovejoy's student years**;
- Royce nevertheless supplies a real institutional/personnel bridge between Lovejoy's Harvard formation and the later logical turn;
- exact Lovejoy enrollment or direct uptake remains unproved.

Use this as a chronology firewall against both under- and over-genealogizing.

## 4. Chapter 4, p. 99 — Lovejoy in the relativity / operationism / meaning controversy

Verhaegh next places Lovejoy inside the American response to relativity.

He reconstructs Lovejoy's 1930 `The Dialectical Argument against Absolute Simultaneity (I)` as a defense of temporal realism and, more importantly for this branch, as an intervention into the theory of meaning associated with Einstein and Bridgman.

Verhaegh reads Lovejoy as accepting Bridgman's diagnosis that Einstein presupposed a radically experimental theory of meaning while reversing its argumentative valence: Lovejoy uses the diagnosis to attack special relativity. Lovejoy distinguishes an experimental criterion/evidence for something from the **meaning** of the term itself.

Coding:

- `interlocutor` / `boundary critic`;
- direct participation in a field connecting relativity, operationism, empirical criteria, meaning, and verification;
- especially relevant to the repo's recurring `jurisdiction partition` hypothesis.

The important structural recurrence is:

`criterion / operation / evidence`

≠ automatically

`meaning / identity of concept`.

This resembles the repo's actor-level 1911 distinction between formal implication, intelligibility/meaning, evidence/verification, and historical truth. It is not proof of an invariant doctrine across two decades, but it is now a strong mature control for **relation-specific jurisdiction**.

## 5. Chapter 5, pp. 151–154 — Lovejoy as part of the American prehistory of verification debates

Verhaegh returns to Lovejoy when explaining why logical positivism found an American audience around 1930.

The central historiographical claim of this chapter is that American philosophers were already converging on disputes about **meaning and verification** before the emigre consolidation. At p. 152 Lovejoy's relativity intervention is paired with E. B. McGilvary's response and Bridgman's operationism. At p. 154 Verhaegh notes that Lovejoy's rejection of special relativity remained tied to his temporal realism, illustrating the mixture of scientific-philosophical and metaphysical commitments within the American field.

Coding:

- `conceptual adjacency` plus direct controversy;
- Lovejoy is part of the **receiving field's prior problem-space**, not a logical positivist convert;
- useful evidence against an `invasive species` model in which logic/verification simply arrive from Vienna after 1930.

This is one of the strongest payoffs for the Lovejoy repo:

> **The later analytic/logical-positivist turn did not create from nothing the distinctions Lovejoy was using. It entered an American field in which Lovejoy had already been arguing about analysis, formal logic, criteria, meaning, and evidential jurisdiction for decades.**

## 6. Chapter 7, pp. 214–215 — direct institutional contact: Lovejoy introduces Carnap at Baltimore APA, 28 Dec. 1935

This is the strongest newly recovered **infrastructural contact**.

Verhaegh reconstructs Carnap's arrival in the United States in December 1935. On **28 December 1935**, Carnap and Quine drove to Baltimore for the APA Eastern Division Meeting. Carnap's paper `Testability and Meaning` was his first talk on U.S. soil. Verhaegh states directly that **Arthur Lovejoy introduced Carnap**, after which Carnap read a shortened version of his reply to C. I. Lewis.

The paper was historically consequential because Carnap was trying to correct the American interpretation of logical positivism and find common ground with pragmatism / American empiricism. Verhaegh then treats the Baltimore address as the beginning of Carnap's American lecture tour.

Coding:

- `infrastructural contact` — direct, dated, named, and located;
- `interlocutor` only in the weak institutional sense unless further evidence establishes substantive exchange;
- a high-value boundary event connecting Lovejoy to the emigre arrival narrative without implying doctrinal affinity.

Do not overread the introduction. But do not flatten it either.

Safe formulation:

> **Lovejoy was institutionally present at a key moment in Carnap's American arrival and introduced Carnap's first U.S. conference address, `Testability and Meaning`, at the Baltimore APA meeting on 28 December 1935.**
