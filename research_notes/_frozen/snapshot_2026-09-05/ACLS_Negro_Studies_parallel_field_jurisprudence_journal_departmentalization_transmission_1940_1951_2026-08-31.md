# ACLS Negro Studies as a parallel field-building jurisprudence: journals, departmentalization, and transmission proof, 1940–1951

Date: 2026-08-31
Status: **DIRECT 1940 PROCEEDINGS CONTROL + OFFICIAL ACLS INSTITUTIONAL CONTROL / PARALLEL HISTORY-OF-IDEAS COMPARATOR / NO DIRECT LOVEJOY TRANSMISSION CLAIM**

## Core result

The 1940 ACLS Conference on Negro Studies at Howard University is now a major comparative control for the institutional history of JHI.

It shows that, in the **same year JHI was founded**, another ACLS developmental project was explicitly wrestling with several of the same problems later associated with Lovejoyian/JHI historiography:

- disciplinary fragmentation;
- whether a new field should build its own specialized journal;
- whether specialized infrastructure promotes or aggravates intellectual isolation;
- how a specialized corpus should contribute to general disciplinary theory;
- whether cultural resemblance licenses historical survival / transmission claims;
- what evidence is required to establish a historical connection;
- how area/cultural scholarship can broaden an American academy described as excessively Western.

The strongest current interpretation is therefore not that the Negro Studies conference borrowed a Lovejoyian method, or vice versa. It is:

> **`History of Ideas` and `Negro Studies` were parallel experiments inside a broader ACLS field-development regime. They confronted homologous jurisdictional problems because ACLS was repeatedly trying to turn dispersed, underrecognized or cross-disciplinary materials into sustainable scholarly fields without simply reproducing university departmental fragmentation.**

This substantially enlarges the eventual Lovejoy/JHI history.

# I. Direct institutional setting

Primary:

Melville J. Herskovits, ed., *Interdisciplinary Aspects of Negro Studies: Proceedings of a Conference on Negro Studies, held at Howard University, Washington, D.C., March 29–30, 1940*, ACLS Bulletin 32 (1941).

Bibliographic/open-access control:

- Folger/ACLS Humanities E-Book record: https://catalog.folger.edu/record/728015
- searchable open carrier: https://dokumen.pub/interdisciplinary-aspects-of-negro-studies-proceedings-of-a-conference-on-negro-studies-held-at-howard-university-washington-dc-mar-29-30-1940-000000620.html
- official ACLS history: https://www.acls.org/our-history/

Participants included Melville J. Herskovits, Herbert Aptheker, Ralph Bunche, J. Franklin Frazier, Alain Locke, Eric Williams and others.

Official ACLS retrospective states that the conference addressed a problem of dispersed research: many disciplines studied the Negro, but the different bodies of work were not sufficiently recognized across disciplinary boundaries.

This is structurally close to the institutional problem Lovejoy names in JHI's 1940 founding programme, but the two should be treated as parallel evidence unless direct coordination is recovered.

# II. The same ACLS development machinery explicitly contains History of Ideas and area-field committees

One of the most important passages in the 1940 proceedings is ACLS executive Waldo Leland's institutional framing.

He describes the Council's development/planning work as an attempt to broaden American scholarship beyond its inherited narrow Western horizon and situates Negro Studies among a wider family of committee-built fields.

The committee ecology he names includes work on:

- Chinese studies;
- Japanese studies;
- Indic and Iranian studies;
- Slavic studies;
- Near Eastern studies;
- Arabic and Islamic studies;
- Latin American studies;
- American culture;
- Renaissance studies;
- musicology;
- **History of Ideas**;
- History of Religions.

This is direct actor-level evidence that `History of Ideas` was institutionally classified by ACLS alongside other **developmental fields** rather than imagined only as a philosopher's autonomous method.

The field-history problem therefore becomes:

`WHAT SPECIAL CORPUS / PROBLEM IS UNDERORGANIZED?`
→ `WHAT PLANNING / SURVEY / COMMITTEE / PUBLICATION INFRASTRUCTURE IS NEEDED?`
→ `HOW CAN THE NEW FIELD CONTRIBUTE BACK TO GENERAL SCHOLARSHIP?`

This is a larger institutional grammar within which the History of Ideas Committee and JHI should be located.

# III. Alain Locke and Mortimer Graves: should an underdeveloped field have its own journal?

The conference contains an extraordinary discussion of scholarly publication as field architecture.

Alain Locke raises the danger that developing Negro Studies only as a bounded research field may frustrate its broader intellectual purpose. He wants the material to become a tool for understanding more general social and cultural problems, not merely a body of information `for and about the Negro`.

Mortimer Graves then generalizes from ACLS's experience developing other underworked fields.

He notes that one of the first things specialists in Indic, Chinese and similar areas often request is a journal. But he warns that specialized journals can **aggravate the separation** already existing among specialists by locking them into isolated organizations. The result can reproduce or intensify departmentalization rather than overcome it.

The desired alternative is not suppression of specialist research. It is to develop tools, evidence and organization in ways that allow economists, sociologists, historians and other disciplinary scholars to incorporate the new corpus into their larger questions.

### Institutional significance

This creates a striking 1940 parallel to JHI's own founding rhetoric.

Lovejoy's JHI programme attacks enclosed university channels and seeks a liaison/cross-fertilization infrastructure.

Graves/Locke, in another ACLS field-development setting, worry that the seemingly obvious solution — a dedicated specialist journal — can itself create a new enclosure.

Therefore:

`SPECIAL JOURNAL`
!= automatically
`INTERDISCIPLINARY INTEGRATION`.

A journal is one possible institutional technology whose effects depend on how it connects specialized evidence to general scholarly problems.

This is especially useful for the history of JHI because it prevents treating the periodical form as self-evidently interdisciplinary merely because the editorial programme says so.

# IV. Frazier / Bunche / Herskovits: resemblance does not establish historical survival

The conference also contains an unusually explicit dispute over historical relations.

The issue is whether contemporary African-American cultural practices can be interpreted as survivals from West African institutions or beliefs.

J. Franklin Frazier resists promoting resemblance into historical continuity. A modern practice that resembles a West African institution does not by itself establish African survival. He asks for an unmistakable historical connection.

Ralph Bunche pushes the evidentiary issue further: to assert the causal relation, must the researcher trace a `thread of contact and correspondence` through the historical chain? In the absence of such a thread, he questions whether the causal inference back to Africa should be accepted.

Herskovits agrees with the general evidentiary principle but insists on the symmetrical warning: the scholar must not rule African survival out in advance. Whether a cultural form is an African survival depends on the particular locality, group and historical situation.

The dispute can be formalized safely as:

`MORPHOLOGICAL / FUNCTIONAL RESEMBLANCE`
↛ `HISTORICAL SURVIVAL`
↛ `CAUSAL DESCENT`.

A stronger historical proposition requires a stronger relation-specific proof route.

At the same time:

`FAILURE TO PROVE SURVIVAL IN ONE CASE`
↛ `A PRIORI IMPOSSIBILITY OF AFRICAN SURVIVAL`.

This is remarkably close in proof-burden structure to several independently recovered Lovejoy/JHI problems:

- Lovejoy notebook: classificatory similarity != temporal relation;
- Lovejoy 1933/48: content/source similarity != one fixed causal genealogy;
- Mandelbaum 1948: recurrence != continuity; independent invention vs diffusion must be separately tested;
- Wiener 1961: conditions for establishing influence;
- Skinner 1969 later notices Wiener's prior treatment of the influence problem.

Do **not** claim direct methodological transmission among these cases. The safer and more historically interesting result is that relation-specific warrant was a live problem across neighboring mid-century human-science field-building projects.

# V. Idea exchange and cultural contact are themselves explicit conference objects

The proceedings also discuss cultural exchange through trade and the extent of European influence in African societies.

This matters because the conference is not simply a political/institutional meeting about creating Black Studies. It is already dealing substantively with:

- diffusion;
- contact;
- persistence;
- modification;
- cultural comparison;
- historical mediation.

Thus the methodological discussion emerges from the object problems themselves, just as the current Lovejoy reconstruction has found in Buddhist philology, comparative religion and primitivism.

A common field-development pattern may be:

`NEW / MARGINALIZED CORPUS`
→ exposes ambiguity in inherited categories and relations
→ forces explicit proof rules
→ generates institutional proposals for coordination and publication.

This is an analytical hypothesis, not an actor-stated universal mechanism.

# VI. Permanent Committee on Negro Studies, 1941–1951

The 1940 conference led ACLS to appoint a permanent Committee on Negro Studies in 1941.

Official ACLS and National Archives accounts describe its mandate as recovery and preservation of the history of the Negro in the Western Hemisphere, including:

- literature;
- art;
- speech;
- African origins and background;
- identification/preservation of source materials;
- guidance of scholarly research.

Concrete infrastructure included work contributing to:

- microfilming Black newspapers;
- source preservation;
- a 1947 guide to materials concerning Negro history in the National Archives.

The committee nevertheless did not stabilize into a durable institution comparable to JHI or the later Association for Asian Studies. ACLS's centennial history says more ambitious projects failed to advance, proposals for joint SSRC/NRC sponsorship were not taken up, a planned conference on `The Negro as Scholar and as Subject of Scholarship` stalled, and the committee voted to dissolve in 1951.

This supplies a powerful institutional counterfactual:

> **ACLS developmental committees did not all converge on the same organizational outcome.**

Some produced durable journals, societies or area-studies networks; Negro Studies built important source infrastructure but dissolved after a decade.

The eventual JHI article should exploit this variation rather than treating ACLS committee formation as a standardized path to institutional success.

# VII. Segregation and authority: important secondary line, not yet direct-lock complete

Robert L. Harris Jr., `Segregation and Scholarship: The American Council of Learned Societies' Committee on Negro Studies, 1941–1950`, *Journal of Black Studies* 12.3 (1982), 315–331, directly studies this committee from ACLS and Herskovits papers.

Current direct web control securely establishes article metadata and source base; later scholarship citing Harris makes stronger claims that Herskovits exercised tight control over the committee and marginalized or excluded leading African-American scholar-activists, even while making use of their expertise.

This line is potentially crucial for explaining the institutional divergence between:

- a Black/Negro Studies project centered initially at Howard but increasingly routed through Herskovits/African Studies institutions;
- JHI/History of Ideas;
- later African Studies.

However, until Harris's full argument is directly read, keep the stronger governance/power claim coded:

`SEGREGATION / SCHOLARLY-AUTHORITY EXPLANATION = SECONDARY-SUPPORTED / DIRECT ARTICLE COLLATION PENDING`.

Official ACLS evidence alone is enough to establish uneven institutional success and stalled programs; it is **not** enough to assign the cause to Herskovits or racial exclusion.

# VIII. Mortimer Graves as a possible ACLS field-planning switchboard

Graves now appears in three independently strong field-development sequences.

## 1. Chinese Studies, 1928–58

Pauline Yu's institutional history reconstructs Graves, newly appointed ACLS Assistant Secretary, as principal architect of the China initiative. His toolkit included:

- planning conferences;
- surveys and reports;
- seed money for faculty positions;
- fellowships;
- curricular promotion;
- bibliographical and journal resources;
- formation of a scholarly society;
- intensive language-training infrastructure.

## 2. Negro Studies, 1940

At Howard, Graves explicitly imports comparative organizational lessons from Indic, Chinese and other `underworked fields` into the debate over Negro Studies, especially on journals, specialist isolation and integration into general disciplines.

## 3. Chinese intellectual history, 1951

David Nivison's retrospective reports that John K. Fairbank began organizing the Committee on Chinese Thought after a suggestion from Graves to experiment with new approaches to the history of ideas in China.

This makes a plausible institutional hypothesis:

> **Graves was not merely a China specialist-administrator; he was an ACLS carrier of comparative field-building know-how across otherwise different developmental projects.**

But one crucial edge remains OPEN:

`GRAVES ↔ ACLS HISTORY OF IDEAS COMMITTEE / LOVEJOY / JHI DIRECT ADMINISTRATION`.

Current broad search has not yet recovered a direct primary or high-grade institutional source proving Graves served as secretary/staff liaison to the History of Ideas Committee or participated in the journal-launch decision.

Do not complete that edge by inference merely because he was ACLS staff.

Highest-value archival search inside the 1929–39 History of Ideas Committee folders:

- Graves correspondence;
- staff memoranda;
- routing slips/minutes;
- reports comparing History of Ideas to China/Indic/Negro field development;
- publication/journal planning.

If found, this could turn the current `parallel institutional grammar` into a documented shared administrative craft.

# IX. Africa/Black Studies as parallel appropriation rather than early JHI convergence

The current comparative regional matrix shows a significant asymmetry.

For China, Latin America and the Wiener–Nakamura transpacific network, we have multiple direct interfaces with JHI/Society governance.

For Africa/African-American studies:

- ACLS field-building infrastructure is early and strong;
- direct 1940 methodological discussion is sophisticated;
- the Committee on Negro Studies generates source infrastructure;
- but the 1940–75 JHI corpus currently yields comparatively few clear Sub-Saharan/African-American internally addressed intellectual-history cases;
- the 1973 original *Dictionary of the History of Ideas* has not yet yielded a top-level Africa/African/Negro headword in the title-level census;
- by contrast, African Studies itself later absorbs and debates history-of-ideas methodology.

Important later examples:

- Leo Spitzer, `Interpreting African Intellectual History: A Critical Review of the Past Decade, 1960–1970`, *African Studies Review* (1972);
- Felix K. Ekechi, `The Future of the History of Ideas in Africa`, *African Studies Review* (1987), whose methodological bibliography includes Lovejoy 1940, Pearce 1948, Wiener 1961 and Spitzer 1972.

Thus the current best hypothesis is:

`ACLS NEGRO/AFRICAN FIELD DEVELOPMENT`
→ `AFRICAN STUDIES / BLACK STUDIES INSTITUTIONAL TRAJECTORIES`
→ `LATER INTERNAL APPROPRIATION OF HISTORY-OF-IDEAS METHODS`

rather than

`EARLY DIRECT CONVERGENCE INTO JHI GOVERNANCE / CANON`.

This remains a comparative bounded hypothesis until the JHI corpus and correspondence are exhaustively checked.

# X. Consequence for the Lovejoy/JHI-scale paper

The biggest conceptual gain is to move one scale higher.

Instead of asking only:

`How did Lovejoy's method become JHI?`

ask:

> **What kinds of scholarly and institutional problems did ACLS developmental fields share, and why did different projects crystallize into different infrastructures?**

The 1940 comparison is especially strong:

### JHI / History of Ideas

`disciplinary enclosures obscure migratory intellectual phenomena`
→ build a liaison/cross-fertilization periodical.

### Negro Studies

`disciplinary research is dispersed and mutually unrecognized`
→ consider field coordination;
but Locke/Graves warn that a separate specialist journal may itself reproduce isolation.

### At the evidence level

both ecologies confront:

`similarity`
!=
`historical relation / transmission / survival`.

The shared historical object may therefore be less `the Lovejoy method` than an interwar/war-era **jurisprudence of field construction**:

- what counts as a field;
- what counts as a relation;
- what evidence warrants movement across domains;
- what institutional form allows specialists to communicate without creating another silo.

That is a much larger HPS/history-of-human-sciences problem, and it leaves room for genuine difference, power and failed institutional outcomes.
