## Core result

The proposed Harvard institutional claim is secure.

Harvard's current South Asian Studies history states explicitly that Sanskrit was first taught at Harvard in **1872**, when **James Bradstreet Greenough, a Latin grammarian**, offered **Sanskrit and comparative philology as Latin electives**. Charles Rockwell Lanman arrived in **1880** and presided over the newly distinct department of **Indo-Iranian Languages**.

A near-contemporary Harvard memorial for Greenough, published in the _Harvard Crimson_ in December 1901, independently gives the same historical sequence but makes the intellectual relation still clearer: Greenough valued comparative grammar `both in itself and as an aid to a student of the Classics`, took up Sanskrit, and in 1872 established Harvard's first courses in Sanskrit and Comparative Philology, teaching them voluntarily **alongside his regular Latin instruction** until a Sanskrit professor was appointed in 1880.

Thus the Harvard side should not be narrated as a pre-existing Classics department facing an externally imported `Oriental Studies` unit. Sanskrit/comparative philology first entered Harvard through classical-philological teaching and only later acquired a separate Indo-Iranian departmental home.

## 1. Greenough is the decisive institutional bridge

The current Harvard South Asian Studies historical page states:

> Sanskrit was first taught at Harvard in 1872, when James Bradstreet Greenough, a Latin grammarian, began offering courses in Sanskrit and comparative philology as Latin electives.

It then says Lanman, beginning at Harvard in 1880, was the first to preside over the department of Indo-Iranian Languages.

This is unusually strong because Harvard itself describes the original administrative location as **Latin electives**.

The 1901 _Harvard Crimson_ memorial independently records that Greenough believed Comparative Grammar valuable `both in itself and as an aid to a student of the Classics`; he established the first Sanskrit / Comparative Philology courses in 1872 and continued them voluntarily with his normal Latin teaching until 1880.

The institutional sequence is therefore:

`Latin / classical philology`  
→ `comparative grammar as classical training`  
→ `Sanskrit + comparative philology offered within Latin`  
→ `Lanman / separate Indo-Iranian departmental structure (1880)`.

This is not merely retrospective departmental branding; the Harvard memorial closest to Greenough's death describes the same sequence.

---

## 2. 1872 is also the year Greenough put comparative grammar into Latin pedagogy

In 1872 Greenough and Joseph Henry Allen published _A Latin Grammar for Schools and Colleges: Founded on Comparative Grammar_. The bibliographic title itself makes the methodological relation explicit.

This coincidence matters because the first Harvard Sanskrit/comparative-philology instruction was not an unrelated extracurricular enthusiasm. Greenough was simultaneously incorporating comparative grammar into the study of Latin.

The safest historical inference is therefore:

> At Harvard, Sanskrit entered as both an object of study and an evidentiary resource within a comparative reconstruction of the classical languages.

Do not overstate this as a single coherent institutional programme without the 1872 course pages; the current evidence establishes the alignment, not every classroom linkage.

## 3. Lanman marks differentiation, not an absolute beginning

There is a minor source-description inconsistency worth preserving.

- Harvard South Asian Studies says Greenough first taught Sanskrit in 1872 and Lanman in 1880 became the first head of Indo-Iranian Languages.
- The HOLLIS biographical note for Lanman's papers says Lanman `introduced the study of Sanskrit at Harvard`.

Taken literally those statements conflict. The more precise chronology is supported by both the departmental history and Greenough's 1901 Harvard memorial: Greenough taught Sanskrit/comparative philology before Lanman; Lanman's historical importance lies in **professionalizing / institutionalizing** Sanskrit through a professorial and departmental structure, not literally being the first person to teach it.

For publication, avoid reproducing the imprecise HOLLIS phrase without qualification.

## 4. What this changes in the Lovejoy framing

The repo already establishes that Lovejoy studied Sanskrit and Buddhist texts with Lanman and that Lanman's April 1896 Buddhism lecture explicitly treated Buddhism's relation to Sāṃkhya and Vedānta. Batch160 identified archival routes capable of testing the classroom carrier more directly.

Batch163 adds a different point: **the institutional lineage of that training ran back through classical philology itself**.

Accordingly, Müller's 1901 contrast between `classical and Oriental scholarship` cannot be mapped straightforwardly onto Harvard in the 1890s. Harvard's own institutional history shows overlap before separation:

- classical Latin scholarship supplied the first Sanskrit/comparative-philology teaching;
- comparative grammar linked Latin, Greek, Sanskrit and historical reconstruction;
- Lanman's Indo-Iranian department then differentiated a specialist space without erasing that genealogy.

This supports the article's emerging formulation:

> the relevant nineteenth-century story is not a collision between two sealed scholarly traditions but a differentiation inside philology, during which evidentiary techniques continued to move across the new institutional boundaries.

This remains contextual; the direct 004 source chain is still Garbe 1894 → notebook 004, not Greenough → Lovejoy.
