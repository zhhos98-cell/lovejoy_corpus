## Core advance

A searchable scan/OCR reproduction of the printed opening has now exposed a passage that sharply calibrates the Lovejoy originality question.

Poussin begins from an unusually strong historical judgment. He says, in substance, that the nidāna formula is made out of **arbitrarily associated formulas** that reveal **several successive stratifications**, explicitly invoking Senart. He adds that there is scarcely a single term — perhaps except `jāti` and `jarāmaraṇa` — whose interpretation is not hazardous. He names **Senart, Garbe, Jacobi and Oldenberg** as scholars whose research had greatly illuminated these difficulties.

He then says that Buddhist exegetes of different schools had all tried to **put some order into the formula’s initial incoherence** and to **introduce some logic into the arrangement of the consecrated terms**.

This is a stronger later parallel than batch 45’s general 1902/1913 controls because it concerns the **same twelve-link formula, the same Senart/Garbe/Jacobi/Oldenberg controversy, and the same tension between stratification and intelligible arrangement**.

## 2. Poussin 1905 openly adopts the strongest Senart-style stratification premise

The opening says:

- the nidāna formula is composed of formulas **“arbitrairement associées”**;
- these allow one to suspect **“plusieurs stratifications successives”**;
- this is explicitly framed as something **Senart had expressed**;
- almost every technical term is interpretively dangerous;
- Senart, Garbe, Jacobi and Oldenberg had already clarified many of the difficulties.

This matters because it rules out an easy contrast in which Senart represents historical fragmentation while later specialists simply reverse him and restore unity. Poussin, seven years after Lovejoy’s paper, is willing to accept an even rhetorically stronger version of historical incoherence while still pursuing dogmatic/systematic interpretation.

Thus:

> **historical compositeness and systematic reconstruction were not competing scholarly camps. A single scholar could affirm both at once.**

That is exactly the conceptual distinction we have been recovering from 004, but Poussin shows it belongs to the broader field repertoire.

## 3. Poussin’s phrase “initial incoherence” is a critical novelty control

Poussin says Buddhist exegetes themselves tried:

- to put order into the **initial incoherence** of the formula;
- to introduce logic into the arrangement of inherited/consecrated terms.

This creates a very important control on Lovejoy 004 p.49.

Lovejoy there grants compositeness, borrowed terminology, mixed objective/subjective categories and a nonhomogeneous temporal sequence, while refusing to conclude that the formula is unintelligible or accidentally ordered. Earlier versions of our argument could make that sound like a distinctive late-1890s rescue of system against Senart.

Poussin shows the safer formulation:

- the **problem** of deriving intelligible arrangement from historically heterogeneous material is field-wide;
- what is distinctive about Lovejoy is not the existence of that problem or the possibility of reconstruction;
- 004’s evidentiary value is that it shows **how Lovejoy himself performs the transition** from source claims to a particular semantic/causal reconstruction.

In other words, notebook value = **microgenesis**, not methodological monopoly.

## 4. The second note directly revisits Lovejoy’s temporal-allocation problem

Poussin states that his second problem is dogmatic: the **distribution of the nidānas between past, present and future**. He thinks there is on this point an **orthodox doctrine**, shared across traditions/commentarial materials.

This directly overlaps Lovejoy 1898’s insistence that dependent origination is not a simple homogeneous chronological sequence while still allowing a broad three-life allocation.

The later Poussin/Abhidharma synthesis, made explicit in 1913, distributes:

- previous life: `avidyā`, `saṃskāras`;
- present life: middle links;
- future life: `jāti`, `jarāmaraṇa`.

But Poussin treats this temporal allocation as only one layer of explanation. He simultaneously distinguishes causal categories — passion/`kleśa`, action/`karman`, and fruit/`vipāka` — and later other systematic models.

This validates a source-control rule for our description of Lovejoy 1898:

> **three-life allocation is not equivalent to claiming that the twelve terms form a simple chronological series. Temporal position, causal role and doctrinal category are separate analytical dimensions.**

That distinction is especially important because Lovejoy’s article can otherwise be paraphrased too crudely as a chronological reading.

## 6. Comparison with Senart 1896 and Lovejoy 1898

The three positions now look less like a simple sequence and more like different allocations of historical and systematic inference.

### Senart 1896

- formula has stratified historical anatomy;
- term filiation reveals layers and borrowing;
- inherited phraseology can tyrannize thought and create scholastic structures;
- strong caution against projecting deep intentional system into nomenclature.

### Lovejoy 004 / 1898

- grants much compositeness and borrowing;
- tests whether alleged repeated terms are actually semantically identical before using recurrence as historical evidence;
- reconstructs different causal jobs for karma and `upādāna` from inherited source material;
- when lexical genealogies remain underdetermined, explicitly brackets etymology and lets usage decide meaning;
- allows historically borrowed material to have a distinctive arrangement/application.

### Poussin 1905

- openly adopts “arbitrarily associated” / successive-stratification language;
- calls the starting formula initially incoherent;
- nevertheless treats Buddhist exegetical systematization as an intelligible object of scholarship;
- pursues an orthodox distribution over past/present/future and later multiple causal/systematic models.

The key historiographical conclusion is therefore:

> **Lovejoy’s significance is not that he alone refused to choose between history and system. His archive is unusually valuable because it preserves the workshop in which he learned to assign separate evidentiary tasks to historical provenance, semantic identity, causal role and present configuration.**

## 7. Poussin 1905 → 1913 strengthens the field-control chronology

The 1905 paper and 1913 _Théorie des douze causes_ now form a useful internal Poussin trajectory:

### 1905

Poussin can speak strongly of arbitrary association, successive strata and initial incoherence, then study how traditions impose order and how links distribute across temporal lives.

### 1913

He makes the methodological separation explicit:

- logical/intelligible analysis is indispensable to understanding ideas;
- such analysis does **not necessarily have historical value**;
- reconstruction of logical development may not reconstruct real historical development beyond plausibility.

The later statement therefore articulates a distinction already visible in his 1905 practice.

This confirms that our phrase **division of evidentiary burdens** is a defensible analytic description of the field problem, but not a Lovejoy-only invention.

### C. Lovejoy generalization

- resemblance ≠ transmission;
- logical sequence ≠ chronological sequence;
- recurring presuppositions/logical motives ≠ intact historical systems.

Poussin’s later work then functions as an anti-teleological control: similar methodological problems existed independently in specialist Buddhist studies.
