# Lovejoy — aggregation-mode threshold topology, 1906–1941

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: **RESTART-SAFE CHECKPOINT / THRESHOLD-ASYMMETRY STRESS TEST / GATE-A MODE REFINEMENT**

## Core correction

The previous backward test introduced `threshold asymmetry`: local cases may have clear exclusion/class-splitting rules while a promoted macro-category has a much thinner rule for what counter-evidence would split, demote, or falsify the aggregation.

Two out-of-sample controls now show that this diagnostic is useful only after specifying the **aggregation mode**. Lovejoy's groupings do not all demand the same kind of identity threshold.

The revised rule is:

> **Before asking how sharp an identity threshold is, identify what kind of aggregation is being performed. A soft threshold can be methodologically appropriate for an overlap family or graded pattern, while it is a substantive warrant burden when a strong equivalence, actor-level common concept, or population-representative claim is being made.**

This adds an antecedent Gate-A question:

`A0b — aggregation mode`.

---

# I. Aggregation modes currently visible in Lovejoy

The case law now supports at least six modes.

## M1 — strict classificatory / equivalence-style identity

Claim form:

`token a, token b ∈ same analytical unit X`.

Typical warrants:

- same proposition;
- same system-function;
- same relation/operator;
- same explicitly delimited doctrinal item.

Expected threshold:

**comparatively sharp**. The historian should be able to specify what change makes a token cease to count as X.

Examples:

- 1898 classificatory identity/nonidentity;
- 1936 continuity / gradation / plenitude when treated as identifiable principles.

## M2 — thin common-core aggregation

Claim form:

`rich meanings a,b,c differ, but all contain invariant core k`.

Expected threshold:

**sharp at the deliberately thin level**: a token belongs only if k is present, even though almost everything above k may vary.

Example:

- 1941 `infinite`: at least five distinct senses/applications, unified only by the negative core `not-limited / not-completed`.

## M3 — overlap / family grouping

Claim form:

`A overlaps B in x; B overlaps C in y; no single x/y need occur in every member`.

Expected threshold:

**non-essentialist and locally relational**. There is no requirement for one global invariant possessed by every member. Asking for a single essence-level A4 is a category mistake.

Example:

- 1924 `Romanticisms`.

## M4 — graded pattern / configuration homology

Claim form:

`domain A and domain B instantiate substantially the same organized profile or scheme, possibly to different degrees and with incomplete component match`.

Expected threshold:

**soft / profile-based**. Membership is not necessarily all-or-none; one case can fit the pattern more closely than another.

Example:

- 1932 `The Parallel of Deism and Classicism`.

## M5 — population-representative comparative type

Claim form:

`local/regional cases a,b,c warrant a type X characteristic/fundamental/dominant for a larger population or historical category`.

Expected threshold:

requires two distinct controls:

1. **type identity** — why are the cases instances of X?;
2. **representativeness** — what distribution/sampling/counter-instance rule licenses promotion to `most`, `many or most races`, `primitive philosophy`, etc.?

Example:

- 1906 `Primitive Energetics` / `fundamental concept of primitive philosophy`.

## M6 — persistence / recurrence grouping

Claim form:

`historical tokens are treated as the same component either through lineage/transformation or independent recurrence`.

Expected threshold:

route-sensitive:

- persistence may tolerate large transformations if lineage + thin invariant survive;
- recurrence without lineage places more weight on conceptual/functional/mechanistic equivalence.

Examples:

- Great-Chain principles across historical transformations;
- 1933 Monboddo/Rousseau as content similarity with genealogy unresolved;
- 1949 persistence vs recurrence distinction.

# II. 1941 `infinite` — thin invariant, relatively sharp threshold

Primary: Arthur O. Lovejoy, `The Meaning of Romanticism for the Historian of Ideas`, *Journal of the History of Ideas* 2.3 (1941), pp.262–263.

Lovejoy first gives the anti-lexical rule: recurrent crucial terms must be scrutinized because different writers may use the same word in different senses. `Infinite` is his worked example. He distinguishes at least five senses/applications, explicitly noting that they are not all mutually exclusive.

He nevertheless says they all have a historically important common element: the negative element. Whatever positive meaning accompanies the term, the recurrent core is `not-limited / not-completed`.

This is important because the aggregation threshold is **thin but not vague**.

At the common-core scale:

`positive content varies radically`
+
`negative no-limit/no-completion core present`
→ candidate M2 membership.

Conversely, a use with no relevant notion of absence of limit/completion would fail this particular common-core aggregation, even if it shared the same surface word.

Therefore:

> **invariant thinness does not necessarily imply threshold softness.**

The historian can choose a very thin invariant and still have a reasonably explicit membership condition.

This also sharpens the Skinner-facing issue. The pressure point is not simply that Lovejoy abstracts. It is whether the chosen thin core is historically explanatory enough to justify stripping away the divergent positive meanings and uses.

# III. 1941 `Romanticism` — broad family/period without one identity threshold

Primary: same paper, pp.261–262.

Lovejoy explicitly rejects the proposition that the new ideas of the 1780s–1790s constituted one `Romantic` idea, one set of implicates, or one systematic unity. He calls them heterogeneous, logically independent, and sometimes antithetic. Different writers traditionally called Romantic can manifest different subsets, and some perhaps none of the candidate ideas.

Yet Lovejoy retains a `Romantic period` and several `Romantic movements` as useful historical scales.

This confirms the M3/M1 distinction:

- essence/unit identity claim: rejected;
- broader period/family/movement grouping: retained.

A broad historiographical family can therefore survive the absence of one common invariant.

The lesson for A4 is precise:

> **lack of a universal invariant is a negative threshold for an essence/equivalence claim, but not for an overlap-family or period grouping.**

So `soft threshold` is not automatically a defect.

# IV. 1932 Deism / Classicism — graded pattern identity rather than strict equivalence

Primary: Arthur O. Lovejoy, `The Parallel of Deism and Classicism`, *Modern Philology* 29.3 (1932), 281–299.

Current source status: argument-level control is secure through Nico Mouton's open-access transcription/discussion and Daniel J. Wilson's annotated bibliography; publication-grade direct-primary quotation/image control remains desirable before verbatim use.

Lovejoy says his object is the `characteristic idea-complex` of Enlightenment rationalism. He distinguishes its elements but emphasizes the `essential unity` and `systematic character` of the complex. The explanatory object is thus a patterned set of preconceptions, not merely one proposition.

Wilson's summary preserves the asymmetry in Lovejoy's application claims:

- deism is treated as the application of this complex to religion;
- neo-classical theory is described more cautiously as `in great part` the application of the same sort of preconceptions to aesthetics.

This is not strict M1 equivalence. It is better read as M4 graded pattern/configuration homology.

The relevant warrant therefore asks:

- how many components of the pattern are present?;
- how strongly do they cohere?;
- which components carry the explanatory weight?;
- how much component substitution is tolerated?;
- is the similarity one of organized structure or merely an analyst-selected list?

A single necessary-and-sufficient feature may be unavailable by design.

### Stress-test result

The `threshold asymmetry` diagnostic should **not** mechanically penalize 1932 for lacking one crisp class boundary. A graded pattern can legitimately have a soft boundary.

But it creates a different burden:

> **profile transparency** — Lovejoy should make visible which components and relations justify saying that two domains instantiate the `same sort` of intellectual configuration, and how partial fit is graded.

Thus M4 requires **profile warrant**, not a fake binary threshold.

# V. 1906 remains different: softness occurs at a stronger macro address

Primary: `The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy`, *The Monist* 16.3 (1906), 357–382; direct-primary article and concordances already preserved in the repository.

1906 is not simply another M4 pattern case.

Lovejoy does several things at once:

1. identifies a causal-mechanism family: impersonal/quasi-mechanical, localized/transmissible efficacy;
2. gives it a name, `Primitive Energetics`;
3. calls it a category more fundamental than personality;
4. promotes it toward `the fundamental concept of primitive philosophy`;
5. makes population-level claims about `most savages` / `many or most races` while retaining explicit procedural and modal qualifications.

The strong warrant burden comes especially from steps 4–5.

A family or pattern can tolerate fuzzy edges without pretending every case belongs to one actor-level fundamental concept. A population-representative claim must additionally specify what distribution of confirming and disconfirming cases supports the promotion.

Hence 1906's `threshold asymmetry` survives the out-of-sample stress test, but in a narrower form:

> **the problem is not merely that the macro category has a soft boundary; it is that a soft or under-specified class-splitting rule is paired with a strong claim of fundamentality and population representativeness.**

This is a specific epistemic burden of M5.

# VI. Threshold topology: different aggregation modes require different failure conditions

The working matrix is now:

| Mode | Aggregation object | Appropriate threshold form | Typical failure condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 strict identity | proposition/operator/class/function | relatively sharp | constitutive invariant lost/reversed |
| M2 thin common core | minimal shared conceptual feature | sharp at thin level | common core absent |
| M3 overlap family | network of partial overlaps | no global essence threshold required | relevant overlap structure disappears / grouping ceases to answer the historical problem |
| M4 graded pattern | multi-feature configuration/profile | graded/weighted | fit too weak, incoherent, or dependent on cherry-picked features |
| M5 population type | type + representative population claim | type threshold + sampling/distribution threshold | counter-distribution undermines `fundamental`/`dominant`/population address |
| M6 persistence/recurrence | identity through lineage or repeated type | route-sensitive | invariant or lineage route fails at the relation being claimed |

This is **analyst formalization**, not Lovejoy's own taxonomy.

# VII. Revised Gate-A architecture

The previous sequence:

`A0 object type`
→ `A1 invariant`
→ `A2 thickness`
→ `A3 tolerated transformation`
→ `A4 negative threshold`

should now become:

`A0 object type`
→ **`A0b aggregation mode`**
→ `A1 invariant / profile / overlap rule`
→ `A2 thickness / profile resolution`
→ `A3 tolerated transformation`
→ `A4 mode-appropriate negative threshold`
→ `I identity/family/pattern judgment`
→ `P presence/operation`
→ `V validity/domain satisfaction`
→ `R representativeness where applicable`
→ `Gate B relation/mediation`.

The insertion of A0b prevents a category error: demanding equivalence-class criteria from a family grouping, or allowing family-resemblance softness to do the work of a population-level `fundamental concept` claim.

# VIII. New general diagnostic: threshold softness vs threshold mismatch

A useful distinction now replaces the earlier undifferentiated suspicion of `soft thresholds`.

## Legitimate threshold softness

The grouping itself is designed as:

- overlap family;
- graded configuration;
- historically useful period/movement category.

The claim is correspondingly modest about identity.

Examples: 1924/1941 Romanticisms; 1932 pattern-level parallel if stated as graded application of a scheme.

## Threshold mismatch

The evidentiary rule remains family-like/soft, but the conclusion is promoted into a stronger claim such as:

- one same unit;
- one actor-level concept;
- fundamental structure;
- dominant/population-wide category;
- direct genealogy.

This is the higher-risk condition.

1906 is currently the strongest candidate for:

`family/mechanism convergence evidence`
→ `strong actor-level / population-level macro address`.

Thus the sharper critical term is:

> **threshold mismatch**, with `threshold asymmetry` as one observable symptom.

# IX. Consequence for the logic genealogy

This materially refines the `logician-like` problem.

Formal logic is especially well suited to M1-style sharp class/relation spaces once the terms/classes are given. It does not dictate how the historian should treat:

- an M3 overlap graph;
- an M4 graded configuration;
- an M5 representative comparative population type.

Therefore the historical portability of Lovejoy's analytical discipline cannot be described as a general conversion of historical categories into formal equivalence classes.

A better formulation is:

> **Lovejoy repeatedly discriminates aggregation modes and relation types, but the sharpness of the warrant varies with the historical object. The most revealing failures occur when evidence appropriate to one aggregation mode is promoted into a stronger mode without an equally strong change in threshold.**

This is more precise than either `Lovejoy was a logician of ideas` or `Lovejoy merely imposed timeless units`.

## Compact conclusion

> **Identity-threshold sharpness is aggregation-mode dependent. Lovejoy's 1941 `infinite` case shows that a very thin common core can still have a clear threshold; his Romanticisms show that a broad family may legitimately lack a universal essence; his 1932 Deism/Classicism operates more like a graded configuration; and 1906 becomes problematic specifically where family/mechanism convergence is promoted into a strong claim of one fundamental, population-representative concept. The key danger is therefore not softness itself but threshold mismatch between the evidentiary mode and the strength of the promoted historical claim.**
