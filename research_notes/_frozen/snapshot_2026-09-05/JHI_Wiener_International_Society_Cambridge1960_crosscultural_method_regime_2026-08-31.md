# Wiener, the International Society for the History of Ideas, and the Cambridge 1960 regime

Date: 2026-08-31  
Status: **HIGH-VALUE INSTITUTIONAL / METHOD / CROSS-CULTURAL BRIDGE — DIRECT ARCHIVAL DESCRIPTIONS + JHI ISSUE CONTROL; ORIGINAL CONFERENCE PAPERS PARTLY OPEN**

## Core result

The 1960 Cambridge first meeting of the **International Society for the History of Ideas** is now a major missing institutional bridge between the Hopkins/JHI first generation and the later internationalized history-of-ideas field.

It should not be treated merely as another conference. The surviving evidence shows that it combined:

- explicit methodological reflection on the history of ideas;
- comparative-civilizational scope;
- Chinese art/literature as central cases rather than supplementary appendices;
- political/social intellectual history;
- direct publication flow into the *Journal of the History of Ideas* in 1961.

This creates a new institutional circuit:

`HOPKINS CLUB / JHI`
→ `INTERNATIONAL SOCIETY FOR THE HISTORY OF IDEAS`
→ `CAMBRIDGE 1960 CONFERENCE`
→ `JHI 1961 PUBLICATION`
→ later conferences / international network.

---

## I. The Society existed as an organized international network by 1960

A 1960 international-associations calendar records:

- `International Society for History of Ideas — 1st meeting`;
- Cambridge, UK;
- 31 August–6 September 1960.

The JHI itself printed an item `International Society for the History of Ideas`, vol.21 no.2 (1960), p.314, demonstrating the Society was directly linked to the journal's communication infrastructure.

Archival survival is unusually strong:

### J. Bronowski papers, Jesus College Cambridge

File `JCPP/Bronowski/Bronowski/3/3/22`, 19 Feb 1960–7 Jul 1965:

- correspondence on topics Bronowski proposed to the Society;
- activities/conferences;
- minutes of a May 1965 meeting;
- associated materials through 1965.

### Paolo Rossi archive, Museo Galileo

Congress file for the **I Meeting**, Cambridge 1960 includes:

- opening report;
- conference report;
- miscellaneous documents.

Rossi archive also records a **II Conference**, Mexico City, 1962.

### Karl Popper papers

A correspondence folder `International Society for the History of Ideas (Aaron Noland, Philip W. Wiener)`, 1959–1974, shows Wiener and Noland operating as Society contacts/organizers and places the Society in a wider philosopher/intellectual network.

These archives make the Society a realistic future archival project rather than a bibliographic ghost.

## II. Conference theme: `Individualism since the Renaissance: Growth or Decline?`

Herbert Butterfield's JHI paper `Reflections on Religion and Modern Individualism` preserves the framing of the inaugural conference.

The title of the proceedings/week was:

`Individualism since the Renaissance: Growth or Decline?`

Butterfield's opening methodological reflection is highly significant. He says the topic can scarcely remain inside the conventional realm of the History of Ideas. Ideas do not merely breed from the logic of preceding ideas or form a genealogy recoverable only from successive books. Intellectual change can emerge from:

- despair/frustration;
- battles/predicaments;
- unrecorded changes in feeling;
- human wrestling with experience.

His compact conclusion is that the History of Ideas repeatedly has to return to the `ocean of General History`.

This is direct evidence that by the first International Society meeting, an explicit contextual/social-historical enlargement of history of ideas was being articulated **within the movement itself**, not only later by external contextualist critics.

---

## III. Comparative civilizations are built into the inaugural methodological problem

Butterfield also makes the individualism problem comparative-civilizational.

He asks why Western civilization proved dynamically developmental after the scholastic era while, in his period language, the Islamic world supposedly did not, and suggests a satisfactory history of individualism may require comparative study of religions and civilizations at a higher level.

The formulation carries obvious civilizational assumptions and should be historically analyzed rather than endorsed.

Methodologically, however, it demonstrates:

`HISTORY OF WESTERN INDIVIDUALISM`
→ requires `COMPARATIVE RELIGION / CIVILIZATION`.

Thus `cross-cultural` comparison is not peripheral to the Society's first meeting; it is one proposed solution to a central explanatory problem.

## IV. Chinese art and literature occupy core conference/publication positions

Paolo Rossi's surviving collection allows a direct conference→publication identification for multiple papers.

### Max Loehr

Typescript:
`The Question of Individualism in Chinese Painting` / later published as `The Question of Individualism in Chinese Art`, JHI 22.2 (1961), 147–158.

Archive note explicitly says it was presented at the **first meeting of the International Society for the History of Ideas, Cambridge 1960**.

### James R. Hightower

Typescript:
`Individualism in Chinese Literature`, subsequently JHI 22.2 (1961), 159–168.

Archive note likewise says presented at the first Cambridge meeting.

### Publication ordering

JHI vol.22 no.2 (Apr–Jun 1961) opens with:

1. Max Loehr, `The Question of Individualism in Chinese Art`, 147–158;
2. James R. Hightower, `Individualism in Chinese Literature`, 159–168.

This is institutionally important. Chinese material is not positioned as a late supplementary regional appendix, as India was in the 1935 *Primitivism* volume. It occupies the opening slots of a normal JHI issue as a direct contribution to a general conceptual problem.

This suggests a historical role change:

`1935 NON-WESTERN SUPPLEMENTARY EXTENSION`
→ `1960/61 NON-WESTERN CORE COMPARATIVE CASE`.

This must be tested more systematically across the conference and issue, but the contrast is strong enough to retain.

## V. Other Cambridge 1960 papers also flowed into JHI

### Aaron Noland

Rossi archive records a typescript `Individualism in Jean Jaurès' Socialist Thought`, presented at Cambridge 1960 and subsequently published JHI 22.1 (1961), 63–80.

### Philip P. Wiener

Rossi/Museo Galileo holdings record the typescript `Some Problems and Methods in the History of Ideas`, presented at the **first meeting** and subsequently published JHI 22.4 (1961), 531–548.

Thus the conference-publication route spans:

- political/social thought (Jaurès);
- religion/civilization (Butterfield);
- Chinese art;
- Chinese literature;
- historiographical methodology (Wiener).

The Society was therefore structurally interdisciplinary and comparative from its inaugural programme.

---

## VI. Wiener 1961: proof of `influence` is already a first-order methodological problem

Wiener's `Some Problems and Methods in the History of Ideas`, JHI 22.4 (1961), 531–548, is now especially significant because Quentin Skinner cites it in `Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas` (1969).

Skinner's footnote states that Wiener had mentioned the relevant condition for using the concept of `influence`, points to the paragraph at **p.537**, and says he had scarcely seen the problems raised by `influence` discussed elsewhere.

Searchable/secondary controls preserve parts of Wiener's paragraph:

- `not all pupils are disciples`;
- direct teacher/pupil proximity is therefore insufficient proof of doctrinal influence;
- Wiener asks whether a broader `climate of opinion` may itself count as influence.

The historical significance is substantial:

> **A central proof problem later canonically associated with Skinner's critique was explicitly present inside JHI and articulated by its second-generation institutional leader by 1961.**

This does not make Wiener equivalent to Skinner. It changes the chronology of the problem.

The direct p.537 original needs full page/image lock before publication quotation and before stating Wiener's exact positive criterion.

## VII. Cambridge 1960 creates a new interpretation of the Lovejoy→Skinner chronology

A simplistic chronology would be:

`LOVEJOY UNIT-IDEAS`
→ `SKINNER 1969 CONTEXT / INFLUENCE CRITIQUE`.

The expanded evidence now requires at least:

`LOVEJOY 1944: recurrence/compositeness are working hypotheses verified by texts`
→ `MANDELBAUM 1948: continuous vs recurrent ideas / diffusion vs independent invention`
→ `PEARCE 1948: analytic phase -> cultural-process synthesis`
→ `WIENER / INTERNATIONAL SOCIETY 1960–61: influence proof + comparative/general-history expansion`
→ `SKINNER 1969: systematic contextualist attack`.

Skinner remains a major methodological rupture, but the problems he canonized were already under active internal differentiation in the history-of-ideas network.

---

## VIII. Internationalization is not merely geographical

The International Society marks several simultaneous enlargements:

### Geographic

US Hopkins/JHI network becomes formally international, with Cambridge 1960, Mexico City 1962, later meetings and participants across countries.

### Object jurisdiction

Chinese visual/literary materials become central cases for a general concept (`individualism`).

### Disciplinary

Art, literature, religion, political thought, philosophy, and general history are placed in one comparative conference.

### Methodological

The Society explicitly debates whether ideas can be understood through book-to-book genealogy alone, and how influence/context/civilization should be handled.

This makes 1960 a plausible **regime-change node**, not just a succession event.

---

## IX. Political/civilizational asymmetry remains visible

The cross-cultural expansion is not equivalent to a contemporary global intellectual-history norm.

Butterfield's Western/Islamic contrast treats civilizations asymmetrically and embeds the conference in midcentury modernization/civilizational questions.

Therefore the historical formula should be:

`COMPARATIVE EXPANSION`
+
`EUROPEAN/WESTERN PROBLEM ADDRESS`
+
`NON-WESTERN CORE CASES`
+
`MIDCENTURY CIVILIZATIONAL HIERARCHIES`.

This is more precise than labeling the Society simply `global`.

## XI. Article-level payoff

This branch materially enlarges the proposed JHI article.

The long history can now be represented as repeated changes in the **institutional carrier** of the same broad jurisdictional problems:

`LOVEJOY'S EARLY SCHOLARLY PRACTICE`
→ `HOPKINS HISTORY OF IDEAS CLUB`
→ `ACLS COMMITTEE / JHI`
→ `INTERNATIONAL SOCIETY FOR THE HISTORY OF IDEAS`
→ `JHI / DICTIONARY / LATER INTERNATIONAL INTELLECTUAL HISTORY`.

And non-Western materials change role along the route:

`INTERNALLY RECONSTRUCTED OBJECT (BUDDHISM)`
→ `COMPARATIVE EVIDENCE (PRIMITIVE RELIGION)`
→ `SUPPLEMENTARY CORPUS (INDIA 1935)`
→ `SOURCE/ANTECEDENT (CHINA-EUROPE)`
→ `CORE COMPARATIVE CASE (CHINESE INDIVIDUALISM 1960/61)`.

This gives a concrete institutional history of `cross-cultural` jurisdiction rather than a retrospective label.
