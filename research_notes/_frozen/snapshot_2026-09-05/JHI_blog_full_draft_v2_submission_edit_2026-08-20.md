# What Was Arthur Lovejoy Doing with Buddhism?

Status: v2 submission edit
Date: 2026-08-20
Target: JHI Blog Think Piece, around 2,000 words including citations/endnotes

In 1969 Quentin Skinner turned Arthur O. Lovejoy’s program of tracing recurrent ideas across historical contexts into one of intellectual history’s canonical methodological problems. Skinner’s objection was not merely that such histories sometimes made bad comparisons. The deeper problem was the assumption that a conceptual object could be fixed in advance and then followed through time: “The notion that any fixed ‘idea’ has persisted is spurious” (Quentin Skinner, “Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas,” 35). The disagreement was real. But among Lovejoy’s papers at Johns Hopkins survives an object that makes the usual chronology look unexpectedly thin: a Harvard Cooperative Society notebook labeled, in his hand, **“Sankhya + Buddhism.”**

The notebook does not contain a forgotten Skinnerian theory. Nor does it reveal the mature “unit-idea” method in embryo. It is more interesting than either possibility. Its pages place the young Lovejoy inside a late-nineteenth-century controversy in which scholars of Indian philosophy were already arguing over a deceptively simple problem: **what kind of relation has the evidence actually established?**

Lovejoy entered that controversy through a dense multilingual literature. Early pages digest Richard Garbe and T. W. Rhys Davids on the relation between Sāṃkhya and Buddhism: how much weight doctrinal resemblance could bear, how late surviving Sāṃkhya texts might support claims about an earlier system, and which features were specific enough to count as historical evidence. At one point Lovejoy marks his own intervention explicitly: “To R.D.’s remark it should be added …” (“Sankhya + Buddhism,” MS p. 63). The physical page matters. It lets us distinguish copied or digested argument from the moment at which Lovejoy begins to alter the test.

A few pages later, a smaller disagreement with the Harvard Buddhist scholar Henry Clarke Warren makes the problem clearer. Warren had warned that the Buddhist formula of dependent origination should not be treated as a simple “chain of causation.” The Pāli term *paccaya*, he argued, marked different relations in different links: antecedent condition, cause and effect, interdependence, even identity. Yet when Warren reached the relation between *taṇhā* (desire) and *upādāna* (attachment), he classified it as one of “identity.”

Lovejoy copied the judgment and then broke from it: “But not so if we are to follow the present text” (“Sankhya + Buddhism,” MS p. 75). He turned instead to Buddhist classifications of the two terms, comparing four kinds of attachment with six forms of desire. The point was not that Warren’s general method was wrong. It was that the relation Warren himself had assigned to this pair did not survive a closer test. Warren had supplied a rule—examine each relation separately—and Lovejoy used that rule against one of Warren’s own conclusions.

The same problem appears at a larger scale in Lovejoy’s engagement with Émile Senart. In 1896 Senart argued that the Buddhist formula of the twelve *nidānas* contained scholastic categories drawn from different sources and fused into an *ordre plus ou moins accidentel*. His treatment of *upādāna* was central to the case. If the term was merely a shortened form of *upādānaskandhas*, the skandhas effectively appeared twice in the series. Repetition and heterogeneity could therefore be read as evidence of a secondary, composite construction.

Lovejoy’s notebook translates Senart’s conclusion almost literally: the categories had been “placed together in an order more or less accidental.” Then the voice changes. “On Senart’s account of the matter there is simply this to be said,” Lovejoy writes (“Sankhya + Buddhism,” MS p. 137). He concedes much of Senart’s premise. The terms need not be original to Buddhism; the formula brings together materials of different kinds; it is not a homogeneous temporal sequence; repetitions occur. Yet he rejects the inference Senart draws from those facts. It is “entirely false,” he writes, to conclude that the formula is therefore illogical or unintelligible, or that its terms have merely been assembled accidentally.

This was not a simple argument against borrowing. Lovejoy could concede borrowed materials and still ask what happened to them inside a new configuration. Evidence about provenance did not, by itself, settle the character of the arrangement.

The distinction survived into Lovejoy’s 1898 article, “The Buddhistic Technical Terms Upadana and Upadisesa.” There he names Senart directly, tests the textual witnesses Senart had cited, and argues that they do not prove the interpretation attributed to them (Lovejoy, “Buddhistic Technical Terms,” 127). The article then states the larger point with unusual clarity: the terms of the Buddhist formula, “perhaps even the ideas,” may be largely borrowed, while their “arrangement and application” remain original and characteristic (132).

The move from notebook to print is revealing in another way. The notebook ranges across the chronology of Sāṃkhya and Buddhism, the composition of philosophical systems, psychological taxonomies, textual witnesses, and competing genealogies. Much of this exploratory surplus disappears from the published article. What remains is narrower: semantic distinctions that can be tested against usage, causal roles that can be differentiated, textual witnesses that can be checked, and claims about configuration that can be separated from claims about provenance.

Lovejoy’s treatment of *upādisesa* shows this compression at its sharpest. In the notebook he argues that *anupādisesa* cannot simply mean physical death or the extinction of the skandhas. For a person freed from desire, he reasons, whether one dies or lives is morally indifferent; to make death itself the *summum bonum* would be profoundly un-Buddhistic (“Sankhya + Buddhism,” MS p. 173). Much of the passage moves almost verbatim into the 1898 article.

But print adds something the notebook states less explicitly: a scale of confidence. Lovejoy concedes that more than one etymology is possible. The meaning of *upādi*, he therefore says, must be settled “rather by an examination of its use than by etymological arguments” (“Buddhistic Technical Terms,” 134). The evidence he adds is “relevant but not necessarily conclusive.” The oldest and probably most numerous passages support one reading, but contrary passages remain. “The matter,” he concludes, “cannot be regarded as finally settled” (136).

At close range, all of this can look like an exceptionally technical quarrel about Pāli vocabulary. At a slightly greater distance, it becomes a dispute over the permissible movement from one kind of evidence to another. Resemblance did not automatically establish identity; compositeness did not establish unintelligibility; etymology did not settle usage; a borrowed element did not prove a borrowed configuration or system.

Nor was this proof problem confined to European scholars classifying Indian materials. In 1871 Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay treated the existence of similarity between Buddhism and Sāṃkhya as distinct both from identity and from the further question of which tradition, if either, had borrowed from the other (“Buddhism and the Sankhya Philosophy,” *Calcutta Review* 53, no. 106 [1871], 191–203). Rajendralala Mitra likewise distinguished the prior existence of philosophical materials from their later construction into a complete Sāṃkhya system and elsewhere rejected genealogy built from phonetic resemblance without adequate proof (*The Yoga Aphorisms of Patañjali*, 1881–83). There is no evidence that Lovejoy read either author. Their importance lies elsewhere. They show that the proof problem itself was transregional: Indian philosophy was not simply raw material to which a completed European method was applied.

This matters for how we place Lovejoy in the later history of intellectual history. Skinner’s critique identified a genuine disagreement. The mature Lovejoy allowed historians to isolate conceptual components and recognize them again across different vocabularies, labels, and historical contexts. Skinner objected that cross-context identity could not simply be presupposed. If an idea was to be understood historically, one had to reconstruct what particular agents were doing with particular expressions in particular situations. On this point, his famous formula appears on the same pages as his critique of Lovejoy: historians should study “not the meanings of the words, but their use” (“Meaning and Understanding,” 37).

Their disagreement was therefore not simply context versus abstraction. It concerned the historical warrant by which something could be identified as the same idea when its contexts changed.

The irony is that the vocabulary of “use” runs through both sides without erasing that difference. In 1898 Lovejoy argued that the meaning of a Buddhist technical term had to be settled by examining its use. Decades later, when discussing Romanticism, he again insisted that meaning depended on usage while rejecting attempts to manufacture a single essence for the term. Skinner’s “use” in 1969 meant something different again: the linguistic action performed by a particular agent on a particular occasion. The recurrence is not a genealogy, and the three uses are not the same. That is precisely the point.

Lovejoy’s Buddhist notebook does not overturn Skinner’s critique. It historicizes the object that the critique helped turn into a disciplinary emblem. Before “Lovejoy” and “contextualism” hardened into rival methodological positions, the young Lovejoy was working in a scholarly field where similarity, identity, use, provenance, function, and genealogy already had to be kept apart. The notebook gives us a rare view beneath those later labels—a workshop in which the historical relation between ideas was itself the problem.
