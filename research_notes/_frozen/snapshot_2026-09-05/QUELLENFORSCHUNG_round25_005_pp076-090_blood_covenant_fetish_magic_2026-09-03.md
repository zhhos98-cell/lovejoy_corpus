## 1. pp.76–77 — messenger sacrifice is an established West-African sacrifice type; local value is mechanism placement, not case invention

The current p.76 editorial summary describes a human victim as a messenger carrying a communication to the dead, potentially a utilitarian act rather than worship. Dahomean and broader West-African literature already treats the slain messenger as a standard functional type of human sacrifice: a victim is instructed with a message and dispatched to a deceased ruler/ancestor.

The later *Encyclopaedia Britannica* summary of the nineteenth-century evidence explicitly lists West-African human sacrifice types including `scapegoat`, `messenger`, and `expiation`, reflecting a much older ethnographic dossier. The underlying messenger practice therefore belongs to the source field.

### Ownership

- victim-as-messenger case and mechanism: F/A0–A1;
- any exact p.76 contrast `utilitarian communication rather than worship` or transition toward mystical/sacramental union: provisional Lovejoy-local only if directly visible in the manuscript; the present page is still an editorial argument summary and cannot support A2 wording on its own.

p.77 likewise remains primarily a comparative source-testing page until direct wording and carriers are recovered.

### Grout source control

Lewis Grout's published Zulu account already contains the ethnographic sequence:

- ancestral shades demand a particular cow;
- the cow is actually surrendered/slaughtered before recovery is requested;
- the prayer asks the cow to cry out and bring/remove the evil in the sufferer/community.

Thus the prayer, particular cow, and evil-removal content are Grout-owned. The W3 manuscript formula asking the cow to carry away the evil is a compressed source rendering, not an independently generated Lovejoy ritual formula.

Focused collation has not recovered Grout wording equivalent to:

- `veritable bargain`;

Tylor's sacrifice theory already treats gift/reciprocity/do-ut-des as a general field category, so `bargain` is not a novel theoretical family. The local seam is the case-specific allocation:

- a bond of fraternity among young men circumcised together;

## 9. pp.88–89 — fetish versus magical object is Tylor-owned; Lang 1898 provides a very close contemporary restatement

The current notebook pages distinguish:

- fetish: effective because a spirit inhabits/is present in the object;
- charm/amulet: may work without an indwelling spirit, through efficacy attributed to the thing itself;
- broader magical objects should not automatically be classified as fetishes.

### Tylor

*Primitive Culture* explicitly restricts strict fetishism to objects for which evidence shows a spirit embodied in, attached to, or acting/communicating through the material object. Tylor warns against calling every mysterious powerful object a fetish and discusses symbolic/magical objects whose efficacy need not require a fully personal indwelling spirit.

Thus the basic classification discipline is Tylor-owned.

### Andrew Lang, 1898

Andrew Lang's *The Making of Religion* provides an especially close contemporary restatement in the year immediately relevant to the Paris notebook. In its chapter on fetishism, Lang notes that objects Europeans call fetishes may be valued for many incompatible reasons, including sympathetic magic, luck, oddness, or mystic qualities; he then quotes Tylor's requirement that strict fetish classification needs an embodied/acting spirit.

This makes the p.88–89 distinction still less suitable for an originality claim.

### What may remain local

The possible Lovejoy delta is not `fetish != charm`; it is the way the notebook may sharpen the contrast into:

`INDWELLING-SPIRIT EFFICACY`
versus
`INTRINSIC / NON-SPIRITUAL EFFICACY`

and then use that contrast to reorganize the blood/charm material around it.

Because current pp.88–89 are mixed diplomatic/editorial summaries, keep this as provisional A2 relation rather than settled wording.

## 10. pp.89–90 — Kingsley owns much of the West-African charm/blood carrier

The p.90 current summary of blood sprinkled at entrances during epidemic disease is therefore strongly Kingsley-owned at ethnographic-content level.
