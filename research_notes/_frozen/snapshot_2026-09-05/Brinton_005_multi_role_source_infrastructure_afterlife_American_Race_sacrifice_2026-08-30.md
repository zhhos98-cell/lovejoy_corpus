- Brinton immediately compares Mexican fertility rites.

# II. Early 005 afterlife architecture and *The Myths of the New World*

Primary notebook controls:

- `archive_transcriptions/MS38_004_001_061_005_p001-015_clean.json`
- `archive_transcriptions/MS38_004_001_061_005_p016-030_clean.json`

The notebook's opening problem is not simply `belief in immortality`.

Lovejoy repeatedly partitions post-mortem differentiation by **what relation licenses the difference**:

- manner of death;
- drowning;
- death in childbirth;
- suicide;
- ritual or funerary qualification/failure;
- social/status continuity;
- kin/clan relation;
- magical condition;
- moral conduct/guilt.

He repeatedly refuses to infer moral retribution merely from the existence of differentiated post-mortem destinations.

This is almost exactly the question formulated in Brinton's *The Myths of the New World*, chapter IX, `The Soul and Its Destiny`.

## 1. Brinton's governing proposition

In the early editions Brinton states, in substance:

- the future world is not a system of rewards and punishments;
- differentiated post-mortem fates are rarely decided by conduct in life;
- they are instead determined by such things as manner of death, the punctual performance of sepulchral rites, or similar conditions outside the individual's control;
- attempts to read Christian heaven/hell or moral retribution into the material are often missionary contamination or misinterpretation.

This is not an incidental sentence. It is the organizing claim of the chapter.

### Version control

- *The Myths of the New World* first appeared in 1868;
- second revised edition: 1876;
- third revised edition: Philadelphia, David McKay, **1896**, 360 pp.
- the 1896 third-edition table of contents still explicitly carries the chapter IX proposition `The future world never a place of rewards and punishments`, while adding/revising material elsewhere (including an expanded `Seat of the Soul` component).

The detailed case-level quotations used in the present audit have been checked in publicly accessible earlier-edition text. The 1896 edition is bibliographically secure and preserves the chapter's governing anti-retribution thesis, but its full case-level variant apparatus has not yet been image-audited here. Therefore do not silently cite first-edition pagination as 1896 pagination.

## 2. Greenland Eskimo case

Brinton's chapter gives the Greenland Eskimo case as follows in the accessible early-edition text:

- the soul remains associated with the body for four days;
- it then goes either upward or below;
- destination varies **according to manner of death**;
- Brinton's footnote points directly to Paul Egede, *Nachrichten von Grönland*, p.145.

Lovejoy's PDF p.11 records a December 14 note on Greenland Eskimo future destiny and explicitly distinguishes:

- two abodes;
- drowned persons and women dying in childbirth as destination-specific cases;
- the absence of a moral notion;
- ritual/magical qualification as distinct from moral fault.

The same page then explicitly says:

> `V. Rink, Tales & Traditions of F. Eskimo, traditions on same point (pp.36–37), p.40.`

Thus Lovejoy is not working only from a Brinton-level synthesis. Rink is on his direct bibliography and is consulted by page.

## 3. Rink provides an adversarial/source-level control

Hinrich Rink, *Tales and Traditions of the Eskimo* (1875), `Religion`, pp.37–43, gives a more complicated account than a clean `non-moral afterlife` formula.

Rink says:

- the dead go to an upper or under world;
- the under world is preferable, warm and rich in food;
- older authorities also reported faint notions of reward/punishment;
- witches and bad people could be placed above;
- persons who performed heroic actions or suffered severely, including those drowned at sea and women dying in childbirth, could go below;
- treatment of the corpse by survivors can affect the soul's condition.

This is precisely why the notebook's analytic labor matters.

Lovejoy does not merely repeat `different destinations = non-moral`.

He asks whether apparent reward-like structure is actually indexed to:

`suffering`
vs
`manner of death`
vs
`ritual condition`
vs
`moral guilt`.

That is a relation-partitioning move inside the evidence, not only a borrowed substantive conclusion.

## 4. Suicide and violence

Brinton's chapter records Huron material in which:

- suicides and persons killed in war live apart;
- `scoundrels` are nevertheless not excluded;
- Brazilian populations divide dead persons by drowning, violent death, and disease without an ethical rationale.

Lovejoy's early notebook similarly refuses to identify suicide or anomalous post-mortem treatment automatically with moral guilt. Suicide, violent death, taboo, clan solidarity and ritual condition are kept analytically distinct.

Again the important concordance is not just the conclusion `no morality`.

It is the **variable set** used to test the conclusion.
