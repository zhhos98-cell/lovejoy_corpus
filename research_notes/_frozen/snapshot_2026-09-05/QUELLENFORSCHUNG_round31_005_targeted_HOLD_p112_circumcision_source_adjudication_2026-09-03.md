## 1. The rival genealogy is historically explicit: Frederick Starr, 1891

A precise contemporary print source is now identified.

Frederick Starr, `Dress and Adornment. IV. Religious Dress`, *Popular Science Monthly* 40 (December 1891), treats circumcision under religious mutilations. Starr argues that blood shedding can establish an artificial relation with a deity and calls Jewish circumcision a blood covenant / tribal mutilation. In the same passage he says that circumcision is also, `very clearly`, a **substitute for human sacrifice**.

This is a particularly useful control because the two explanatory lines later separated on Lovejoy's p.112 already coexist in one pre-Paris source:

`CIRCUMCISION -> BLOOD COVENANT`

and

`CIRCUMCISION -> SUBSTITUTE FOR HUMAN SACRIFICE`.

Therefore Lovejoy is not confronting an anachronistically reconstructed theory. The rival genealogy was explicit in published anthropology by 1891.

This source does not by itself establish direct Starr -> Lovejoy uptake. It establishes the historical availability and specificity of the theory Lovejoy rejects.

## 3. The anti-single-genus / negative-case method is also upstream

The general methodological move `outward similarity / bodily injury / bloodshed does not by itself establish one explanatory genus` is not safely Lovejoy-specific.

### Marillier 1897

Marillier explicitly rejects classifications built from `ressemblances extérieures` and argues that when one theory fails to explain the whole group, other motives must be sought. The existing 005–Marillier operator concordance already places p.112 under this `external resemblance != explanatory genus` relation.

### Steinmetz / Mauss

Contemporary accounts of Steinmetz show a comparative method already attentive to contrary facts, deviations, variation, and source reliability. Mauss's critique then moves to the level of **classification before counting**: broad rubrics can remain defective even when contrary facts are conscientiously recorded.

Batch 47 therefore correctly warns against a simple genealogy in which Lovejoy invents counterexample-sensitive comparison.
