# Batch 16 — Lovejoy’s 1907 Vedānta review inside the Deussen–Woods Harvard Indic-philosophy network

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced

## Research move

The 1907 Lovejoy review of Paul Deussen’s *Outline of the Vedānta System of Philosophy According to Shankara* should no longer be treated only as an isolated late survival of the 1898 Buddhist episode. A new institutional/source pass identifies the English book Lovejoy reviewed as a translation by **James Haughton Woods and Catharine Bird Runkle**, and places Woods inside Harvard’s professional formation of Indian philosophy and comparative religion. This gives the review a much more concrete scholarly ecology.

## 1. The reviewed book and the translators

Lovejoy’s review is bibliographically confirmed as:

- Arthur O. Lovejoy, review of Paul Deussen, *Outline of the Vedānta System of Philosophy, According to Shankara*, *Journal of Philosophy, Psychology and Scientific Methods* **4.1 (1907), 23–24**.

PhilPapers independently records the review and page range. The reviewed English *Outline* is a translation by **James Haughton Woods and Catharine Bird Runkle**. Google Books, CiNii and Open Library all confirm the translator pair and a 1906 English edition; bibliographic records differ over the precise imprint/edition relationship (Harvard University Press vs. Grafton Press), so the current project should not collapse those records without consulting the title/copyright pages of the exact copy Lovejoy reviewed. The stable fact is the Woods–Runkle translation.

Wilson’s annotated bibliography supplies the current content synopsis of Lovejoy’s two-page review: Lovejoy calls the translation a useful “convenience” for teachers of Hindu philosophy and urges greater attention to **Rāmānuja’s Vedānta**, rather than further concentration on Śaṅkara. This wording remains secondary until the primary review pages are extracted.

## 2. James Haughton Woods was a major Harvard Indic-philosophy node

Harvard’s official East Asian Languages and Civilizations history gives Woods an unusually important institutional role. It describes him as a scholar of **Greek and Indic philosophy**, says that he studied under **Paul Deussen**, then pursued further study in India at Benares and Kashmir, and returned to Harvard in 1903 to the Philosophy Department, eventually becoming Professor of the **Philosophical Systems of India**. Harvard also credits him with translations of Pāli and Sanskrit scriptures, comparative-religion scholarship, and later institutional work supporting Japanese/Chinese studies and the Harvard-Yenching Institute.

Harvard University Archives, **HUG 1880.2xx**, independently preserves Woods’s papers. The finding aid describes correspondence, writings on comparative religion/languages/philosophies, philosophy lecture notes, and a 1903 diary probably written while he was studying philosophy in India. It likewise records his investigations of Indian philosophy (1902–03), Japan (1907–09), translations of Pāli/Sanskrit scriptures, and Buddhist interests.

This means the Deussen book Lovejoy reviewed in 1907 entered English through a translator who was not merely a general Harvard philosopher but a specialist building Indian philosophy into Harvard’s philosophy curriculum and Asian-studies infrastructure.

## 3. Evidence limit: Woods was not Lovejoy’s Harvard teacher in 1895–98

The chronology blocks a tempting but unsupported story. Harvard/Crimson biographical notices place Woods’s Philosophy appointment from **1900 onward** (with Anthropology in 1900–01 and Philosophy positions after his Deussen/India period); the Harvard EALC history has him returning to Harvard in 1903 after study with Deussen and in India. Lovejoy’s principal Harvard formation was 1895–98.

Therefore:

- Lanman/Everett/Toy remain the confirmed direct teachers relevant to Lovejoy’s early Orientalist/comparative-religion formation.
- Woods belongs to the **professional/institutional Indic-philosophy network** surrounding the 1907 review.
- A Woods→Lovejoy teacher-student relation in the 1890s should not be claimed.

## 4. Later Woods–Lovejoy institutional relation is directly documented

A much later connection is secure. The *Harvard Crimson* reported on **18 March 1932** that Lovejoy had been appointed the second William James Lecturer and that **J. H. Woods, chairman of the Department of Philosophy, announced the appointment**. The report already identifies Lovejoy’s planned lecture series as *The Great Chain of Being* / a study in the history of ideas.

This creates a genuine long-range professional link:

- 1906–07: Woods translates Deussen’s Śaṅkara outline; Lovejoy reviews it and, according to Wilson, calls for Rāmānuja work.
- 1932–33: Woods, now Harvard Philosophy chair, announces Lovejoy’s William James Lectureship, whose lectures become the *Great Chain of Being* project.

The existence of this long relation is useful institutional history, but **it does not establish a causal line from Woods’s Indology to Lovejoy’s mature history of ideas**. Direct correspondence or departmental records would be needed before making that stronger claim.

## 6. Interpretation for the “Lovejoy as Orientalist” project

The strict-core timeline now looks less like one 1898 Buddhist publication followed by disappearance. By 1907 Lovejoy is still reviewing current specialist Indian-philosophy literature, and the book he reviews belongs to a live Harvard pipeline connecting Deussen, Woods, Indian study, and the institutionalization of Indian philosophy inside a philosophy department.

If Wilson’s review synopsis is confirmed by the primary text, Lovejoy’s call for attention to Rāmānuja is especially significant. It would show him resisting the reduction of “Vedānta” to Śaṅkara/Advaita alone and asking for comparative differentiation **within** the Indian system. That analytical reflex would fit the broader operation already visible in 004/1898/1902: do not treat a named tradition as an indivisible doctrinal block.

For now the correct evidentiary formulation is:

> **Lovejoy’s 1907 Vedānta review is strict-core evidence of continuing engagement with Indian philosophy and can now be placed inside the Deussen–Woods Harvard Indic-philosophy network. The institutional context is strong; direct Lovejoy–Woods exchange in 1907 remains unproved.**
