# Lovejoy as Orientalist — web sweep batch 06: China and the mature history of ideas, 1933

Date: 2026-08-18

## Primary text and bibliographic status

Arthur O. Lovejoy, **“The Chinese Origin of a Romanticism,”** *The Journal of English and Germanic Philology* 32.1 (January 1933), 1–20. JSTOR independently confirms the article, issue, author, and pagination. The essay was later **revised and expanded** for *Essays in the History of Ideas* (1948); the repository’s OCR corpus contains that expanded version.

The article has a continuing afterlife in scholarship on eighteenth-century landscape gardening, Anglo-Chinese cultural exchange, and Romanticism. Recent scholarship still treats Lovejoy’s 1933 claim as a foundational or at least unavoidable intervention, even when revising his emphasis on irregularity.

## What kind of “Orientalist” work is this?

Close reading of the 1948 expanded primary text shows that this is **not Sinology in the same sense as Lovejoy’s 1898 Buddhist technical philology**. The evidentiary architecture is overwhelmingly a history of **European representations, translations, reports, and appropriations of China**. Lovejoy’s causal question is how the European image of Chinese civilization and Chinese gardening entered European aesthetic argument and helped legitimate a new taste for irregularity, variety, and “beauty without order.”

The essay’s principal evidentiary chain includes:

- sixteenth/seventeenth-century European reports on China;
- Juan González de Mendoza’s early history of China and Montaigne’s response to it;
- Jesuit descriptions and translations, including *Confucius Sinarum philosophus*;
- Athanasius Kircher’s *China illustrata*;
- Leibniz’s *Novissima Sinica*;
- Fénelon, Christian Wolff, Quesnay, Goldsmith, and Voltaire on Chinese government, ethics, and natural religion;
- Sir William Temple’s reports of Chinese gardening and `sharawadgi`;
- Louis Le Comte and other European China accounts;
- the Jesuit Jean-Denis Attiret’s description of the imperial gardens;
- William Chambers’s writings on Chinese gardens and architecture;
- eighteenth-century English and French aesthetic/garden literature (Addison, Pope, Walpole, Mason, etc.).

The Chinese case is therefore mediated through a European documentary chain. Lovejoy is reconstructing a **transmission history of an image of China**, not primarily interpreting Chinese-language philosophical or aesthetic texts.

## The methodological continuity is nevertheless strong

Despite the change of source regime, several operations familiar from the 004/1898 Buddhist material persist:

1. **Separate historical components before naming a movement.** The essay begins by distinguishing several movements that later get bundled into a single story of “Romanticism”: new landscape-garden practice, the picturesque, Gothic revival, and the admiration for Chinese gardens.
2. **Trace a proposition through changing documentary carriers.** The relevant object is not “China” in the abstract but a historically circulating claim about Chinese irregular gardening, carried through travelers, missionaries, translations, essays, and garden theory.
3. **Distinguish source from later arrangement.** Lovejoy asks when Europeans began to attach a specific aesthetic value to what they believed Chinese gardens exemplified, and how that Chinese attribution was reassembled within European arguments about nature, order, and taste.
4. **Treat reception as causal evidence.** The essay does not require the European representation to be an ethnographically perfect account of Chinese gardens in order for it to matter historically. What matters for the European history of ideas is that contemporaries took the representation as Chinese and used it as authority.

This resembles the early Buddhist case in a formal sense: inherited/borrowed material changes historical function when inserted into a new conceptual arrangement. The object of analysis has shifted from technical terms within an Indic doctrinal system to representations moving between cultural systems.

## Important limit: do not call the 1933 essay direct Sinological expertise

At present we have no evidence that Lovejoy used Chinese-language sources for this article. The expanded version’s visible source architecture is European-language and missionary/travel literature. A defensible formulation is:

> By 1933 Lovejoy was using China as a transmission problem within the mature history of ideas. This was Orientalist in subject and archive, but its method was principally the reconstruction of European representations of China rather than direct Sinological philology.

This sharply distinguishes the mature China strand from his confirmed earlier Sanskrit/Buddhist-text training and Pāli-Buddhist ambitions.

## Historiographical afterlife

- JSTOR confirms the original 1933 publication.
- The modern *Wordsworth Circle* notes that Lovejoy’s thesis—that one source of a Romantic aesthetic lay in Chinese irregular landscape gardening—was unusually early and remains relevant to current work on Qing China as a Romantic-period referent.
- Recent work on English landscaping continues to cite Lovejoy alongside Osvald Sirén and later scholarship; some scholars revise his explanation by emphasizing “naturalness” rather than irregularity as the crucial category.
- A modern Journal of the History of Ideas in East Asia has republished the essay in Chinese translation, itself evidence of a later East-Asian reception of this classic history-of-ideas argument.

## Relation to the larger Orientalist trajectory

The working three-phase model becomes more precise:

1. **1896–1901 — direct training and specialist practice:** Sanskrit/Buddhist texts under Lanman; private sacred-text reading with Sylvain Lévi; 004; 1898 JAOS; 1901 Buddhism lectures.
2. **c.1901–1906 — comparative-religion transformation:** 005; sacrifice/magic/efficacy; 1906 “primitive philosophy.”
3. **1930s — mediated/global transmission history:** non-European traditions return as causal nodes within history-of-ideas problems. The 1933 China essay works mainly through European textual representations; the 1935 primitivism volume distributes Indian and ancient West-Asian expertise to P.-E. Dumont and W. F. Albright.

The discontinuity matters as much as the continuity. Lovejoy does not simply remain the same kind of Orientalist throughout his career. His relation to Asian material changes from student/philologist to comparative theorist to organizer of cross-cultural transmission histories and distributed specialist expertise.
