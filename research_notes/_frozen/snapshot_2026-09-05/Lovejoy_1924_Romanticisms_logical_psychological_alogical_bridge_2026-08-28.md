# Lovejoy 1924 — Romanticisms: logical, psychological, semasiological, and alogical relations

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: **DIRECT-TEXT INTERMEDIATE BRIDGE / CONTEXT-DIFFERENTIATION CONTROL**

Primary: Arthur O. Lovejoy, `On the Discrimination of Romanticisms`, *PMLA* 39.2 (June 1924), 229–253.  
Purpose: test the gap between the 1911 formal-logic jurisdiction boundary and the mature 1941 partition of logical / psychological / historical-genetic relations.

## Core result

The 1924 Romanticisms paper supplies a strong intermediate bridge, but not a bridge from `formal logic` to `history` in the simple sense.

Lovejoy explicitly coordinates several differently typed operations:

- discrimination of historical episodes/movements;
- semasiological study of the word `romantic`;
- analysis of thought-complexes into constituent ideas/aesthetic susceptibilities;
- logical relations among ideas;
- psychological/associative relations among ideas;
- historical genesis of labels and complexes.

Most importantly, he explicitly says the elements of individual Romanticisms were, for the most part, **not** joined by indissoluble logical necessity but by **alogical associative processes**.

This gives direct actor-level evidence for a distinction that the current logical-analysis branch must preserve:

> **analytical decomposition can identify components and logical relations without implying that the historical composition of the complex was itself logically generated.**

The constitution of a historical complex can instead depend on associative, semantic, psychological and historical mechanisms.

---

## 1. The object is literary/intellectual history, not formal logic

Lovejoy begins from a historiographical-semantic problem: `Romanticism` has accumulated so many discrepant definitions and applications that the singular label cannot safely be assumed to designate one real entity.

His first remedy is semasiological: reconstruct how the term acquired its changing connotations and denotations through associative processes.

The sequence is therefore already multi-jurisdictional:

`word/label`
→ `history of usage and association`
→ `discriminate historical episodes`
→ `analyze each episode/complex into elements`.

Formal logic is not the primary object.

Coding: `A2 explicit-logical-method / H + semantics + psychology`, with local historical objects.

---

## 2. Historical sameness is not licensed by shared nomenclature

Lovejoy insists that different historical episodes called `Romanticism` must not be presumed identical merely because later scholars gave them the same name.

This continues the 1908–09 anti-transfer rule in a different domain:

`same label`
≠ `same historical entity`
≠ `same constituent ideas`
≠ `same relations among those ideas`.

There may be overlapping elements, but the overlap can vary pairwise. Romanticism A may share X with B while sharing Y with C, with X absent from C.

This is set-like or relation-sensitive reasoning, but the local problem is historiographical identity, not formal-logical opposition.

---

## 3. Decomposition and composition are separate questions

Lovejoy asks that each historical Romanticism be resolved into constituent ideas and aesthetic susceptibilities. This is decompositional and directly relevant to the later unit-idea programme.

But he immediately blocks a crucial inference: the components of the complex are generally not joined by logical necessity.

Working distinction:

`analyst can discriminate components`
≠ `components are logically necessitated by one another`
≠ `their historical co-presence has a logical cause`.

This is an important control against reading the chemical/analytical metaphor too literally. The analyst's ability to isolate units and test logical relations does not turn a historical compound into a logical system.

---

## 4. `alogical associative processes` are part of the explanatory machinery

The paper gives associative processes a positive explanatory role. Ambiguity of the word `romantic` facilitates the bundling of heterogeneous ideas; historically formed complexes may therefore have psychological/associative rather than logical bonds.

This produces a richer relation architecture:

`logical relation among ideas`
≠ `psychological association`
≠ `semantic association through a label`
≠ `historical co-occurrence`
≠ `genesis/transmission of the complex`.

The relation types can interact without collapsing.

This is extremely close in architecture to the mature 1941 instruction to distinguish logical, psychological and historical/genetic relations, though direct causal continuity from 1924 to 1941 should not be assumed merely from resemblance.

---

## 5. 1911 → 1924 → 1941: a controlled bridge

### 1911, `Existence and Formal Logic`

Lovejoy limits the jurisdiction of formal logic:

`formal implication`
≠ `meaning/intelligibility`
≠ `verification/evidence`
≠ `historical/existential truth`.

### 1924, `On the Discrimination of Romanticisms`

Lovejoy distinguishes:

`logical relations / necessity`
≠ `psychological or alogical association`
≠ `semantic history of a label`
≠ `historical identity of movements`.

### 1941, `The Meaning of Romanticism for the Historian of Ideas`

Lovejoy explicitly partitions the historian's questions into logical, psychological, and historical/genetic relations, treating logical and hypothetical psychological relations first as analyst-constructed hypotheses that must be returned to historical evidence.

### Verdict

The three moments support a **person-level recurrence of jurisdictional partition**.

They do not yet prove one unchanged technique transmitted continuously for thirty years. The safer formulation is:

> **Across changing objects, Lovejoy repeatedly prevents one relation-type from doing the evidentiary work of another; by 1924 this discipline is explicit inside literary/intellectual history itself, not only inside formal logic.**

---

## 6. Consequence for the unit-idea genealogy

The unit-idea problem now has to be split at least four ways:

1. **analytical individuation**: by what criteria does the historian isolate a component?;
2. **logical relation**: what does that component imply, exclude, presuppose or permit?;
3. **historical composition**: why were particular components bundled together in a movement, system or period?;
4. **historical identity/recurrence**: what licenses the claim that a component is the same idea across changed contexts?

The 1924 paper is especially strong for (2) versus (3): historical composition may be alogical/associative even when components are analytically separable and logically comparable.

The Skinner-facing controversy remains mainly at (4), not at the mere fact of decomposition.
