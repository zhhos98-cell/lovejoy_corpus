## 1. Published conceptual architecture

### p.357–358 — attack on anthropomorphic master theories

Lovejoy begins with Brinton, Guyau, Tylor, Spencer and Grant Allen. He accepts anthropomorphism/animism as real but rejects its elevation into the foundational explanatory category. The conventional model makes primitive religion a world of transactions between persons, expressed as propitiation, entreaty, bargain, alliance, friendship and cajolery.

### p.359 — `category` and hidden logic

Lovejoy says the apparently contradictory body of practice is not a haphazard collection of superstitions: it has a **logic** that can be unraveled. He calls the paper the first chapter of a larger study intended to bring more system and connectedness to familiar ethnographic data.

He then explicitly posits a **category more fundamental than personality**: a conception of causation that is **impersonal and quasi-mechanical**.

### pp.359–360 — transferable energy model

The proposed category includes:

- a diffused/interconnected impersonal energy or vital force;
- unequal localization in persons and things;
- unusual ability/power/productivity as evidence of high concentration;
- transfer from one person/thing to another;
- mechanical control, regulation and insulation;
- transmission by contact without volition;
- rites whose practical function is manipulation of this force.

### p.361 — no sharp magic/religion divide

Lovejoy directly disputes Frazer’s attempt to separate magic from religion by mechanical versus personal causation. **Diffusive and transferable life-energy** can determine practices classified as religious just as much as those called magical. The practical problem is not primarily friendly relations with supernatural persons but well-adjusted **quasi-mechanical relations** with sources of energy.

### p.362 onward — lexical/philological test

The paper then asks whether early languages contain terms for universal, impersonal, communicable energy. It organizes evidence under:

- North Americans: `manitou`, `oki`, `wakonda`;
- Oceania: `mana` and related vocabulary;
- Bantu: words commonly translated as god/spirit but reinterpreted through power/property/efficacy.

The paper repeatedly argues that ethnographers have mistranslated words of power/efficacy as personal gods or spirits.

## 2. Direct notebook concordance

### `quasi-mechanical`

005 p.105 calls agrarian sacrifice of the plant-soul to itself **“quasi-mechanical and magical rather than sacramental,”** because it acts without yet addressing a personal plant deity.

1906 pp.359–361 uses `impersonal and quasi-mechanical` for the foundational causal category and `quasi-mechanical relations` for the practical religious relation to energy.

This is both a lexical and structural match. The date of p.105 itself remains unknown; the notebook as a whole is independently documented in use through Dec. 1905.

### machine / forces of nature

005 p.64 describes magical sacrifice as operating **“in a sort of machine”** and distinguishes rites acting directly on forces of nature from rites constraining the wills of gods.

That is nearly the conceptual opposition with which the published paper is constructed: causal efficacy need not be conscious volition or personal agency.

### fetish vs charm/amulet

005 pp.88–90 separates:

- fetish: efficacy through an inhabiting/present spirit;
- charm/amulet/plant/stone/blood: efficacy can be intrinsic to the thing, with no spiritualistic idea required.

Published 1906 opens precisely by challenging the assumption that efficacy implies an animating spirit/person and turns to power terms that ethnographers have misrendered as `god` or `spirit`.

### blood/body parts and transferable vitality

005 pp.84–90 attributes direct protective, curative, rejuvenating and transferable efficacy to blood and bodily parts; p.119 treats selected organs as transmitting intelligence, courage/life or sexual power. These mechanisms are repeatedly separated from communion or spirit possession.

The published model treats life-energy as localized in bodies and things, transferable by contact, and the object of ritual manipulation.

### agriculture / fecundative transfer

005 pp.98–105 analyzes blood on crops/seed, agricultural human sacrifice, life transfer and plant-soul sacrifice as mechanical/fecundative mechanisms distinct from communion with a personal deity.

The 1906 North-American material uses totemic/agricultural evidence to illustrate common life/directive energy and the transfer/manipulation of power.

### sacramental/personal theory demoted

005 p.117 concludes that sacramental union is comparatively exceptional and that sacrifice more often operates through alimentary, expiatory or propitiatory mechanisms. Throughout the notebook, shared blood/food/cannibalism is insufficient evidence for communion.

Published 1906 similarly demotes the personal/social theory of religion and denies that `religion` can be reserved for personal relations while quasi-mechanical efficacy is relegated to `magic`.

### Frazer as a common foil

005 repeatedly criticizes Frazerian and communion/evolutionary over-systematization. Published p.361 explicitly singles out Frazer’s sharp magic/religion distinction for rejection.

### overlapping source ecology

Secure overlaps or near-overlaps include:

- **Brinton**: 005 contains Brinton bibliography/material; 1906 begins from Brinton’s theory, though the securely transcribed notebook item is a different Brinton title.
- **Codrington/Melanesia**: Codrington is in 005’s opening bibliography; Oceanic `mana` is a central regional test in 1906.
- **Bantu/southern Africa**: a major 005 evidence field and one of the three major lexical zones in 1906.
- **North American material**: Pawnee/Dakota and related evidence already occurs in 005; 1906 opens its empirical proof with North-American `wakonda/manitou/oki`.

## 3. New production model

The 1906 paper describes itself as less an attempt to add ethnological data than to **correlate familiar data and bring out their significance**. This self-description fits 005 remarkably well as a long empirical/classificatory dossier.

The strongest current model is:

> **Marillier/EPHE empirical and comparative-religion formation, 1898–99 → longitudinal notebook 005, demonstrably reused through 1905 → 1906 extraction of one cross-cutting category from that dossier: impersonal, diffusive, transferable, quasi-mechanical life-energy.**

This is stronger than thematic resemblance. It is not yet equivalent to identifying 005 as the literal article draft.

## 4. Methodological significance beyond comparative religion

The primary article itself uses vocabulary relevant to Lovejoy’s later method:

- a fundamental `category` behind more visible doctrines;
- a recoverable `logic` inside heterogeneous practices;
- a demand for more `system` and `connectedness`;
- diverse facts becoming `connectedly intelligible` through analytical correlation;
- surface labels (`god`, `spirit`, `magic`, `religion`) being decomposed in favor of underlying causal structure.

Combined with the 1902 *Religion and the Time-Process* (`categories`, `presuppositions`, `logical motives`, rejection of resemblance-as-borrowing) and the 1898/004 Buddhist analysis (composite terms/system but intelligible arrangement), this materially strengthens the genealogy of Lovejoy’s later history-of-ideas operations.

The mature `unit-idea` language should still not be projected backward. What is now documentable is continuity in **analytical operations**.
