## Core finding

The American Lectures on the History of Religions explicitly described their model as popular history-of-religions courses `somewhat after the style of the Hibbert lectures in England`.

The Boston/Cambridge side of the organization directly overlapped with Lovejoy's Harvard teachers:

- Charles Rockwell Lanman and Crawford H. Toy participated in the 1891 founding network from Boston/Cambridge;
- Toy became chairman/president of the committee and represented Boston/Cambridge;
- first course, winter 1894–95: T. W. Rhys Davids, *The History and Literature of Buddhism*, including Boston at the Lowell Institute;
- Rhys Davids had himself delivered the 1881 Hibbert Lectures and co-translated *Vinaya Texts* for Müller's *Sacred Books of the East*;
- second course, winter 1896–97: Daniel G. Brinton, *The Religions of Primitive Peoples*, again including Boston at the Lowell Institute;
- Toy served on the publication committee for Brinton's 1897 volume.

Independent controls place Lovejoy at Harvard during the 1896–97 academic year in the Everett/Toy/Lanman environment.

Therefore Brinton's `primitive peoples` synthesis was **institutionally and temporally proximate** to Lovejoy's Harvard formation, rather than only a later book he happens to attack in 1906.

## JHI significance

This strengthens, rather than changes, the existing Harvard paragraph.

The point is not that Harvard transmitted a unified method. The ecology had several adjacent resolutions:

- Lanman: primary-language / Buddhist textual work;
- Toy: religion-specific historical comparison;
- Everett: general comparative religion;
- American Lectures: public synthetic courses by specialists.

The first two American series themselves placed:

`BUDDHISM (RHYS DAVIDS)`
next to
`PRIMITIVE PEOPLES (BRINTON)`.

So Buddhist philology and broad anthropological religion-comparison were institutionally adjacent before Lovejoy's own work later crossed both domains.
