## Core rhetorical rule

The article should not move with one continuous emotional or argumentative tone. It should repeatedly establish an order, unsettle it, change scale, and return to an earlier object with altered meaning.

Desired movement:

- playful retrospective surprise;
- immediate correction of the surprise;
- narrowing into a small textual disagreement;
- expansion into a larger historical-compositional problem;
- stillness/close reading at manuscript p.123;
- cooling into publication selection and institutional context;
- change of evidence in Paris;
- brief conceptual clarity in 1902;
- discomfort and political stakes in 1906;
- refusal of a tidy developmental line within 1906 itself;
- quiet mature comparison in *Great Chain*;
- return to bibliography/historiography with the opening transformed.

The article should not feel as though one thesis simply grows stronger paragraph by paragraph.

## 中文故事版本

后来收入《Essays in the History of Ideas》的著作目录，给 Lovejoy 安排了一个颇为古怪的开端。排在第一项的，不是《Great Chain of Being》的前声，也不是后来人熟悉的 unit-ideas，而是一篇 1898 年讨论两个佛教技术术语的文章。这个开端当然是假的——Lovejoy 更早已经发表过东西。可这个错误的第一印象反而有用。因为还没开始读 Lovejoy，我们已经撞见了一件后来者会反复做的事：把一段并不整齐的学术生涯重新排列出一个“起点”。

然而，事情也没有简单到“原来大家都忘了 Lovejoy 研究过佛教”。他们没有。Wilson 知道他的比较宗教训练，知道 Sanskrit、Buddhist texts，也知道 Paris；到 2010 年，Schaffer 仍然可以顺手写下 Lovejoy 在 Paris 学过 Pāli 和 Sanskrit。这个事实一直在那里。真正模糊的，是另外一件事：当佛教材料真正占据 Lovejoy 的工作桌时，他到底在做什么？传记保存了一个经历，却没有自动保存这段经历的学术形状。

Notebook 004 把问题一下子缩得很小。Lovejoy 抄下 Warren 对 `taṇhā` 和 `upādāna` 的判断，然后中途插了一句：“But not so.” 这不是什么宏大的 methodological manifesto。甚至恰恰相反，Warren 本人已经知道 dependent origination 中的关系并不都一样；Lovejoy 并没有发明“应该区分关系”这个问题。他只是拒绝接受 Warren 在这里给出的那个具体答案，然后回到分类和文本里重新检查。一个后来以“大观念”著称的人，最先出现在这里的姿态，反倒相当琐碎：这个判断，凭这些证据，真的成立吗？

可 notebook 很快又不再琐碎。到了 Senart，问题从两个词之间的关系膨胀成一个传统本身是怎样被构成的。Lovejoy 可以承认历史混合，承认借用，甚至承认不同部分可能来自不同地方；他却不愿意因此直接接受，整个组合已经被证明是偶然的、杂乱的、不可理解的。这里也不能把 Lovejoy 写成孤独的突破者——Oldenberg 等人已经在反驳 Senart 的具体语义判断。Lovejoy 的动作更窄，也更麻烦：证据也许已经说明“这些部分从哪里来”，却还没有说明“这些部分组合起来以后是什么”。

1898 年发表时，这种麻烦被留下了一部分，也被删掉了一部分。`arrangement and application` 进入了公开论证；更宽的 Sāṃkhya/Buddhism chronology、更大胆的历史猜测、同时并存的解释可能性，则大量退到了纸外。于是 notebook 与 article 的关系并不是“早期草稿终于成熟”。更像是一个研究现场被压缩成了一项可以承担公开责任的论证。发表不是把 notebook 擦亮，而是缩小了其中一些句子的尺寸。

有一页尤其难忘。`viññāṇa` 可以在分类上落在 `nāma` 之内，同时又在时间上先于 `nāmarūpa`。如果硬要把它们压成一种关系，Lovejoy 看起来会自相矛盾；可 notebook 根本没有在问同一个问题。分类、先后、因果、同一、来源，并不自动排成一条轴。这里最好不要急着说“这就是 Lovejoy 的方法”。这页纸的力量恰恰在于它还没有成为一种方法论声明：它只是让不同问题同时占据同一张纸，而且谁也没有权利替另一个问题回答。

到这里，Harvard 才值得出现。但它不能被写成答案。Lanman 的 Sanskrit/Pāli、Toy 的比较性 Semitic religion、Everett 的 comparative religion，能说明这种混合的工作方式在制度上并非无处可归；它们却不能合成一个叫作“Lovejoy method”的东西。Harvard 在这里不是源头，而只是让 notebook 看起来不那么凭空。它解释一种邻接，不解释一种必然。

随后材料又换了。Notebook 005 不再围绕 Pāli technical terms，而是死亡、仪式、来世、社会关系。看上去像奖赏或惩罚的死后命运，未必由道德行为造成；也可能与死法、仪式、身份、家庭或群体有关。Marillier 和 Söderblom 已经在这个领域做类似区分，因此这里同样没有一个清洁的原创故事。可换了材料以后，麻烦没有消失：一个可见的结果，究竟允许你推出哪一种解释？

1902 年，Lovejoy 自己又把读者带回 1898 年的佛教论文。但这一次对象变成 Aristotle 和 Vedānta。两者可以有 affinity，却未必因此证明 Oriental transmission；如果 Greek intellectual history 足以解释 Greek doctrine，相似本身就不能承担传播史的重量。这不是“佛教方法已经扩张到别处”的证据。更谨慎地说，是同一种不耐烦再次出现：不要让一种关系冒充另一种关系。

然后，1906 年突然变得不舒服了。对象不再是词，也不是仪式，而是 “Man”。Lovejoy 说十九世纪把抽象的人一方面 “longitudinally” 按发展阶段切开，另一方面又 “cross-wise” 按 race 或 nationality 区分。此时 historical differentiation 不再只是解释文献时的谨慎。它开始进入殖民治理、黑人选举权、disfranchisement。区分得更细，并不会自动把政治变得更好。一个人完全可以反对某种过宽的历史分类，同时又制造新的、具有政治后果的分类。

更麻烦的是，同一个 1906 年并不肯替我们整理出一条发展线。Lovejoy 可以在 Kant 文章里拆掉一个经不起局部检查的宏观分类，在 More review 里仍然回到 `Sutta Nipāta` 做一个很窄的 Nirvāṇa 解释，又在 `Primitive Philosophy` 中把大量地方材料重新聚成一个大类。这里没有“年轻时具体，后来越来越抽象”的舒服故事，也没有“他逐渐学会尊重语境”的进步叙事。细分和总括，是同时存在的可能性。

所以到了《The Great Chain of Being》，真正值得注意的并不是“佛教去了哪里”。它没有消失。Buddhism 和 Vedānta 仍然在书里。但位置变了：早年 notebook 中，它们是需要近距离解决的历史和文本问题；成熟著作中，它们更多成为一个主要由 Occidental/Western thought 组织起来的历史叙事中的比较项。我们可以描述这个变化，却没有必要把它写成背叛，也没有必要把成熟作品当作青年 notebook 的隐藏真义。

这时，最开始那个古怪的 bibliography 又回来了。Wilson 保存了一种 Lovejoy，Schaffer 保存了另一种；佛教学术继续保存 1898 年的技术论证，而 history of ideas 更容易保存 *Great Chain* 和那个后来被 canonized 的人物。没有谁简单地“忘记”了他。只是不同的学科把不同部分变成了中心。

于是那篇排在 bibliography 第一项的佛教论文，最后不再只是一个漂亮的 opening trick。它提醒我们：一段学术生涯不只被作者自己写过一次。Notebook 把材料排成一种样子，publication 又排成另一种样子，成熟著作再次改变它们的位置，而后来学科还会继续决定哪个 Lovejoy 最容易被看见。最初的问题看似只是两个佛教术语；走到最后，问题已经变成了：究竟是谁，有权替一段知识生活决定它的中心。

## English story version

The bibliography appended to *Essays in the History of Ideas* gives Arthur Lovejoy an unlikely beginning. Its first entry is an 1898 article on two Buddhist technical terms—not a precursor to the *Great Chain*, not a statement of the unit-idea programme, but a piece of specialist Buddhist scholarship. The impression is false: Lovejoy had published before 1898. Yet the false beginning is useful. Before we have learned anything about the young Lovejoy, a later bibliography has already arranged his career for us.

Nor was the Buddhist episode simply forgotten. Daniel Wilson knew about Lovejoy’s comparative-religion training, his Sanskrit and Buddhist studies, and his time in Paris. Simon Schaffer could still remark in 2010 that Lovejoy studied Pāli and Sanskrit there. The biographical fact survived. What survived less clearly was the work inside the fact. What, when Buddhism occupied his notebooks, was Lovejoy actually doing?

Notebook 004 answers that question first on an almost embarrassingly small scale. Lovejoy copies Henry Clarke Warren’s identification of `taṇhā` and `upādāna` and then stops: “But not so.” Warren himself had already argued that the links of dependent origination did not all express the same kind of dependence, so there is no discovery here of a general principle. Lovejoy’s disagreement is narrower. He refuses one answer, checks it against Buddhist classifications, and asks whether the evidence in front of him can really bear the claim Warren has made.

The notebook does not stay small for long. With Émile Senart, the problem expands from the relation between two terms to the historical composition of a Buddhist formula. Lovejoy can concede mixture, borrowing, and heterogeneous origins without conceding that these facts have already shown the resulting arrangement to be accidental or unintelligible. Others, including Oldenberg, had already challenged important parts of Senart’s reading. Lovejoy’s point is therefore not priority. It is a refusal to let evidence about where the parts came from settle, by itself, what the whole had become.

Publication preserved that refusal, but not the full disorder from which it emerged. In 1898 Lovejoy could write that terms, “perhaps even the ideas,” might have been borrowed while their “arrangement and application” remained original and characteristic. Much else receded: wider conjectures about Sāṃkhya and Buddhism, competing chronological possibilities, stronger speculative claims. The article did not simply polish the notebook. It reduced the size of some of its claims.

One manuscript page makes the problem unusually visible. `Viññāṇa` can fall within `nāma` as a matter of classification and precede `nāmarūpa` as a matter of sequence. Forced onto one axis, the two statements look contradictory. On the page, they are answers to different questions. Identity, sequence, causation, classification, origin: the notebook does not permit one of these to answer for the others. That is not yet a “method.” Its force comes partly from the fact that it remains a working page rather than a methodological declaration.

Only at this point does Harvard become explanatory. Lanman’s Sanskrit and Pāli teaching, Toy’s comparative study of Semitic religion, and Everett’s comparative religion placed close textual study and much broader comparison within the same graduate environment. They did not constitute a single transmitted programme. Harvard explains why the mixture visible in the notebook was institutionally possible; it does not explain it away.

Then the evidence changes. In a later notebook associated with Paris, the objects are no longer Pāli terms but death, ritual, the afterlife, and social practice. An apparent reward or punishment after death does not establish moral causation merely by looking like one; manner of death, ritual status, kinship, or group membership may provide different explanations. Marillier and Söderblom were already making many such distinctions. Again, there is no clean invention to assign to Lovejoy. What persists is the problem of inference: what does an observable difference actually warrant?

Lovejoy himself later carried that problem beyond Buddhist material. In 1902 he referred readers back to the 1898 article while allowing an affinity between Aristotle and Vedānta without accepting that affinity as proof of Oriental transmission. If Greek intellectual history could account for the Greek doctrine, resemblance did not have to become genealogy. This is not evidence that a Buddhist “method” had spread intact. It is evidence that Lovejoy could still object when one kind of relation was asked to do the work of another.

By 1906, however, the stakes had changed. Lovejoy was no longer classifying a Buddhist term or an ethnographic report but “Man” itself. Nineteenth-century thought, he argued, had divided generic humanity “longitudinally” by stages of culture and “cross-wise” by race or nationality. Historical differentiation had moved from the interpretation of texts to the classification of populations. And at that scale, finer distinctions did not produce caution alone: they entered arguments about colonial government, Black enfranchisement, and disfranchisement.

The same year refuses to supply a tidy developmental story. Lovejoy could dismantle an inherited classification of Kant when its particular claims failed to support it; he could still return to the *Sutta Nipāta* for a local argument about Nirvāṇa; and he could gather ethnographic reports into the broad category of “Primitive Philosophy.” He could resist an overlarge classification and make large classifications of his own. The tension is not a stage he had not yet resolved. It is part of the historical object.

By the time of *The Great Chain of Being*, Asian materials had not vanished, but their place had changed. Buddhism and Vedānta remain in the book, while its principal historical line is explicitly Occidental and Western. Materials that had once occupied the centre of a close textual and historical problem now more often serve as comparisons within another narrative. The mature book is therefore not the hidden meaning of the early notebooks. It is another arrangement of intellectual material.

Later scholarship performed another rearrangement. Wilson preserved the comparative-religion formation in biography; Schaffer preserved Pāli and Sanskrit as part of that formation while reconstructing Lovejoy’s historiography through other materials; Buddhist scholarship retained the technical 1898 intervention; intellectual history more readily retained the founder associated with the *Great Chain*. No single Lovejoy was simply lost. Different disciplines made different Lovejoys central.

The strange first entry in the later bibliography can now return with a different force. It is no longer merely an archival curiosity. Lovejoy’s materials were arranged once in notebooks, again in publication, again in mature synthesis, and again by the disciplines that inherited him. What began as a question about two Buddhist terms ends with a less comfortable question about intellectual history itself: who gets to decide which part of a scholarly life counts as its centre?

## Structural return to preserve in drafting

The opening bibliography must return at the end with a changed meaning.

Opening meaning:

> a misleading retrospective beginning.

Closing meaning:

> evidence that intellectual careers are repeatedly reordered by notebooks, publications, mature syntheses, bibliographies, and disciplinary memory.

Do not announce this as a named theory. Let the repeated act of arrangement become visible through the narrative itself.

## Tone / pacing control

Do not smooth the article into one voice. Preserve changes in register:

1. **Opening:** lightly ironic / curious.
2. **Warren:** small, concrete, almost anti-grandiose.
3. **Senart:** scale expands.
4. **1898 publication:** cooler, selective, disciplined.
5. **p.123:** quiet close reading / pause.
6. **Harvard:** contextual cooling; explicitly not an origin story.
7. **Paris:** scene change.
8. **1902:** brief conceptual clarity.
9. **1906 Democracy:** discomfort; political stakes become explicit.
10. **same-year 1906:** undo any newly tidy developmental narrative.
11. **Great Chain:** quieter mature comparison.
12. **Historiography:** return to the opening at a higher level.

The narrative should produce recognition through recurrence, not through repeated restatement of the thesis.
