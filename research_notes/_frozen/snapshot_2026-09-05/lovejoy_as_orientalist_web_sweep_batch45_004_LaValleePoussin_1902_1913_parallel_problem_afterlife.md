# Lovejoy as Orientalist / comparative religion — batch 45

Date: 2026-08-18  
Focus: test Louis de La Vallée Poussin’s 1902–03 Buddhist dogmatics and 1913 _Théorie des douze causes_ as **near-contemporary parallel / problem-afterlife controls** for the 004 → 1898 argument. The aim is not to claim influence in either direction. Current indexed searches have not produced a `Lovejoy` hit in these Poussin texts. The value is to determine which inferential problems remained live after 1898 and how another specialist distributed the evidentiary burden between textual history, systematic reconstruction and causal explanation.

## Core finding

La Vallée Poussin substantially strengthens the historiographical control around 004, but not by supplying a Lovejoy citation. His 1902–03 and 1913 texts show that the specialist field continued to wrestle with **exactly the tension our manuscript analysis has isolated**:

- early/canonical texts are historically uneven and not fully systematized;
- later scholastic syntheses may clarify, force, or distort them;
- apparently incompatible explanations of individual nidānas do not by themselves settle whether the chain can receive an intelligible synthesis;
- causal explanation requires distinguishing several kinds of function and several levels of analysis.

This means the paper should not present Lovejoy as the only scholar capable of holding **historical heterogeneity** and **systematic intelligibility** together. What remains distinctive to our archive is documentary: 004 lets us watch how Lovejoy arrived at his own version of that balancing act from named sources before publishing it in 1898.

## 1. Poussin 1902 begins with a problem Lovejoy knew well: karman without a self

Louis de La Vallée Poussin, “Dogmatique bouddhique. La négation de l’âme et la doctrine de l’acte,” _Journal asiatique_, 9e série, t.20 (1902), pp.237–306, opens by stressing the conflict between:

- the efficacy/fecundity of karman;
- the denial of any permanent self.

He explicitly frames the question: how can the Buddhist theory of moral action and fruit be reconciled with `nairātmya`? He credits Kern with important prior discussion and immediately situates Rhys Davids and Oldenberg as interpretive authorities.

This is useful because Lovejoy 1898’s Hardy-derived distinction between karma and `upādāna` is likewise an attempt to distribute causal work inside the rebirth problem. Poussin is not repeating Lovejoy’s solution, but the shared problem confirms that the causal allocation in 004 belongs to a live specialist difficulty rather than an isolated conceptual exercise.

## 2. Poussin 1902 explicitly calibrates how much system should be demanded from the Tipiṭaka

The most important parallel appears at printed p.253.

Poussin says, in substance, that one should **not demand of the Tipiṭaka the clarity and finished systematization** found in the Sanskrit schools, Vaibhāṣikas or Mādhyamikas. Yet he immediately adds that the germ of the later learned dogmatic theory can nevertheless be established in the early texts.

This is extremely close to the historiographical tension around 004 p.49:

- historical/canonical material is not a finished homogeneous system;
- lack of finished systematization does not mean absence of recoverable conceptual organization.

The similarity should be treated as a **parallel problem-form**, not evidence of direct transmission. Poussin 1902 currently yields no indexed `Lovejoy` citation.

## 3. Poussin 1903 makes methodological caution explicit when causal theory becomes opaque

In the continuation, “Nouvelles recherches sur la doctrine de l’acte,” _Journal asiatique_, 10e série, t.2 (1903), Poussin says Buddhist doctors themselves recognize “mysteries” in the operation/course of action and the incomprehensibility of the `pratītyasamutpāda`.

His response is methodological: once these uncertainties are acknowledged, **the terrain is cleared and the method of exegesis becomes safer**. He then itemizes the actual causal questions:

- by what mechanism an act leaves traces in the intellectual series;
- how it ripens in the same or another life;
- under what conditions an act becomes sterile;
- how the series is simplified and finally interrupted.

This is another useful control on our Lovejoy thesis. A serious Buddhist dogmatics after 1898 does not choose between “the chain is coherent” and “the chain is incomprehensible” in one move. It can acknowledge obscurity while still decomposing the causal problem into distinct mechanisms and functions.

That is precisely why our Lovejoy claim should remain at the level of **evidentiary allocation**, not a heroic “restoration of coherence.”

## 4. Poussin’s 1903 series-analysis gives a mature parallel to causal-role decomposition

Poussin’s 1903 discussion treats personal continuity as a series (`vijñānasaṃtāna`) and asks how successive phenomena are causally necessitated without a permanent agent. He distinguishes the causal sequence from a substantial self and, in discussing internal dependent origination, separates the moments/elements of the series from the conditions that enable its development.

Again, this is not Lovejoy’s exact `karma / upādāna` allocation. But it shows that by 1902–03 the field’s dogmatic analysis increasingly worked by:

- decomposing a causal chain;
- assigning different causal/explanatory jobs to different components;
- distinguishing the continuity of a configuration from identity of a permanent bearer.

This parallel reduces the value of any claim that functional decomposition itself is a Lovejoyian innovation. The archive’s value is the recoverable source transformation that leads *his* argument there.

## 5. Poussin 1913 returns directly to the whole-chain synthesis problem

The strongest later problem-afterlife is _Bouddhisme, études et matériaux: Théorie des douze causes_ (1913).

In § III, “Interprétations de la liste considérée dans son ensemble,” Poussin says the detailed explanations of the individual members may be ingenious and well-founded while becoming **incompatible when the chain is examined as a whole**. He then says that one must review theories attempting to gather them into an **organic synthesis**.

Crucially, he refuses to settle the historical question by fiat. Nothing, he says, prevents us from supposing that the first redactors knew approximately what they meant, or that later theologians merely extracted an older doctrine obscured by exegesis; **he will not take a position on that question at this point**.

This is almost a textbook version of the distinction the 004 route has forced us to make:

- the historical genesis/stratification of the list;
- the possibility of a systematic synthesis of its present form;

are related but non-identical questions.

Poussin does not need to decide the first before examining the second.

## 6. 1913 gives an especially useful control on the three-life interpretation

Poussin’s § III describes the scholastic three-life interpretation as a dominant system:

- previous life: `avidyā`, `saṃskāras`;
- current life: the middle causal complex;
- future life: birth and ageing/death.

He treats this not merely as chronology but as one **systematic distribution** of causal categories (kleśa, karman, vipāka / birth-suffering) across the chain. He then surveys other syntheses, including interpretations involving the intermediate being and later Abhidharma models.

This is a useful later control for Lovejoy’s 1898 claim that the formula is not simply a strict homogeneous temporal succession. The field after Lovejoy continued to distinguish:

- chronological allocation;
- causal role;
- doctrinal category;
- explanatory model of the whole.

The categories should therefore not be collapsed in our description of 004.

## 7. Poussin 1913 explicitly reuses Senart, not Lovejoy, in indexed passages

The 1913 text directly cites Senart’s _Mélanges Harlez_ paper on difficult terminology and on the wheel/chain problem. For example, its opening chapter sends the reader to Senart p.293 for a difficult term; § III cites Senart p.289 in discussing the wheel (`cakra`) and the tripartite/cyclical representation of dependent origination.

Current searches of the Wikisource-indexed 1913 text for `Lovejoy` have not produced a hit. This is **not proof of absence** from every footnote or edition, but it is a useful reception signal: Poussin’s explicit genealogical conversation is visibly with Senart, Warren, Oldenberg, Kern and later scholastic sources, while Lovejoy is not yet recovered from the indexed text.

That distinction matters for reception history. The **problem** clearly survives; direct uptake of Lovejoy remains unlocated.

## 8. This changes the reception question

Batch 44 asked whether we could find direct early citations to Lovejoy 1898. Batch 45 suggests a second, more productive question:

> **Even when Lovejoy is not cited, does the exact problem he intervened in remain structurally visible in later specialist work?**

For La Vallée Poussin the answer is clearly yes.

The problem-afterlife includes:

- how much systematicity can be attributed to early texts;
- how to reconcile incompatible explanations of individual links with an overall synthesis;
- how to distribute causal work among karma, passion, consciousness and rebirth;
- how to separate historical origin from the explanatory form adopted by later dogmatics.

This does not establish Lovejoy’s influence. It establishes that his 1898 paper is legible as one intervention in a problem that remained central to Buddhist dogmatics.

## 9. Stronger historiographical formulation after Poussin control

The Lovejoy paper should now avoid two symmetrical exaggerations.

### Too strong

> Lovejoy invented the practice of reconstructing a coherent causal system from heterogeneous Buddhist materials.

Poussin, Oldenberg and others make that indefensible.

### Too weak

> Lovejoy merely repeated contemporary Indology and therefore tells us nothing about his later historical method.

004 disproves that simplification because it preserves the **transformational steps** from named readings to Lovejoy’s own published allocations.

### Better

> The late-1890s/early-1900s Buddhist field already supplied Lovejoy with a repertoire of decomposition, genealogy, textual stratification and system reconstruction. Notebook 004 matters because it records how he learned to police the boundaries between those operations: historical derivation did not determine semantic identity, compositeness did not determine intelligibility, and a causal term’s inherited provenance did not determine the explanatory job it performed in a later configuration. Poussin’s subsequent dogmatics shows that these were durable specialist problems, while Lovejoy’s 1902 article shows him redeploying an analogous division of evidentiary burdens outside technical Buddhist philology.

This is now the safest paper-level argument.
