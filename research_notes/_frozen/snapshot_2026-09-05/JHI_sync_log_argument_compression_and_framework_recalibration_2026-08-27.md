## 5. Relation to the editor's unusually dense brief

The editor asked a short Blog first draft to do several things simultaneously:

1. reconstruct what Lovejoy read;
2. explain how materials/arguments reached him;
3. show how he reconfigured them in the published Buddhist article;
4. provide enough Harvard/Lanman/philosophy/Oriental-philology context;
5. return briefly to *Great Chain* or related later writing;
6. state a disciplinary consequence;
7. avoid a full institutional history and avoid causal overclaim;
8. remain roughly 1,600–2,000 words, with a hard maximum of 2,400.

This is a high evidentiary and compositional expectation. The drafting response should not be to answer every possible window equally. It should show that the windows are controlled while keeping only a few open in the prose.

The first draft should therefore aim to make the editor see that the larger questions were pursued and delimited, not to reproduce the entire research process.

## 7. Working thesis hierarchy

### A. First-order historical thesis

The notebooks show Lovejoy repeatedly asking what particular textual and historical evidence could establish about identity, sequence, causation, borrowing and the relation of parts within a larger arrangement.

### B. Publication thesis

The 1898 article does not transparently reproduce the notebook. It selects and narrows claims, especially around technical usage, borrowing and the limits of etymological or genealogical inference.

### C. Later-text thesis

Later texts show Lovejoy handling comparison and historical classification at larger scales, but resemblance to the notebook is not evidence of a continuous method or causal development.

### D. Historiographical thesis

Lovejoy's Buddhist/Indic formation remained visible in biography and later historiography, but its technical scholarly practice was rarely integrated into the histories told about Lovejoy as a historian of ideas.

## 8. Publication-facing governing sentences

Preferred two-sentence version:

> **This essay does not add a forgotten Buddhist episode to Lovejoy's biography; it changes the status of a known one. His notebooks show Buddhist materials as a site where the young Lovejoy repeatedly tested what textual and historical evidence could establish about identity, sequence, causation, borrowing, and arrangement; reading those notes beside the 1898 article and later writings shows what entered print, what was narrowed or left behind, and why later histories of Lovejoy made other parts of his career more central.**

More compact one-sentence version:

> **Lovejoy's early Buddhist scholarship was not forgotten, but his notebooks reveal it as something more than a biographical episode: a place where he repeatedly tested what evidence could establish about identity, sequence, causation, borrowing, and arrangement, before those judgments were selectively carried into print and later remembered under other accounts of his career.**

Safest short thesis for an early paragraph:

> **The question is not whether Lovejoy studied Buddhism, but what kind of historical work he did when he did so.**

Do not use all three in the published piece. They are alternatives for draft calibration.
