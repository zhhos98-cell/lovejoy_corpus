# How the Lovejoy -> Skinner rupture became memorable: anthology selection, field labels, and retrospective compression, 1985–2014

Date: 2026-08-31
Status: **CANON-FORMATION MECHANISM IDENTIFIED / DIRECT 1985 POCOCK TRANSCRIPTION + 1988 ANTHOLOGY CONTENT CONTROL / INTENTIONAL ERASURE NOT CLAIMED**

## Core result

Round 2 recovered a reciprocal methodological exchange:

`WIENER 1961 -> SKINNER 1969 -> PAREKH/BERKI JHI 1973 -> SKINNER 1974 -> BERKI/PAREKH 1976`.

The next question is why later historiography so often remembers something much cleaner:

`LOVEJOY / HISTORY OF IDEAS`
-> `SKINNER / CAMBRIDGE CONTEXTUALISM`.

A first concrete canon-formation mechanism is now visible.

James Tully's 1988 anthology *Meaning and Context: Quentin Skinner and His Critics* became a portable canonical package of Skinner's methodological writings and selected criticism. It **reprinted Skinner's 1974 `Some Problems in the Analysis of Political Thought and Action`**, the article in which Skinner repeatedly responds to Parekh and Berki, but **did not reprint Parekh and Berki's 1973 JHI critique or their 1976 rejoinder**. Its selected critics were a different group.

This produces a selection asymmetry:

`RESPONSE ENTERS CANONICAL ANTHOLOGY`
while
`ONE IMPORTANT PROVOCATION / COUNTER-BRANCH DOES NOT`.

No evidence currently shows this omission was intentional suppression. The historical claim is narrower:

> **Anthology selection changed the accessibility and visibility of the debate. A reader encountering the canonical 1988 package could read Skinner's 1974 response without receiving the Parekh/Berki article to which substantial portions of that response were addressed. This is one plausible material mechanism by which a reciprocal controversy became retrospectively centered on Skinner's evolving corpus rather than on a multi-venue argument ecology.**

A second correction comes from J. G. A. Pocock around 1985. His methodological self-description differentiates his `history of political discourse` from Lovejoyian history of ideas, but he explicitly allows that Lovejoy-scale history can be legitimate **when the historical evidence actually contains durable `great traditions` capable of coordinating intellectual activity across long periods**. This is not a simple repudiation of longue-durée history of ideas.

Thus even within the canonical Cambridge orbit:

`DISCIPLINARY SELF-DIFFERENTIATION`
!=
`TOTAL METHODOLOGICAL IMPOSSIBILITY OF LOVEJOYIAN OBJECTS`.

## I. Tully 1988 as a canonizing publication technology

Direct bibliographic controls for *Meaning and Context* describe the volume as assembling:

- five of Skinner's most widely discussed methodological articles;
- seven articles by critics;
- a new fifty-seven-page `Reply to My Critics` by Skinner;
- an editorial introduction giving a systematic overview of the evolution of Skinner's work and reactions to it.

This is more than a bibliography. It is a **portable debate architecture**: scattered journal interventions are selected, ordered, republished and made teachable as a coherent methodological controversy.

The visible table of contents includes Skinner's:

- `Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas`;
- `Motives, Intentions and the Interpretation of Texts`;
- `Social Meaning and the Explanation of Social Action`;
- `Some Problems in the Analysis of Political Thought and Action`;
- `Language and Social Change`.

The critical section includes contributions by:

- Martin Hollis;
- Keith Graham;
- Joseph V. Femia;
- Nathan Tarcov;
- Kenneth Minogue;
- John Keane;
- Charles Taylor.

Then comes Skinner's `A Reply to My Critics`.

Crucial omission for the recovered JHI line:

- Bhikhu Parekh and R. N. Berki, JHI 1973 — not included;
- R. N. Berki and Bhiku Parekh, *Political Theory* 1976 communication — not included.

Yet Skinner's 1974 article, which directly engages their objections, **is** included.

This asymmetry should be treated as a historical fact about canon construction, not as evidence of motive.

## II. Why selection asymmetry matters methodologically

A reprint anthology alters the ecology of citation and teaching.

Before 1988, the relevant argument was distributed across:

`HISTORY AND THEORY 1969`
+
`JHI 1973`
+
`POLITICAL THEORY 1974`
+
`POLITICAL THEORY 1976`
+
other methodological venues.

After the anthology, a new object becomes available:

`SKINNER + SELECTED CRITICS IN ONE VOLUME`.

The selection itself implicitly defines which criticisms count as the canonical challenges that Skinner's later reply must answer.

The historical mechanism can be stated cautiously:

`DISTRIBUTED CONTROVERSY`
-> `SELECTED ANTHOLOGY`
-> `PORTABLE CANON`
-> `DIFFERENTIAL VISIBILITY OF BRANCHES`.

This is directly analogous to the wider project's distinction between:

`PRACTICE`
!= `INFRASTRUCTURE`
!= `CANON`.

Here the **anthology is canon infrastructure**.

## III. Parekh/Berki are absent from the anthology's critic roster but present inside the reprinted Skinner response

This is the particularly interesting structural feature.

Skinner 1974 directly:

- says Parekh/Berki return to the claim that some texts can be intelligible without context;
- answers their criticism of one definite intention for a complex work;
- answers their claim that he treats recovery of intention as sufficient;
- quotes their claim that some contexts are negligible and audiences transhistorical;
- disputes their use of *Leviathan* as a text making sense on its own;
- criticizes their failure to engage his empirical Hobbes evidence;
- discusses their preference for traditions / Greenleaf-type histories;
- answers their charge concerning political ideas and action.

Because the 1974 essay is reprinted while the 1973 critique is not, the anthology preserves **their presence as objects in Skinner's argumentative development** while reducing direct access to them as an autonomous methodological position.

Again, do not call this intentional silencing without editorial correspondence.

Better phrase:

> **The 1988 canon preserves Parekh and Berki intertextually inside Skinner's response rather than symmetrically as a reprinted counter-text.**

That is a precise material fact with potentially large historiographic consequences.

## IV. Pocock c.1985: self-differentiation without absolute prohibition

A direct transcription in the St Andrews Intellectual History Archive preserves J. G. A. Pocock's `What is Intellectual History?`, c.1985.

Pocock says he now calls himself a historian of **political discourse**, rather than necessarily a historian of ideas or an intellectual historian. This marks a real field-label differentiation from older `history of ideas`.

But his account of Lovejoy is much more conditional than a clean rupture narrative suggests.

He describes a Lovejoyian `history of ideas` as possible when dominant thought-patterns:

- exercise authority across multiple practical/theoretical activities;
- display persistence or coordinated change over long periods;
- are sustained by highly authoritative and comprehensive textual `great traditions`.

His examples include:

- Aristotle's *Organon*;
- the Confucian Six Classics.

He explicitly says there seems **no reason in principle** why cultural conditions conducive to ideas on this scale should not exist.

Therefore Pocock's position can be typed:

`DO NOT ASSUME LONG-RANGE IDEA CONTINUITY AS METHOD`

but

`ALLOW LONG-RANGE IDEA / GREAT-TRADITION CONTINUITY WHEN IT IS AN EMPIRICAL PROPERTY OF THE HISTORICAL SITUATION`.

This is remarkably close to the larger project's governing proof principle:

`ANALYTICAL POSSIBILITY`
requires
`RELATION-SPECIFIC EMPIRICAL WARRANT`.

Do not call Pocock Lovejoyian. The point is that the Cambridge-side methodological boundary is itself porous and conditional.

## V. The 1985 field moment is therefore more plural than `new intellectual history replaces history of ideas`

Pocock's c.1985 text appears in the same broader moment as the History Today 1985 discussion `What is Intellectual History?` and the emergence of Cambridge's `Ideas in Context` publishing programme.

His recommended/referenced recent works include not only contextualist projects but also:

- Preston King, ed., *The History of Ideas* (1984);
- Dominick LaCapra, *Rethinking Intellectual History* (1984);
- Francis Oakley, *Omnipotence, Covenant and Order: Essays in the History of Ideas from Abelard to Leibniz* (1985).

This suggests that `history of ideas` had not simply disappeared as an operative label/object by the mid-1980s even while discourse/context approaches were consolidating.

The historical field was being **repartitioned**, not cleanly replaced.

---

## VI. By later retrospective narration, however, a clean succession becomes easy to state

Darrin McMahon's 2013/14 `The Return of the History of Ideas?` opens from a retrospective diagnosis that by around 1980 `history of ideas` was already passé and identifies Skinner and Robert Darnton as two major forces in its discrediting.

This is valuable not because it should be accepted as a transparent description of 1980, but because it documents a later stabilized historiographic memory:

`LOVEJOYIAN HISTORY OF IDEAS`
-> `DISCREDIT / DEMISE`
-> later `RETURN`.

Daniel Wickberg's 2014 article and more recent apologias similarly operate against a background in which Lovejoy has been remembered principally through familiar criticisms of decontextualized unit-ideas and trans-temporal continuity.

The emerging research object is therefore **the production of the demise narrative itself**.

Potential chronology:

1. 1960s–70s: live methodological disagreement with multiple internal and external branches;
2. 1970s: Skinner polemics become especially visible and portable;
3. mid-1980s: field labels stabilize around discourse/context/intellectual history, while Pocock still admits conditional Lovejoyian objects;
4. 1988: *Meaning and Context* packages Skinner + selected critics, preserving Skinner 1974 but excluding Parekh/Berki's originating counter-text;
5. 1990s: Cambridge School / contextualism increasingly narrated as a recognizable methodological formation;
6. 2000s–2010s: Lovejoy becomes available as an object of `revisit`, `return`, or rehabilitation precisely because a demise/rupture story has stabilized.

This chronology is a working hypothesis. Each step needs direct citation/curriculum/review/citation-network control.

## VII. This is a canon-formation problem, not merely a historiography-of-method problem

The project's existing architecture can now be extended:

`PROGRAMME`
!= `PRACTICE`
!= `INFRASTRUCTURE`
!= `CANON`
!= `RETROSPECTIVE MEMORY`.

A method may be plural in practice but compressed in memory.

A controversy may be reciprocal in real time but linearized by later canon infrastructure.

An anthology, textbook, course syllabus, collected essays volume or disciplinary self-history can perform this compression without any need for conscious falsification.

New useful term:

### `CANONICAL COMPRESSION`

The process by which a distributed, reciprocal and internally heterogeneous controversy becomes portable as a smaller set of exemplary figures, texts and oppositions.

### `SELECTION ASYMMETRY`

When one side's response is canonically reprinted while a historically important provocation or rejoinder is not, altering later readers' access to the structure of the original debate.

These are institutional mechanisms, not accusations of motive.

## IX. Article-level payoff

This line may supply the closing section of the emerging JHI-scale paper.

Instead of ending:

`Skinner criticized Lovejoy and intellectual history moved on`,

end with:

> **The most consequential transformation may have occurred not only in method but in disciplinary memory. Midcentury history of ideas contained repeated internal disputes over continuity, transmission, cultural process and influence; Skinner acknowledged one of these prior formulations, was criticized in JHI, answered that criticism, and was answered again. Yet later canon infrastructure increasingly made the story portable as a contrast between Lovejoyian history of ideas and Cambridge contextualism. The history of methodology is therefore also a history of how methodological controversies are edited, anthologized, named and forgotten.**

That is a much stronger HPS/history-of-knowledge problem than adjudicating whose method was correct.
