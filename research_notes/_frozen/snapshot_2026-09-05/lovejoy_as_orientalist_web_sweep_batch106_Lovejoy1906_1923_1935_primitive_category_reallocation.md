# Batch 106 — Back to Lovejoy: from `primitive philosophy` to `primitivism`, and from reconstructing the actor's category to policing the historian's category

Date: 2026-08-19
Status: synced
Scope: deliberately suspend the downstream Durkheim/Hardy reception line and return to Lovejoy's own primary texts. Compare the 1906 *Monist* paper with Lovejoy's 1923 Rousseau essay and the Lovejoy-authored methodological portions of *Primitivism and Related Ideas in Antiquity* (1935), then run a bounded negative lexical audit across the currently ingested later Lovejoy corpus.

## Core result

The strongest continuity from the 1906 comparative-religion paper into Lovejoy's later history-of-ideas work is **not** continuity of the substantive `primitive energetics` theory. Within the later Lovejoy texts currently ingested in the clean corpus, the distinctive 1906 vocabulary (`mana`, `manitou`, `wakonda`, `orenda`, `primitive energetics`, `manitouism`, Codrington, Marett, etc.) does not recur substantively. The title of the 1906 paper survives in the bibliography appended to *Essays in the History of Ideas*, but the theory's characteristic lexicon is otherwise absent from the sampled later canonical works.

What does recur—and becomes much more explicit—is an **operator of discrimination**:

> surface label / received synthesis → detect conflation → split into distinct senses or propositions → reassign evidence → reconstruct a narrower historical claim.

The object to which this operator is applied changes significantly.

In 1906 Lovejoy uses it to recover what he presents as a category of `primitive philosophy` beneath missionaries' and ethnographers' mistranslations. The analyst's abstraction is ultimately **re-addressed to the indigenous actor**: `manitou`, `wakonda`, `mana`, etc. are treated as evidence that `savage thought` itself contains an impersonal, communicable, quasi-mechanical causal category.

In 1923 and still more explicitly in 1935, the same anti-conflation operation is increasingly directed **at the historian's own vocabulary**. `State of nature` is split into chronological, juristic and cultural senses; `primitivism` is then formally defined as an historian's name for distinct tendencies that must not be fused. By 1935 the category-maker is overtly the historian, and the first methodological task is to discriminate the analyst's units before tracing their histories.

The best formulation is therefore:

> **the operator survives while the epistemic address changes.**

Or more concretely:

> **Lovejoy moves, in this sampled trajectory, from reconstructing the other's hidden category to policing the historian's category.**

This is not a claim that the mature `unit-idea` method already existed in finished form in 1906. It is a claim about continuity of an analytical operation combined with a change in where Lovejoy locates the burden of conceptual discrimination.

---

## 1. 1906: the analytical category is reconstructed and then attributed to `primitive philosophy`

The direct 1906 primary text has already been controlled in Batches 12 and 77–78.

The opening move is an attack on an inherited master-description: primitive religion has been treated as fundamentally anthropomorphic, a traffic between human persons and supernatural persons. Lovejoy does not simply add another ethnographic theme. He argues that the apparently heterogeneous practices possess a recoverable `logic`, and he posits a `category more fundamental than personality`: an impersonal and quasi-mechanical conception of causation.

His source procedure is already highly discriminating:

- observer's custom report can be retained while observer's theological/personifying gloss is rejected;
- lexical occurrence can be accepted while translation is corrected;
- grammatical morphology and native causal explanation can license stronger propositions than superficial observer vocabulary;
- independent cross-regional convergence licenses a minimal common core, while richer North-American evidence is used for stronger mechanism claims.

But the destination of the analysis is important. Lovejoy does not present `primitive energetics` merely as a convenient modern comparative type. He treats the evidence as recovering a conception genuinely present in primitive thought: diffused and communicable efficacy, localization and transfer, manipulation, and ultimately operation according to `quite regular and intelligible laws`.

Thus the 1906 alignment is approximately:

`analyst decomposes ethnographic reports`
→ `analyst reconstructs abstract causal category`
→ `category is attributed to primitive/native thought`.

The historian/analyst owns the reconstruction procedure, but the **epistemic address of the reconstructed abstraction is actor-level**.

That is precisely the feature later critics such as Jung would contest; here, however, the point is internal to Lovejoy's own trajectory.

## 2. 1923 Rousseau: the first object of correction is now the historian's category

Lovejoy's `The Supposed Primitivism of Rousseau's "Discourse on Inequality"` appeared in *Modern Philology* 21.2 (November 1923), pp.165–186. The text is also preserved in Lovejoy's later *Essays in the History of Ideas*.

Its first sentence identifies a persistent **historical error**: the belief that the *Discourse on Inequality* is essentially a glorification of the state of nature and chiefly promotes `primitivism`.

Lovejoy then says directly that the confusion arises partly from failure to notice **ambiguity in the terms employed**. He divides `state of nature` into at least three distinguishable senses:

1. chronological — the primeval condition of man, whatever its characteristics;
2. juristic — the condition of persons or groups not subject to common governmental authority;
3. cultural — the condition in which arts, sciences and the non-political elements of civilization have made least progress.

The critical sentence is not merely taxonomic: these senses are **not necessarily identical in denotation**. The same expression can therefore generate a false historical synthesis if the historian slides between its meanings.

Lovejoy's reconstruction of Rousseau then multiplies the distinctions. Rousseau's pre-political period is divided into four long cultural stages; Rousseau's own `state of nature` normally denotes only the first; that first stage is not the ideal stage; the third is the stage whose loss Rousseau regrets; and the final pre-political stage culminates in conflict. Lovejoy then separates source lineages: Pufendorf/Voltaire, Montaigne/Pope and Hobbes supply different historical models that are combined in Rousseau's narrative.

The operator is strikingly familiar from 1906:

`received label`
→ `detect semantic conflation`
→ `separate evidence-bearing senses`
→ `reconstruct the actual proposition bundle`.

But the target has shifted. The principal error is no longer a missionary's translation of `mana` or `wakonda`; it is a **modern historian's use of `state of nature` and `primitivism`**.

The 1923 alignment is therefore:

`historian's broad label`
→ `Lovejoy decomposes the label`
→ `historical actor/text is reconstructed only after the analyst's vocabulary has been disciplined`.

This is an important change in epistemic address. The category is no longer simply recovered from an alien actor and then universalized. The historian's own terms become an evidentiary hazard.

## 3. 1935: `primitivism` is explicitly an analyst-owned name for distinct tendencies

The opening of *Primitivism and Related Ideas in Antiquity* makes the shift explicit.

The book states that `Primitivism`, **as the term has come to be used by historical and philosophical writers**, is a name for **two distinct tendencies** in human thought. Though they became fused and interpenetrated historically, Lovejoy and Boas say that clear understanding requires beginning by distinguishing them: `chronological primitivism` and `cultural primitivism`.

The two are then defined as answers to different questions. Chronological primitivism concerns the temporal distribution of value: at what point in the world's or mankind's history is the best condition located? Cultural primitivism concerns the comparative valuation of modes of life associated with less complex or `primitive` social conditions.

Lovejoy's chiefly authored Chapter I then does something methodologically decisive: it does not accept historical vocabulary as already supplying adequate units. It constructs a classified space of logically possible positions—finitist/infinitist, decline/ascent/undulation, multiple variants within each—while repeatedly warning that historically current beliefs often combine these possibilities incongruously and cannot always be classified with confidence.

This is no longer an attempt to prove that one abstract category is silently possessed by a generalized `primitive mind`. It is an overt **analytical taxonomy whose ownership belongs to the historians**.

The opening formula is particularly revealing:

`primitivism = a name used by historical/philosophical writers`
→ `name covers distinct tendencies`
→ `distinction is necessary for clear historical work`.

The address has changed from:

`what category did primitive thought possess?`

to:

`what distinct analytical categories must the historian construct so that unlike historical phenomena are not confused?`

## 4. 1935 arrangement rule: category discrimination becomes documentary method

The same volume contains an unusually explicit statement of the mature arrangement principle.

It says that the history of ideas should exhibit the fortunes of distinct `unit-ideas` and their relations of congruity or opposition, rather than preserve whole philosophical `systems`, because systems conjoin heterogeneous notions and arguments. Therefore the first essentials are:

- analysis and `anatomizing` of texts;
- separating out passages pertinent to the several ideas under investigation;
- bringing passages illustrating a given unit and its vicissitudes together as a separate part of the history.

The physical/documentary consequence is important: passages from one text may be separated into different chapters; writings from the same period may appear far apart because arrangement follows the discriminated idea rather than source integrity or simple chronology.

This is the mature form of an operation already visible in the 1906 source audit, where one witness is decomposed by proposition and evidentiary competence. But the two should not be collapsed.

### 1906

`source document`
→ `custom / lexical item / translation / ontology / causal inference`
→ different reliability verdicts.

### 1935

`historical text or system`
→ `distinct idea-units / passages`
→ rearranged documentary history.

The family resemblance is strong: **whole documents and whole labels are distrusted as natural evidentiary units**.

The difference is equally important: 1935 makes the operation self-conscious, methodological and historian-owned.

## 5. A bounded negative result: the 1906 substantive lexicon largely disappears from the ingested later Lovejoy corpus

A word-boundary audit was run against the currently ingested later Lovejoy corpus:

- *Essays in the History of Ideas*;
- *Primitivism and Related Ideas in Antiquity*;
- *The Great Chain of Being*;
- *The Revolt Against Dualism*.

Search terms:

`mana`, `manitou`, `wakonda`, `wakan`, `orenda`, `primitive energetics`, `manitouism`, `Codrington`, `Marett`, `McGee`, `Hetherwick`, `primitive philosophy`, `quasi-mechanical`, `Fletcher`.

### Result

No substantive recurrence was found for:

- `mana`
- `manitou`
- `wakonda`
- `wakan`
- `orenda`
- `primitive energetics`
- `manitouism`
- `Codrington`
- `Marett`
- `McGee`
- `Hetherwick`.

`primitive philosophy` occurs once in *Essays*, but only in the appended bibliography entry for the 1906 article itself.

`Fletcher` occurs in *Essays*, but the occurrences refer to Giles Fletcher and Harris F. Fletcher, not Alice C. Fletcher.

`quasi-mechanical` does recur, but in unrelated later philosophical contexts:

- *Essays*: empirical association of ideas in discussion of Coleridge/Kant;
- *The Great Chain of Being*: Leibnizian volition and the emergence of actuality from possibles;
- *The Revolt Against Dualism*: scientific reduction and physical explanation.

So `quasi-mechanical` is not evidence that the 1906 `primitive energetics` packet survived. It is a generic member of Lovejoy's philosophical vocabulary with multiple applications.

### Claim ceiling

This is a **corpus-bounded negative finding**, not a claim that Lovejoy never returned to `mana` or primitive energetics anywhere after 1906. The currently ingested later corpus is substantial but not a complete edition of Lovejoy's oeuvre, and the unresolved 1914 Leuba review is already known from secondary bibliographic control to refer back explicitly to the 1906 paper.

The safe result is:

> **within the sampled later canonical corpus, the distinctive 1906 ethnological lexicon becomes a lexical cul-de-sac, while the operation of conceptual discrimination remains highly visible.**

## 6. The 1914 Leuba review is now the critical internal bridge, but remains a primary-text hold

The Lovejoy-authored review of James H. Leuba, *A Psychological Study of Religion*, is bibliographically secure:

Arthur O. Lovejoy, review, *International Journal of Ethics* 24.2 (January 1914), pp.216–220.

Daniel J. Wilson's annotated bibliography says Lovejoy attempts there to correct a misconception by Leuba concerning Lovejoy's own position in the 1906 `Fundamental Concept` paper.

Leuba's 1912 primary text is directly controlled and makes the likely locus of dispute clear: he accepts Lovejoy's evidence for nonpersonal power but rejects Lovejoy's characterization of its operation as mechanical/regular, preferring `magical force` and then `dynamism`.

However, the Lovejoy 1914 review itself has **not yet been recovered at proposition level** in the current environment. The exact content of Lovejoy's correction therefore remains HOLD.

This source is unusually valuable now because it could show whether Lovejoy himself, only eight years after 1906, had already begun to qualify the epistemic or causal strength of `primitive energetics`.

Until that text is recovered, do not infer the correction from Leuba's criticism alone.

## 7. Revised internal genealogy

The direct Lovejoy-side sequence can now be coded without relying on downstream reception:

### 1906 — reconstructive category attribution

`observer reports / native words / grammar / rites`
→ decompose source statements
→ recover `impersonal quasi-mechanical energy`
→ attribute the abstraction to `primitive philosophy`.

### 1923 — historiographic semantic discrimination

`received claim: Rousseau = primitivist / state of nature = ideal`
→ split ambiguous historian's terms
→ separate stages and propositions
→ reconstruct Rousseau's actual position.

### 1935 — explicit analyst taxonomy + documentary decomposition

`primitivism` as historian's broad name
→ split chronological/cultural tendencies and subtypes
→ anatomize texts into idea-bearing passages
→ rearrange evidence by discriminated unit rather than by whole system.

Thus the most defensible continuity is not:

`primitive energetics → unit-ideas`.

It is:

> **anti-conflation → proposition/sense discrimination → selective evidentiary reassignment → reconstructed historical object.**

And the strongest change is:

> **the burden of abstraction moves increasingly onto the analyst's side of the relation.**

## 8. Historiographic payoff

This reframes the early comparative-religion material in a way that avoids two opposite errors.

First, it avoids treating the 1906 paper as a forgotten juvenile curiosity simply because its ethnological vocabulary later disappears. The paper already contains a recognizably Lovejoyan distrust of whole-source authority, surface terminology and undifferentiated theories.

Second, it avoids treating 1906 as if the mature history-of-ideas method were already fully present. A major difference remains. In 1906 Lovejoy is willing to make a very strong move from heterogeneous ethnographic evidence to a generalized actor-level `primitive philosophy`. By 1923–35 the explicit methodological anxiety is increasingly about the historian's own tendency to fuse meanings and to impose an undifferentiated label on heterogeneous historical propositions.

The resulting developmental hypothesis is therefore:

> **Lovejoy's durable inheritance from comparative religion may lie less in the primitive-energy doctrine than in a learned suspicion of composite categories; later history of ideas turns that suspicion back upon the historian's own conceptual instruments.**

This is a hypothesis about Lovejoy's own methodological development and can now be tested against additional intermediate primary texts rather than against downstream anthropological reception.
