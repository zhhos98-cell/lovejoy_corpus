# Lovejoy as Orientalist — web sweep batch 07: 1935 and distributed Orientalist expertise

Date: 2026-08-18

## Primary evidence from the book

The repository’s full-text corpus of Arthur O. Lovejoy and George Boas, *Primitivism and Related Ideas in Antiquity* (1935), makes the organization of non-Greek/non-Roman expertise unusually explicit.

In the Preface the editors state that they are **“deeply indebted”** to Johns Hopkins colleagues, **especially Professors Albright and Dumont**, for “valuable Supplementary Essays” which **“extend the perspective of the history of primitivism into two important regions in which it has not hitherto received special study.”** The table of contents then places two supplementary essays after the Greek/Roman core:

1. W. F. Albright, **“Primitivism in Ancient Western Asia (Mesopotamia and Israel)”**, pp. 421 ff.
2. P.-E. Dumont, **“Primitivism in Indian Literature”**, pp. 433 ff.

This is direct primary evidence for what we have been calling **distributed expertise**. The editors did not simply assimilate Indian and ancient Near-Eastern textual material into Lovejoy’s own voice. They assigned those regions to named specialists and made the division of competence visible in the architecture of the book.

## Why this matters for “Lovejoy as Orientalist”

The contrast with 1898 is sharp:

- **1898:** Lovejoy himself performs technical Buddhist philology, working through Pāli terminology, editions, dictionaries and competing Indological interpretations.
- **1933:** in “The Chinese Origin of a Romanticism,” he mainly reconstructs the European documentary representation of China.
- **1935:** the project is globalized by **collaboration with area specialists** when direct command of the relevant language/textual archive is required.

The mature form of Orientalist engagement is therefore not simple continuity of youthful philology. It is a change in the *organization of scholarship*: Lovejoy becomes an editor/theorist who defines historical units and questions, while specialist colleagues supply regionally and linguistically grounded extensions.

## Albright’s essay: specialist method remains visibly distinct

Albright opens with a strongly disciplinary statement about Mesopotamian cultural history and the cuneiform archive. He discusses the history of decipherment, Assyriological method, priestly/scribal guilds, textual chronologies, and the transmission of Mesopotamian materials into Israel. He also explicitly compares Babylonian and Indic cosmological chronologies and refers forward to Dumont’s Indian essay.

That cross-reference is important: the supplementary essays are not mere appendices. They participate in a comparative framework while preserving specialist authorship.

## Dumont’s essay: direct Sanskrit/Indic textual expertise

Dumont’s “Primitivism in Indian Literature” begins from the doctrine of the four Yugas and traces it through Vedic texts, the epics and Purāṇas. The essay uses Sanskrit technical vocabulary and primary textual references, then organizes Indian material around Lovejoy/Boas’s conceptual distinction between forms of primitivism.

This is exactly the sort of evidence that should prevent us from describing the mature Lovejoy project as if Lovejoy himself were still directly doing Indology. The Indian component is explicitly delegated to a Sanskrit/Vedic specialist, P.-E. Dumont.

Independent bibliographic evidence confirms Dumont’s specialist profile: his publications include *L’aśvamedha* (1927) and later major descriptions of Vedic ritual such as *L’agnihotra* (1939). Persée’s authority record also links him directly to the 1935 Lovejoy/Boas volume.

## Institutional relation at Johns Hopkins

Johns Hopkins ArchivesSpace confirms **William Foxwell Albright as a related person in the Arthur Oncken Lovejoy papers (MS-0038)**. This is stronger than simple co-publication: an archival connection exists at collection level, though the exact folder/correspondence relevant to the 1935 project still needs locating.

No equally clear online JHU hit for Dumont correspondence has yet been located. The absence of a web hit should not be treated as evidence that correspondence does not survive.

A second potentially useful repository is the **Johns Hopkins University Press records**, whose related names include both Lovejoy and Albright and whose genre includes correspondence. Because the 1935 book was published by Johns Hopkins Press, project correspondence may be distributed between personal papers and press records.

## Contemporary reception

The book was reviewed quickly and across several disciplinary venues in 1936, including *Philosophy*, *The Classical Review*, *Isis*, *Nature*, *The American Journal of Philology*, and *Classical Weekly*. Bibliographic records consistently foreground the supplementary Albright and Dumont essays in the book’s title/description. The *Nature* review characterizes the enterprise as a documentary/source-book project organized by compilation and classification of texts.

This reception is useful because it situates the volume as a collaborative documentary program, not simply another synthetic Lovejoy monograph.

## Methodological significance

The 1935 Preface also restates Lovejoy’s mature “anatomizing” method in unusually concrete editorial terms: philosophical texts contain heterogeneous notions; historical study begins by separating passages pertinent to different ideas, then regrouping those passages according to the unit under investigation rather than leaving them in the original author/text sequence.

This gives us a possible long arc from the notebooks:

- 004 decomposes Buddhist doctrinal systems and distinguishes original/derived elements;
- 005 decomposes rituals by mechanism rather than visible form;
- 1935 explicitly theorizes **analysis and anatomizing of texts** and organizes a multi-specialist documentary project around separated conceptual units.

The continuity is methodological, while the linguistic/area expertise becomes increasingly distributed.

## Current formulation

A defensible mature-career formulation is:

> Lovejoy’s relation to Orientalist scholarship changes from direct student/philologist to organizer of comparative documentary expertise. In *Primitivism and Related Ideas in Antiquity*, the Indian and ancient Western-Asian extensions are explicitly entrusted to P.-E. Dumont and W. F. Albright. The book therefore institutionalizes a division between the history-of-ideas problem and the specialist philological competences required to extend it across regions.
