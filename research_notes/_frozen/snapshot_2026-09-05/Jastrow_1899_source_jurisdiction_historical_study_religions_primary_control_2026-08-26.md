# Morris Jastrow Jr. 1899 — source jurisdiction and division of labor in the historical study of religions

Date: 2026-08-26
Status: **DIRECT PRIMARY / AMERICAN DISCIPLINARY-JURISDICTION CONTROL / PERIOD EVOLUTIONISM FIREWALL ACTIVE**

## Core result

Morris Jastrow Jr.'s `The Historical Study of Religions in Universities and Colleges`, *Journal of the American Oriental Society* 20 (1899), 317–325, is more than an institutional plea for chairs in the history of religions. The full primary body directly articulates a **division of epistemic labor** in which the historical study of a particular religion belongs first to specialists competent in its sources, while the general/comparative study occupies a different, secondary synthetic level.

This materially upgrades the project's `multiple evidentiary jurisdictions` model from a retrospective analytical reconstruction to a partly actor-declared contemporary programme.

Compact result:

> **Jastrow makes source competence jurisdictional: India belongs to the Indologist, Semitic/Egyptian/Judean/Greek/Roman religions to specialists trained on their sources, and so-called primitive religious phenomena to the anthropologist. General comparison is necessary but cannot substitute for direct source work on a particular religion.**

The article must be read with a strong period-language firewall. Jastrow also explicitly ranks `advanced` religions against `naive` / `primitive` religious life and treats the latter as privileged access to fundamental religious problems. That hierarchy is evidence for the contemporary discipline-building project, not a neutral project category and not a proxy for Lovejoy's own political semantics.

## I. Harvard itself is presented as a distributed field

Jastrow opens by surveying the weak institutional position of the historical study of religions in American universities. He says Harvard has no dedicated chair, but courses falling within the field are regularly given by:

- Charles Carroll Everett;
- Crawford H. Toy.

This directly corroborates the current reconstruction of Lovejoy's Harvard training as occurring inside a **distributed rather than departmentally unified** religion-study ecology.

Lovejoy's independent course evidence confirms that he actually studied comparative religion / psychology of religion with Everett and Hebrew religion with Toy in 1896–97. Jastrow does not mention Lovejoy and his 1899 article cannot establish Lovejoy's exposure to this particular formulation.

## II. Discipline genealogy: comparative philology, archaeology and newly opened sources

Jastrow describes the historical study of religions as a very new discipline. He calls it, in a qualified formulation, a `child of comparative philology` and `foster child of archaeology`.

The methodological revolution, in his account, came from:

- comparative-philological research;
- the opening of previously unknown ancient-historical sources in Egypt, Chaldea, Phoenicia, Cyprus, Greece and Rome;
- the resulting new attitude toward religious phenomena.

The discipline is therefore narrated as **source-infrastructure dependent** from the start.

Jastrow then points to the institutionalization of the field in France and Holland, specifically naming the Paris École des Hautes Études section for `Sciences Religieuses`.

This is important for the transatlantic Lovejoy story: the Paris infrastructure that Lovejoy entered in 1898–99 is being held up in the same American scholarly journal as an organizational model for the United States.

## III. Anti-dilettantism is a source-competence problem

Jastrow reports a German concern that the study of religions opens a door to dilettantism and says the danger is real. His solution is institutional and epistemic: universities must make the difficulty of proper investigation visible and provide a scientific standard.

He then turns that anti-dilettantism principle into a concrete division of labor.

## IV. First axiom: one scholar cannot properly cover the field

Jastrow writes that it is `almost an axiom` that the study of religions cannot properly be carried on by a single individual because it consists of numerous subdivisions requiring specialists.

His model is Paris, where an entire faculty participates.

He allocates jurisdictions explicitly:

- religions of India → Indologist;
- Semitic religion → specialist in Semitic sources;
- Egypt, Judea, Chaldea, Greece, Rome → investigators fitted to study them from their sources;
- religious phenomena of `primitive man` → anthropologist.

This is not merely administrative specialization. The decisive criterion is **competence to work from the relevant evidentiary base**.

Analytical reconstruction:

`historical object`
→ `specific source field`
→ `specialist competence`
→ `licensed first-order historical reconstruction`.

## V. Particular-religion research can exist without a universal generalist

Jastrow says a university ideally needs someone to represent the general subject, but substantial work can occur even without such a figure.

A doctoral student can investigate a particular religion under the professor whose expertise covers the relevant sources. He explicitly wants university language/literature departments — Greek, Latin, Semitic, Sanskrit, Germanic, Romance — to make the religions of the peoples whose texts they interpret into legitimate research objects.

This is a striking institutional articulation of the exact ecology around Lovejoy:

- Sanskrit / Buddhist-text work under Lanman;
- Hebrew religion under Toy;
- comparative religion / psychology under Everett;
- broader philosophical training alongside them.

The distributed structure is not an accidental deficiency in Jastrow's programme. It can itself furnish the source-grounded first layer of the new discipline.

## VI. Second axiom: particular religion requires direct recourse to sources

Jastrow later states a second, even cleaner rule. In a university course it should be an `axiom` that **no particular religion can be studied except through direct recourse to the sources**.

He then assigns a more limited but still useful role to general/comparative work: it broadens the student's horizon and helps situate the particular religion in relation to general religious phenomena.

The proposition-level hierarchy is therefore:

`direct source work + specialist competence`
→ primary warrant for a particular historical religion

while

`general/comparative study`
→ second-order relation, horizon and synthesis.

This should not be converted into a claim that comparison is epistemically inferior in every context. Jastrow is defining the training and research architecture he wants for the historical study of religions.

## VII. Contemporary `jurisdictions` were heterogeneous, not one method

Placed beside the newly recovered 1899 teacher controls, Jastrow's programme helps organize a real contemporary plurality:

### Everett 1899

- whole-school psychological synthesis;
- actor-declared compression of author/commentator strata;
- modern conceptual reconstruction;
- differential confidence across genealogical propositions.

### Toy 1899

- antecedent vs active cause;
- reinforcement vs origin;
- mechanism-sensitive category restriction;
- local mechanism vs universal genealogy.

### Jastrow 1899

- source domain determines specialist jurisdiction;
- no single scholar can cover the general field adequately;
- particular religion requires direct sources;
- general/comparative synthesis is a distinct level.

### Lanman / JAOS / Indological controversy

- Sanskrit/Pāli technical competence;
- textual witnesses and editions;
- source chronology, genre, semantics and historical dependence.

Thus the current project need not invent one `Harvard comparative-religion method`. A more historically grounded description is:

> **The American field was trying to coordinate multiple scholarly jurisdictions whose evidentiary objects differed: texts and languages, system psychology, comparative mechanisms, anthropology, and general history of religions.**

Notebook 004 and later notebook 005 can be studied as working objects moving across those jurisdictions without assuming that Lovejoy inherited one unified procedure.

## VIII. Paris is explicitly an institutional model, not simply a later influence

Jastrow's explicit praise of the Paris faculty structure matters for the Lovejoy chronology.

Lovejoy's Paris year (1898–99) is independently documented as including active participation in Marillier's EPHE Vth Section seminar. Jastrow's 1899 article shows that the same concentrated Paris `Sciences Religieuses` structure could simultaneously appear in American discipline-building discourse as a model of how specialist subdivisions should be coordinated.

Safe claim:

> **Lovejoy moved into a Paris infrastructure that American proponents of the field were themselves holding up as an institutional solution to the problem of coordinating source specialisms and general religious history.**

Do not infer that Lovejoy chose Paris because of Jastrow or had read the 1899 article while abroad.

## IX. The period evolutionism is constitutive, not incidental

Jastrow's source-jurisdiction programme sits inside a strong developmental hierarchy.

He says `advanced religions` such as Christianity, Judaism, Islam and Buddhism offer comparatively little opportunity for investigating the most fundamental problems of religion. For those, he turns to religions described as:

- more `naive`;
- less the result of conscious effort;
- less shaped by speculation;
- direct manifestations of human emotional/religious nature;
- religions of `savages` and peoples in a `primitive condition of culture`.

This is not decorative vocabulary. It helps define the object assigned to anthropology and the presumed epistemic privilege of `primitive` evidence for origins/fundamentals.

This adds a useful control to the larger `primitive` project:

> **disciplinary division of labor and temporal/cultural stratification were built together. The anthropologist's jurisdiction was partly constituted by assigning living peoples to an epistemically earlier layer from which fundamental religious mechanisms were expected to be visible.**

That claim is direct for Jastrow's programme. Do not yet transpose it into Lovejoy's 1906 political semantics; MD-001 remains the blocker for Lovejoy's own democracy/race/stage vocabulary.

## X. Relation to the JHI Blog argument

This control helps solve a framing problem. The Blog does not need to say simply that Lovejoy moved from Harvard `Orientalism` to Paris `comparative religion`.

A stronger architecture is now available:

> **At Harvard and in the AOS, the emerging historical study of religions was already being organized as a federation of source-bound specialisms and second-order comparison. Lovejoy's own training crossed several of these jurisdictions before his Paris year. Paris then supplied a more concentrated institutional workshop in which source criticism, anthropology and comparative classification were coordinated under `sciences religieuses`.**

The notebooks can therefore be read as archival evidence for what happens when one researcher carries propositions and evidence across jurisdictions whose proof standards are not identical.

This reinforces, without proving causal transmission, the repository's governing rule:

`source competence for proposition A`
≠
`automatic authority over proposition B`.

## XI. Chronology / uptake firewall

Jastrow's article was published in the 1899 JAOS volume and presented at the Cambridge AOS meeting while Lovejoy was in his Paris 1898–99 period. Current evidence does not establish:

- Lovejoy attendance at the 1899 Cambridge presentation;
- Lovejoy reading of the article in 1899;
- Jastrow's programme as the cause of Lovejoy's Paris choices;
- direct use of the article in notebook 004 or 005.

Its value is **field-level and actor-level disciplinary context**, not a textual influence edge.
