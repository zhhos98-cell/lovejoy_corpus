## 3. Anthropology 1 — the major new pre-Paris bridge

### 3.1 Actor-level fact

Lovejoy's card directly records:

> **Anthropology 1 — A, 1897–98.**

This is no longer a milieu-level inference. Lovejoy completed Harvard's numbered introductory anthropology course in his third graduate year and received an A.

### 3.2 Near-contemporaneous Harvard primary course statement: 26 Aug 1898

A public-domain Harvard/Peabody Museum guide, dated **26 August 1898**, contains a section `INSTRUCTION`, identifies anthropology as Division XIV of the Faculty of Arts and Sciences, and names:

- Frederic Ward Putnam — Peabody Professor of American Archaeology and Ethnology;
- Frank Russell — Instructor in Anthropology;
- Roland B. Dixon — Assistant in Anthropology.

It gives Course 1 as:

> `General Anthropology.— Somatology (Physical Anthropology); Archaeology; Ethnology; Ethnography. — Lectures and theses. Mon., Wed., Fri., at 9. Dr. Russell.`

The course is described as introductory to special study. It begins with somatology and comparative physical criteria of race, then moves to ethnology with special attention to the **origin and development of primitive arts and culture**. The listed ethnological subjects include technogeography / culture-environment relations; utilitarian and aesthetic arts studied with Museum objects; social life of `natural` peoples; government, marriage, law and ethnic jurisprudence; and, critically:

> `Religion, psychological origin and lines of development of primitive religions, fetishism, animism, shamanism, ritual.`

It then treats mythology, folklore, linguistics, archaeology and ethnography, human distribution, groups, origins, prehistoric ages, races, classification and migrations. Students were to be familiarized with standard and current anthropological literature, with Museum collections used materially in instruction.

Primary scan: *Guide to the Peabody Museum of Harvard University; A Statement Relating to Instruction in Anthropology* (Cambridge: University Press, John Wilson and Son, 1898), instruction section pp. 26–29 / PDF pp. 31–34.

### 3.3 Chronological firewall

The guide is dated **26 Aug 1898**, just after Lovejoy's 1897–98 academic year. It is therefore an exceptionally close institutional control, but it is not yet the exact 1897–98 FAS course-announcement page. Accordingly:

- `Lovejoy enrolled in Anthropology 1 and received A`: **DIRECT ACTOR PRIMARY CLOSED**.
- `Anthropology 1 at Harvard in the immediately adjacent 1898 course statement = General Anthropology under Frank Russell`: **DIRECT HARVARD PRIMARY, NEAR-CONTEMPORANEOUS**.
- `the exact wording of Lovejoy's 1897–98 Anthropology 1 syllabus was word-for-word identical to the Aug 1898 statement`: **HOLD pending exact 1897–98 announcement**.

Do not collapse these three propositions.

### 3.4 Continuity controls around Russell and Course 1

A Harvard Crimson notice of **2 June 1896** already treats `Anthropology 1` as an established numbered course and announces a possible new `Anthropology 2` in 1897–98 devoted especially to physical anthropology / somatology. It advises students intending to take Anthropology 2 in 1897–98 to take Anthropology 1 and Zoology 3 in 1896–97. Thus Course 1 clearly precedes Lovejoy's enrollment year and sits inside a developing sequenced anthropology curriculum.

Independent institutional-history controls place **Frank Russell in Harvard anthropology from 1897**. Harvard's quinquennial catalogue lists `1897 Frank Russell, Anthropology`; a later Crimson notice states he had been instructor in anthropology since 1897. The August 1898 Peabody statement then names him explicitly as instructor of Course 1.

This creates a strong temporal bracket:

`Anthropology 1 established by 1896`

`-> Frank Russell enters instructor rank in Anthropology in 1897`

`-> Lovejoy takes Anthropology 1 in 1897–98 and receives A`

`-> 26 Aug 1898 Harvard/Peabody statement: Anthropology 1 = General Anthropology, Dr. Russell, including primitive religion / ethnology / archaeology / ethnography`.

**Instructor attribution to Russell is therefore very strong and primary-bracketed, but the exact 1897–98 catalogue line remains the desired final control.**

## 4. Critical distinction: Course 1 itself contains primitive religion; Lovejoy did not record Course 3

The August 1898 guide also lists a separate graduate half-course:

> `3. Primitive Religions` — Professor Putnam and Mr. Dixon; theories of origin, Animism, Totemism, Fetishism, Ceremonial, Symbolism, Comparative Mythology and Folk-lore, with special study of the religions of three primitive peoples.

This is significant for departmental specialization, but **Lovejoy's card records Anthropology 1, not Anthropology 3**. Do not write that he took Putnam/Dixon's `Primitive Religions` course.

The stronger and safer result is more interesting anyway: **primitive religion was already an explicit component inside General Anthropology 1 itself**, not only a specialized optional course. Therefore Lovejoy's completed Anthropology 1 can be connected to a Harvard instructional regime in which religion was treated through `psychological origin`, developmental lines, fetishism, animism, shamanism and ritual, while archaeology, ethnography, social organization, language and material culture were taught alongside it.

## 5. Historiographical consequence: Harvard -> Paris is no longer a one-step jump

Before the record card, a plausible narrative could make Lovejoy's 1898–99 Paris work on primitive religions look like a sharp shift away from Harvard Buddhist / comparative-religion training.

The actor-level chronology is now more differentiated:

`1895–96` — formal Sanskrit sequence + Everett philosophy/comparative-religion structure

`1896–97` — Lanman advanced Pāli 5 (**A**) + Toy Semitic 13 (**A**) + AOS-announced *Milinda-pañha* communication

`1897–98` — **Anthropology 1 (A)** + continued philosophy seminar work

`Aug 1898 near-contemporary Harvard control` — General Anthropology 1 explicitly integrates ethnology, material culture, social institutions, primitive religion, mythology, folklore, linguistics, archaeology and ethnography

`1898–99 Paris` — comparative-religion / primitive-religion notebook work.

This does **not** establish a causal chain in which Harvard anthropology generated the Paris notebooks or Lovejoy's mature method. It changes the historical problem: Paris is no longer the first documented point at which Lovejoy's scholarly formation can be placed next to an anthropology curriculum organized around primitive religion, ethnology, material culture and comparative social forms.

The key transition to investigate is now:

> **How did Lovejoy move between Harvard's textual-comparative religious training and its museum/anthropological classification of `primitive` social and religious forms before entering the Paris comparative-religion environment?**

That is a much narrower and more sourceable question than a generic `Orientalism influenced Lovejoy` thesis.

## 6. Philosophy 12 — Lovejoy registered, then dropped Santayana's new Plato/Aristotle course

The card's row alignment gives:

> **Philosophy 12 — Dropped, 1897–98.**

George Santayana's own retrospective account supplies an unusually exact chronological identification. He says that when he returned to America in **September 1897**, he **began a new course, Philosophy 12, on Plato and Aristotle in English**. He names the principal texts: *Republic*, *Phaedrus*, *Symposium*, *Phaedo*, and *Nicomachean Ethics*.

The 1898 Harvard mid-year examination schedule independently lists `Philosophy 12` for 11 February 1898, confirming the numbered course was active in Lovejoy's third year.

Therefore the best current statement is:

> **Lovejoy registered for Santayana's new Philosophy 12 on Plato and Aristotle in 1897–98, but his student card marks the course `Dropped`.**

This is important evidence against a casual genealogy: the registration shows attempted proximity; the drop status prevents treating the course as completed training or claiming Santayana's course as a demonstrated source of Lovejoy's later classical materials.

## 7. Philosophy 20d — exact same-year identity closed: Palmer's Ethical Seminary

## 8. Philosophy 20c — registration secure, completion/instructor still not exact-year closed

The card records `Philosophy 20c` but shows no visible grade in the third-year block.

Royce's Harvard archive strongly associates `Philosophy 20c` with his seminary: it preserves `Philosophy 20c` questions, later class lists/plans and diaries explicitly calling it `Philosophy 20c Logic`; an editorial history of Royce's seminar says that his regular Metaphysical Seminary had begun in 1888–89, ran through the 1890s, and **by 1898 its announced topic was the problems of logic**. Later Harvard evidence explicitly identifies 20c as Royce's seminar.

Nevertheless, the exact 1897–98 catalogue line linking `20c` to Royce and its topic has not yet been directly recovered in this project. Status:

## 11. Relation to the `primitive` project

This evidence materially changes how the pre-1898 `primitive` question should be framed.

The most defensible current structure is not:

`Lovejoy studies Buddhism at Harvard -> goes to Paris -> encounters primitive religion`.

It is:

`textual/philological and comparative religion`

`+`

`general anthropology organized across somatology / ethnology / ethnography / archaeology, including developmental categories of primitive religion`

`->`

`Paris comparative-religion and primitive-religion work`.

That structure is especially relevant to the broader project claim that the problem is one of **layering rather than a simple ideological contradiction**. Harvard itself placed several resolutions of human difference side by side: textual language work, comparative religions, bodily/racial classification, material culture, social institutions, myth/ritual, ethnography and archaeology. Lovejoy's card now shows that he actually traversed more than one of those resolutions.

The next evidentiary task is to compare the **specific vocabulary and classificatory operations** of Harvard Anthropology 1 with notebook 005 and the Paris seminar materials, without assuming transfer in advance.
