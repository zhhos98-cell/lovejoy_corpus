# Batch 63 — Marillier’s source-criticism pedagogy before Lovejoy: 1888 primary programme + N. W. Thomas’s 1896 eyewitness account

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced
Scope: continue the Paris/EPHE line after Batch 62 by replacing retrospective reconstruction with earlier direct evidence from Marillier’s own teaching programme and a contemporary student witness.

## Core result

Batch 62 used Mauss’s 1902 inaugural lecture to reconstruct the Marillier workshop as a culture of bibliography, collective criticism, close document analysis and student research. That evidence was strong but retrospective.

This round finds a much earlier primary control. In Marillier’s **first EPHE free course in 1888–89**, a full decade before Lovejoy’s documented participation, the official annual report says that he began by comparing the evidentiary value of different kinds of testimony about the religions of peoples then classified as `non civilisés`. He separated direct testimony, myths, rites and customs; then examined different documentary classes — travellers’ accounts, missionary reports, official inquiries, etc. — and explained the **different rules of criticism to be applied to them**. He also supplied a **reasoned bibliography** and devoted a lesson to the methodological use of folktales.

This is direct evidence from Marillier’s own teaching record for an established documentary/source-critical pedagogy, not a retrospective attribution by Mauss.

The chronological chain can now be written much more securely:

> **Marillier 1888: compare testimony-types → distinguish document classes → apply source-specific critical rules → reasoned bibliography**
> → **Marillier 1894–97: critical comparison of myths, imported missionary traditions, marriage rites and competing anthropological theories**
> → **N. W. Thomas 1896: pupil work under lecturer direction, written essay → teacher comment → peer discussion**
> → **Lovejoy 1898–99: active auditor in Marillier’s survivance / sacrifice conferences + notebook 005**
> → **Mauss 1902: explicitly claims to maintain Marillier’s `critique des faits`, bibliography and seminar analysis.**

This materially strengthens the paper’s claim that Paris offered Lovejoy a **new evidentiary ecology**, while preserving Batch 52’s correction that Paris did not invent his analytical caution from scratch.

## 1. Marillier’s 1888–89 free course: direct primary evidence for source criticism

Primary source:

Léon Marillier, `XI. Cours libre`, *Rapport sur l’École pratique des hautes études, 1888–1889* (1888), pp.238–239.

Persée:
`https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0002_1888_num_4_1_19334`

The first-semester course was titled around **religious phenomena and their psychological basis**. Before moving to psychological explanation, however, the official report records a distinct methodological sequence.

### A. Compare the evidentiary value of testimony-types

Marillier first conducted a critical study of the **comparative value of different orders of testimony** concerning religions of peoples then classified as `non civilisés`.

The report differentiates:

- beliefs known through direct testimony;
- myths;
- rites;
- customs.

Thus the evidentiary problem precedes theoretical interpretation.

### B. Distinguish document classes

He then examined the different classes of documents available, specifically including:

- travellers’ narratives;
- missionary reports;
- official inquiries;
- other documentary genres.

This is exceptionally important for notebook 005, whose strongest early source-critical operations concern exactly the status of missionary/contact reports and the date/provenance of ethnographic testimony.

### C. Apply different critical rules to different documents

The report says Marillier showed **what rules of criticism should be applied** to these document classes.

The historical significance is not merely that Marillier advised skepticism. His pedagogy treated testimony as **genre- and provenance-dependent evidence**. A missionary report, traveller narrative, myth, rite-description or official inquiry could not automatically carry the same evidentiary weight.

### D. Bibliography was itself a methodological tool

Marillier supplied a **`bibliographie sommaire et raisonnée`** and gave a special lesson on how folktales could be used for religious study.

This places bibliography inside the research procedure from the beginning of his EPHE career, well before Mauss’s 1902 call for bibliographic research that was `exhaustive autant que possible`.

### E. The second semester then moved into substantive comparison

EPHE’s current prosopographic notice summarizes the second semester as work on the cult of the dead and animism in Africa and Oceania. The sequence is important:

`source/testimony criticism → bibliography → psychological/comparative interpretation`.

Do not reverse this and treat source criticism as an incidental technique attached to an already fixed theory.

## 2. This method persists through Marillier’s 1890s courses

The 1888 evidence is not an isolated inaugural flourish.

### 1893–94: critical study of Melanesian legends

Marillier’s EPHE report for 1893–94 divides his work into two blocks. The first is the survivance/afterlife programme later so closely matched by Lovejoy 005. The second is explicitly an **`Étude critique des légendes héroïques et divines de la Mélanésie`**, using Codrington and Haddon and comparing analogous traditions across groups.

Primary:
`https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0002_1893_num_7_3_19386`

### 1894–95: missionary-imported biblical traditions become a source problem

In the flood-myth course, Marillier explicitly includes the **influence of biblical traditions imported by missionaries** as part of the analysis.

Primary:
`https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0002_1894_num_8_4_19409`

This directly anticipates a recurring source-critical operation in the early pages of 005: apparent moral or doctrinal content may be a contact-period overlay and must be separated from earlier local evidence.

### 1895–97: comparison used to test established anthropological generalizations

The 1895–96 report says Marillier’s Australian marriage research contradicted several claims associated with Morgan, Fison and Howitt about primitive communal marriage. The 1896–97 report continues by carefully distinguishing rites of marital union from magical rites of purification/preservation and tracing changes in social/religious meaning with changes in family structure.

Primary:
- `https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0002_1895_num_9_5_19432`
- `https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0002_1896_num_10_6_19455`

The important continuity is not any particular theory of marriage. It is the repeated refusal to infer one social/religious meaning from outward ritual morphology alone.

## 3. N. W. Thomas 1896: a first-person witness to the seminar procedure

Primary source:

N. W. Thomas, `The École Pratique des hautes Études`, *Folk-Lore* 7 (1896), pp.200–201.

Wikisource facsimile/transcription, p.200:
`https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Folk-lore_-_A_Quarterly_Review._Volume_7,_1896.djvu/226`

Thomas describes the Religious Section for an English audience from **personal experience**. His compact statement is unusually valuable because it makes the pedagogical unit explicit:

> **`work by the pupils, directed by the lecturer`**.

For Marillier’s course specifically, Thomas says pupils could be asked to write an essay on a defined point; the essay would be read in the conference, **commented on by Marillier, and discussed by the other pupils**.

The procedure is therefore:

`assigned research problem → student paper → oral presentation/readout → teacher criticism → peer discussion`.

This is much stronger than a vague seminar label. It describes a recurring production cycle capable of generating the kind of heterogeneous, source-linked working material preserved in 005.

## 4. Thomas is not merely an outside observer: the EPHE record documents his student work

The contemporary EPHE report for 1896–97 states that **`M. Thomas, élève de la conférence sur les religions des peuples non civilisés`** received the title of `élève diplômé` for a thesis on **`Survivance du culte des animaux dans le pays de Galles`**.

Primary:
`https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0002_1896_num_10_6_19453`

Modern scholarship identifies this student as **Northcote Whitridge Thomas**, who studied with Marillier in Paris and submitted the thesis in 1897. N. W. Thomas subsequently published the corresponding study in *Revue de l’histoire des religions* 38 (1898), pp.295–347.

Useful modern control:
Paul Basu, `Who was N. W. Thomas?`, [Re:]Entanglements, 2018:
`https://re-entanglements.net/n-w-thomas/`

A further bibliographic control is N. W. Thomas’s own 1896 *Folk-Lore* note itself: it is clearly written by the same British folklorist who later published on Welsh animal cult survivals.

### Cataloguing caution

Later EPHE retrospective diploma tables repeatedly print the 1897 thesis under **`Albert Thomas`**. That attribution conflicts with the contemporary `M. Thomas` report, N. W. Thomas’s first-person 1896 account of the Marillier course, modern biographical research, and the later RHR publication under N. W. Thomas.

For publication, do **not** silently cite the retrospective table as if its first name were secure. Prefer the contemporary report + N. W. Thomas’s own article + modern biographical identification.

## 5. Important identity caution: the `Thomas (Anglais)` in Marillier’s 1888 class is not N. W. Thomas

The 1888 report lists among Marillier’s auditors a `Thomas (Anglais), élève de l’Université de Londres`.

This should **not** be identified with Northcote Whitridge Thomas.

N. W. Thomas was studying History at Trinity College, Cambridge from 1887 to 1891 and did not move into Paris ethnological/folklore study under Marillier until the mid-1890s. The 1888 auditor is therefore almost certainly another English `Thomas` unless new evidence proves otherwise.

This correction matters because the new 1888 method evidence is strong enough without manufacturing an implausibly early N. W. Thomas link.

## 6. Direct relevance to Lovejoy notebook 005

The first thirty pages of 005 already show the exact sort of source discipline documented in 1888.

Existing repository collation (`MS38_004_001_061_005_pp001-030_vs_Marillier_Survivance.md`) shows Lovejoy repeatedly asks whether apparently moralized afterlife evidence has been altered by:

- missionary influence;
- Hindu/Buddhist diffusion;
- date of collection;
- social/ritual determinants mistaken for moral desert;
- later reinterpretation of older structures.

The notebook does not simply reject contaminated evidence. It asks what part of the report can still be used, what mechanism produced the reported distinction, and what historical layer it belongs to.

The relation to Marillier’s 1888 programme is therefore now much tighter:

### Marillier 1888

`What kind of testimony/document is this?`  
→ `what rules of criticism apply to it?`  
→ `what evidentiary value may it carry?`

### Lovejoy 005, 1898–99

`When/through whom was the belief reported?`  
→ `is the category indigenous, imported, ritual, social, or moral?`  
→ `what determinant actually explains the observed outcome?`

This does not prove that Lovejoy copied a formal Marillier methodological lecture. It shows that the source-critical operations in 005 fall inside a **documented and long-standing Marillier pedagogy** rather than being reconstructed from thematic resemblance alone.

## 7. The Harvard → Paris argument can now be stated more exactly

Batch 52 correctly rejected the claim that Paris taught Lovejoy analysis from scratch. 004 already demonstrates evidentiary separation in philological work.

The new evidence lets us say more specifically what Paris added.

### Harvard / 004

Lovejoy works mainly with printed philological scholarship and canonical texts. He asks whether lexical/formal recurrence licenses claims about semantic identity, derivation and system.

### Paris / Marillier environment

Marillier’s documented pedagogy explicitly foregrounds **heterogeneous testimony-types and document genres**: travellers, missionaries, official inquiries, myths, rites, customs. Different sources require different rules of criticism before comparison.

### 005

Lovejoy’s unit of evidence correspondingly becomes a **reported practice or belief whose documentary provenance itself must be analyzed**.

Thus `term-in-text → rite-in-report` can now be decomposed into a more historically concrete transformation:

> **philological semantic control → ethnographic testimony criticism → mechanism-based reclassification.**

Paris’s contribution is therefore not simply a new topic or a new doctrine. It is an expansion in the **types of evidence whose provenance must be policed**.

## 8. Marillier 1888 → Thomas 1896 → Lovejoy 1898–99 → Mauss 1902 is now a genuine institutional-method sequence

The four points are independent and complementary.

### 1888 — teacher’s own programme

Different testimony types and documentary classes have different evidentiary values and require distinct rules of criticism; bibliography is part of the method.

### 1896 — student eyewitness

The school’s operative principle is pupil work directed by the lecturer; in Marillier’s course an essay is read, teacher-commented and peer-discussed.

### 1898–99 — Lovejoy’s documented participation

The EPHE report names Lovejoy among auditors `ayant pris une part active aux travaux`; 005’s dominant blocks match Marillier’s survivance and sacrifice/anthropophagy conference problems.

### 1902 — successor’s retrospective inheritance claim

Mauss says that for `critique des faits` he follows Marillier’s example and reconstructs bibliography, collective criticism and close seminar analysis of ethnographic documents.

The resulting methodological continuity is now much harder to dismiss as retrospective projection.

## 9. Historiographic consequence

The strongest interpretation is no longer merely that Marillier ran an interactive seminar.

More exact:

> **Marillier’s EPHE teaching joined a stable source-critical programme to a research-producing seminar form. Students were not only exposed to comparative theories; they were trained to discriminate document genres, evaluate testimony, construct bibliographies, prepare research papers and subject classifications to collective criticism. Lovejoy’s 005 notebook can therefore be located inside an institutional practice of producing comparative-religion evidence, not merely inside an intellectual milieu where certain theories happened to circulate.**

This is the level on which the French line can most plausibly matter to a later history-of-ideas argument.

It remains distinct from claiming that Marillier supplied Lovejoy’s mature theory, `unit-ideas`, or a wholly novel method.

A bibliographic correction after direct TOC verification:
