## Core result

Donald R. Kelley's `What is Happening to the History of Ideas?`, originally JHI 51.1 (1990), 3–25 and reprinted in *Intellectual News* in 1996, is now a major control on the `Lovejoy -> Skinner rupture` memory problem.

Kelley writes as JHI's executive editor after five years of reviewing more than a thousand submissions, in direct succession to Philip P. Wiener. His account is therefore not merely an external historiographic survey; it is an editor-level diagnosis of what the field and journal had become by the end of the 1980s.

The strongest result is:

> **Kelley does not narrate the new intellectual history as the replacement or death of Lovejoy's history of ideas. He criticizes the philosophical and canonical limits of unit-idea history, language-naive transmission models, and inherited internalism; yet he ultimately characterizes many of the new approaches as an extension of Lovejoy's encyclopedic and eclectic vision in a recognizably `Lovejovian spirit`.**

Compact formula:

`CRITIQUE OF LOVEJOYIAN ASSUMPTIONS`
+
`EXTENSION OF LOVEJOYIAN JURISDICTION`
!=
`CLEAN RUPTURE`.

This is especially important because the article appears in 1990, only two years after James Tully's 1988 *Meaning and Context* canonized a portable Skinner-and-critics debate.

## I. Editorial position and evidence status

The 1996 reprint identifies Kelley with JHI and states that the essay is reprinted from JHI 51 (1990), 3–25.

Kelley's opening footnote says the reflections draw on:

- five years as JHI executive editor;
- review of over 1000 submitted articles;
- more than 1500 `books received` notices;
- succession to Philip P. Wiener, who had presided over the Journal for its first forty-five years;
- comments from current Johns Hopkins History of Ideas Club members including Jerome Schneewind, J. G. A. Pocock and Orest Ranum;
- further advice from Lewis Beck, Allan Megill, Anthony Grafton and Bonnie Smith.

Thus the essay is a particularly valuable junction between editorial practice, older History of Ideas institutional memory, and the emerging contextualist/new-intellectual-history generation.

## II. Kelley explicitly rejects a narrow unit-idea/canon definition of the field

Kelley opens by saying the American history of ideas is associated above all with Lovejoy, his colleagues, epigones and critics, while insisting the field has a much longer and international history.

He then criticizes the attempt to define the enterprise philosophically around enduring categories of thought and successions of speculative systems.

His diagnosis is severe:

- inherited philosophical conventions have encouraged tracing durable categories and recognized speculative systems;
- the result has been to close rather than open inquiry;
- methodologically it can confine research to a culturally impoverished canon;
- his preferred object is instead the historical investigation of the textual and cultural remains of human thought processes;
- `intellectual history`, he says, is a better term for the enterprise because it sits at the junction of several disciplines.

This is a genuine break with a narrow philosophical ontology of `ideas`.

But it is not yet a break with Lovejoy's institutional/eclectic project.

---

## III. Kelley historicizes Lovejoy inside a much older field rather than treating him as absolute founder

A large portion of the article reconstructs the history of the history of ideas from ancient and early modern historiography of philosophy through encyclopedic humanist projects, post-Kantian philosophy of the history of philosophy, and later disciplinary traditions.

Lovejoy becomes one historically specific inheritor of a long problem:

- how should histories of thought be organized?
- internal vs external method;
- disciplinary/philosophical doctrine vs wider intellectual/cultural process;
- rational succession vs sociological/affective factors;
- canonical authors vs larger textual/cultural fields.

This matters for the current project because it refuses another clean boundary:

`BEFORE LOVEJOY`
vs
`LOVEJOY INVENTS HISTORY OF IDEAS`.

Kelley instead makes Lovejoy a node in a much longer institutional and epistemic genealogy.

---

## IV. Kelley criticizes Lovejoy's resistance to context/language/social explanation

Kelley says Lovejoy, as a professional philosopher, made relatively few concessions to historians asking for greater social context, literary scholars emphasizing language/textuality, or sociological approaches stressing relativism and ideology.

He describes an older conceptual world in which:

- ideas could be imagined as comparatively pure;
- authorial control seemed straightforward;
- communication from intellect to intellect seemed easy;
- `meaning` appeared recoverable without the full complexity later assigned to context, unconscious structures, ideology and language.

He explicitly says Lovejoy had some suspicions of this comfortable model but did not place skepticism at the center of his agenda.

This is one of the strongest editor-level retrospective critiques of Lovejoy in the current corpus.

But Kelley then immediately adds that the field changed as disciples became masters or heretics and old texts acquired new meanings.

The transition is historical and branching, not simply revolutionary.

---

## V. Old practices remain active after the supposed rupture

Kelley says that by the late 1980s mainstream history of ideas still preserved important ties with Lovejoy's prewar vision.

He observes continuing emphasis on:

- individual authors;
- canonized texts;
- ideas, doctrines, theories, systems and `-isms`;
- national and disciplinary traditions;
- periodization;
- questions of `influence`;
- `thought-content` passed from thinker to thinker;
- internalist histories of doctrines and disciplines.

This is crucial evidence against a strong replacement model.

Even after Skinner, linguistic turns, social/cultural history and poststructuralism:

`OLDER RELATION-ROLES PERSIST`.

This parallels the JHI non-Western census result:

`ROLE DIVERSIFICATION != LINEAR DECENTERING`.

At the methodological level:

`NEW METHODS ACCUMULATE`
!=
`OLD METHODS DISAPPEAR`.

---

## VI. Kelley sees linguistic/rhetorical critique as practical revision of unit-ideas

Kelley treats the linguistic turn as having practical consequences for JHI.

He points to:

- history of terms and technical vocabularies;
- philological and linguistic analysis of philosophical/political works;
- rhetoric and textuality;
- critique of the implicitly idealistic conception of unit-ideas.

The important point is that these changes appear **inside JHI execution**, not only as external attacks on the journal.

Thus the field's practice incorporates criticisms that might otherwise be narrated as having rendered the old field obsolete.

This supports the emerging institutional thesis:

`EXTERNAL CRITIQUE`
can become
`INTERNAL PRACTICE TRANSFORMATION`
without
`INSTITUTIONAL DEATH`.

---

## VII. The decisive formulation: the `new intellectual history` as Lovejovian extension

Kelley identifies several expansions of intellectual history by the late 1980s:

- more sophisticated treatment of texts and influence;
- histories of intellectual traditions and canons;
- attention to race, gender and class as epistemological/ideological forces;
- questions of canon formation;
- unconscious attitudes and unexamined `foreknowledge`;
- broader cultural and social dimensions of meaning.

He then gives the crucial retrospective judgment:

> the new intellectual history involves criticism of Lovejoy's `endemic assumptions`, but more importantly it represents an **extension of Lovejoy's original encyclopedic and eclectic vision in a quite Lovejovian spirit**.

This is perhaps the cleanest counter-evidence yet to a simple `Lovejoyian history of ideas died and Cambridge contextualism replaced it` narrative.

The distinction is:

`LOVEJOY'S SPECIFIC EPISTEMIC ASSUMPTIONS`
can be criticized
while
`LOVEJOY'S EXPANSIVE FIELD JURISDICTION / ECLECTIC PROGRAMME`
can survive and be enlarged.

This maps exactly onto the project's firewall:

`PROGRAMME != PRACTICE != INFRASTRUCTURE != CANON`.

---

## VIII. Skinner enters as one branch of disciplinary intellectual history, not sovereign arbiter

Kelley cites Skinner and Pocock within the discussion of the history of political thought and authorial intention.

He treats authorial intention as unavoidable — at least as a `necessary fiction` — in disciplinary histories such as science or political thought, explicitly identifying political thought as Skinner's and Pocock's primary domain.

At the same time, Kelley treats the general problem of `context` as not fully resolvable through theoretical argument alone. Context is reconstructed through scholarship, probability, interpretation and informed imagination; it cannot be verified absolutely or achieved totally.

This places Skinnerian questions inside a broader hermeneutic and evidentiary field rather than granting one universal theory jurisdiction over all intellectual history.

The safe interpretation is:

`SKINNER/POCOCK = IMPORTANT DISCIPLINARY METHOD BRANCH`
within
`PLURAL INTELLECTUAL-HISTORICAL JURISDICTION`.

---

## IX. 1987–1990 JHI is actively reconstructing itself, not announcing its own demise

Kelley's 1987 `Horizons of Intellectual History: Retrospect, Circumspect, Prospect` appears in JHI 48.1.

JHI 48.2 then contains a cluster of critical tributes around Lovejoy and *The Great Chain of Being* after fifty years.

By 1990 Kelley writes `What is Happening to the History of Ideas?` from the executive editor's chair.

He also points to the planned `Library of the History of Ideas` and his volume *The History of Ideas: Canon and Variations* as infrastructures for reprinting/reflection on Lovejoy and theoretical questions.

So the late-1980s/early-1990s JHI regime is best represented as:

`RETROSPECTIVE REASSESSMENT`
+
`METHOD DIVERSIFICATION`
+
`CANON REPACKAGING`
+
`INSTITUTIONAL CONTINUITY`.

Not:

`POST-SKINNER TERMINAL DECLINE`.

## X. Relation to Tully 1988 and Grafton 2006

We now have three different canon/self-memory technologies in a narrow chronological arc.

### Tully 1988

A portable anthology centered on Skinner and selected critics.

Parekh/Berki 1973 and 1976 are not reprinted, while Skinner's 1974 response is.

### Kelley 1990

JHI executive-editor field survey.

Criticizes philosophical unit-idea assumptions but describes new intellectual history as extension of Lovejoy's encyclopedic/eclectic programme in a Lovejovian spirit.

### Grafton 2006

JHI retrospective emphasizing Lovejoy/Wiener institutional vision, Lovejoy–Spitzer and Colie as internal debates, Skinner as a major transformation, and explicit refusal of a single proper method.

Therefore there was no single post-1988 memory regime.

Better:

`MULTIPLE CANON PACKAGES`
compete and overlap.

The later `clean rupture` is likely an emergent simplification produced across teaching, citation, disciplinary labels and retrospective surveys rather than a single authoritative declaration.

---

## XI. New conceptual result: critique can preserve institutional genealogy

Kelley 1990 makes visible a pattern important beyond this case.

A scholarly field can preserve continuity by distinguishing:

- objects from methods;
- jurisdiction from epistemic assumptions;
- infrastructure from doctrine;
- institutional identity from one founder's preferred analytic technique.

Thus:

`CRITIQUE OF METHOD`
need not imply
`REJECTION OF FIELD`.

And:

`NEW PRACTICE`
can be narrated as
`EXTENSION OF OLD JURISDICTION`.

This may explain why JHI survived changes that later historiography described as the demise of `history of ideas`.

---

## XII. Article-level payoff

The canon-memory ending can now become substantially stronger.

Do not ask only:

`Why was Skinner remembered as defeating Lovejoy?`

Ask:

> **How did different institutions and publications assign different kinds of continuity and rupture to the same methodological changes?**

Tully 1988 constructs a Skinner-centered debate canon.

Kelley 1990 constructs a critical-but-continuous JHI genealogy.

Grafton 2006 constructs a plural institutional memory.

Later field surveys can then retrospectively compress these plural memories into a simpler `decline / return` narrative.

This is a history of **jurisdiction over methodological memory**.
