# Martin Guerre — book ↔ stage epistemic-constraint round

Date: 2026-09-03  
Status: **TERMINAL COMPARATIVE CONTROL / BOOK-STAGE LINK / NON-GENEALOGICAL**

Scope: final-stage return from verification-regime research to Natalie Zemon Davis's book and the musical. This note asks how historical uncertainty is redistributed when the same case moves between archive, book, film, and musical theatre. It does not infer the content of the October 2026 Old Vic performance before opening.

## Thesis

Davis's *The Return of Martin Guerre* is not best treated as the musical's simple `source text`. It functions more powerfully as an **epistemic constraint** on later representation: it identifies where the archive permits a claim, where contextual reasoning makes a claim plausible, and where staged form can create an interior certainty unavailable to historical proof. The history of the musical is therefore readable as repeated reallocation of the epistemic variable `what does Bertrande know, and who else is allowed to know that she knows?`

The chain is not:

`historical event → accurate book → less accurate adaptation`.

It is closer to:

`archival traces → Davis's warranted reconstruction → film-generated historical possibilities → musical assignment of private knowledge → later revision/re-withdrawal of that assignment`.

## 1. The book was generated partly by performance pressure

Davis's 1983 preface explicitly describes collaboration on Daniel Vigne and Jean-Claude Carrière's film as a stimulus for further historical inquiry. Writing for actors rather than readers raised questions about sixteenth-century motivation that the archival prose problem did not force in the same way; watching Gérard Depardieu construct Arnaud gave Davis new ways to think about the historical impostor. Her formulation of the filmmaking process as a `historical laboratory` that generated possibilities rather than proofs establishes a clean distinction:

`performance can generate a possible historical configuration`

≠

`performance can prove that configuration historically`.

The second half of the preface is equally important: Davis was troubled when cinema's simplifications removed or softened elements she regarded as historically explanatory, including Basque background, rural Protestantism, Bertrande's double game, and the judge's contradictions. Thus the book does not simply follow the film. It is partly a **corrective response to the epistemic compression imposed by film form**.

## 2. Bertrande's knowledge is a historical claim with a visible proof burden

The most useful control is Davis's 1988 reply to Robert Finlay, `On the Lame`. Davis deliberately puts `knew` in quotation marks and says that the degree of Bertrande's conscious accommodation is hidden from us. She nevertheless argues that the cumulative legal evidence makes collaboration highly plausible and eventually necessarily explicit. This is stronger than mere free invention but weaker than direct archival access to a private mental state.

That gives a useful three-level model:

- `E`: surviving legal/source evidence;
- `I`: Davis's contextual and cumulative inference that Bertrande collaborated;
- `M`: a staged character's explicit interior knowledge.

The historical controversy lives at the transition `E → I`. The musical can bypass that transition by simply making `M` audible.

## 3. 1996 musical: inference becomes privileged audience knowledge

Barnden's 2024 article makes the formal transformation unusually explicit. The 1996 musical follows Davis's speculative/argumentative position that Bertrande recognizes the imposture, but musical-theatre conventions convert that position into something different in kind. Once Arnaud and Bertrande are alone, duet, soliloquy, reprise, and the `I want` structure give the audience access to private intention and recognition. The audience no longer has to adjudicate whether Bertrande knew from external behavior. The form tells them.

Thus:

`Davis: K_historiographical = strongly argued but evidentially indirect`

becomes

`1996 musical: K_dramatic = explicit to audience`.

That conversion changes agency. If Bertrande knows, then continued intimacy becomes choice/consent inside the fictional world; if she does not know, the same acts can read as deception and sexual violation. The adaptive decision is therefore simultaneously epistemic, ethical, and causal.

Barnden also identifies an important trade-off: the 1996 version gains explicit agency but loses mystery. Rebecca Lock, the original Bertrande, later described the show as dramatically weakened because the audience already sees that the returned man is not Martin. In other words, **epistemic privilege has a dramaturgical cost**.

## 4. 2007: knowledge is deliberately withdrawn again

According to Barnden, later iterations, especially the 2007 revival, move back toward the film's uncertainty. This restores jeopardy but reopens ambiguity over Bertrande's consent. The same narrative therefore cycles through distinct knowledge distributions:

`film: audience uncertainty ≈ de Coras/Bertrande uncertainty`

→ `1996 musical: audience privileged; Bertrande's knowledge explicit`

→ `2007 revival: uncertainty partially restored`.

This is not merely rewriting plot. It is **version-level redistribution of epistemic access**.

## 5. What the 2026 Old Vic paratext tells us before performance

As of 3 September 2026, the Old Vic production has not opened. Claims must therefore stay at paratext/workshop level. What is already secure:

- it is marketed as an `all-new version` with new music, lyrics, folk-inflected orchestrations, semi-staged form, and in-the-round staging;
- the Old Vic says the new version places Bertrande's `choices` at the emotional heart of the show;
- Boublil and Schönberg describe the story in publicity as one of lies and passionate love versus truth, or more importantly `what we each perceive to be true`;
- the theatre asks audiences `You think you know Martin Guerre? Think again.`

These signals do not tell us whether the 2026 script restores or abolishes ambiguity around Bertrande's knowledge. They do show that **perception rather than simple revelation is being foregrounded in the production's own actor/paratheatrical vocabulary**.

## 6. Strongest formulation

The book-stage relationship is best described as a **constraint-and-release system**.

The archive constrains what Davis can warrant. Davis uses contextual reasoning to expand the field of plausible historical action while marking unresolved interiority. Performance releases itself from that proof burden by assigning thoughts and recognition directly to characters. But every such release changes the ethical and causal story, and later versions can deliberately withdraw the certainty again.

Therefore the durable object is not simply `the story of Martin Guerre`. It is a repeatedly adjusted relation among:

`historical evidence` → `warranted inference` → `represented interiority` → `audience knowledge` → `agency/consent judgement`.

## 7. October 2026 performance test

When the Old Vic run opens, the decisive checks are narrow:

1. Does Bertrande learn or know Arnaud's identity before the audience does, with the audience, or after the audience?
2. Are private songs/soliloquies still used to authenticate her interior knowledge?
3. Does the production preserve an Arnaud/Martin distinction visually and vocally, or blur it?
4. Does in-the-round staging make the audience a distributed witness community rather than a privileged omniscient observer?
5. Is the trial the site where truth is produced, merely announced, or destabilized?
6. Does the new version turn `truth` into a stable revelation or into competing perceptions?

Until performance evidence exists, these remain tests, not claims.

## Compressed conclusion

**Davis's book and the musical are linked less by fidelity than by proof burden. Davis's historical reconstruction makes Bertrande's collaboration plausible through cumulative evidence while preserving the opacity of private consciousness; the 1996 musical converts that inference into explicit audience knowledge, thereby making consent legible but weakening mystery; later versions restore uncertainty. The 2026 Old Vic paratext, with its emphasis on Bertrande's choices and on what people `perceive to be true`, suggests that the newest version is again reorganizing the distribution of knowledge. The adaptation history is therefore an epistemic history: each version decides who may know what, when, and with what consequences.**
