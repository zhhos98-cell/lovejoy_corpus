## Core finding

The p.18 passage should no longer be treated as source-unresolved in a general sense. Henry Clarke Warren’s **_Buddhism in Translations_ (1896), Chapter II, introductory discourse, pp.111–116**, supplies almost exactly the two interpretive possibilities that Lovejoy’s notebook subsequently separates and reprioritizes.

Warren asks why the Buddha refused to answer the standard questions about the eternity/finitude of the world, soul/body identity, and post-mortem existence. His answer has two parts:

1. the questions are **“out of court”** because they **take for granted what he denies**; a Yes/No answer would therefore concede a false presupposition;
2. the Buddha also objects to metaphysical speculation because his is a **“purely practical aim”** and an applied philosophy directed to suffering and its cessation.

The notebook on PDF p.18 takes up exactly this interpretive fork. It says that the refusals can look at first like the `philosophical agnosticism of a purely practical teacher`, but closer scrutiny shows that this is not a necessary interpretation: the refusal is based on an objection to the **form of the questions put**, while Buddhism remains a doctrine of salvation based on insight.

The best source-controlled reading is therefore:

> **Warren supplies both the false-presupposition explanation and the practical/metaphysical explanation; Lovejoy sharpens the distinction and privileges the first as philosophically deeper than a simple practical agnosticism.**

This is a cleaner example of analytical transformation than the earlier assumption that the p.18 idea had no identifiable immediate source.

## 1. Warren’s Chapter II is unusually close to 004’s architecture

But Warren clearly functions as a **local structural and textual scaffold** for the turn from unanswered questions to the analysis of sentient existence and dependent origination.

## 2. Warren explicitly distinguishes false presupposition from practical aim

Warren’s introductory discourse states that the ten unanswered theories have troubled modern students because it is unclear whether the Buddha knew but refused to say, regarded the questions as outside his philosophy, or had another reason.

He then says that the questions are `out of court`: all, perhaps even the finite/infinite-world questions, **take for granted what the Buddha denies**. He compares them to a loaded question such as asking someone whether he has stopped beating his mother. A direct Yes/No answer is impossible because the form of the question imports a false premise.

Warren immediately adds a second explanation. The Buddha also objected to the questions because they were metaphysical and speculative. His was a **purely practical aim**; if he taught the truth about misery and its cessation, that was sufficient. Warren calls Buddhism a religious system and its philosophy an **applied philosophy**.

Thus both elements of Lovejoy’s p.18 note were already juxtaposed in a source demonstrably central to the notebook.

## 3. Lovejoy’s p.18 operation is a reweighting, not an invention

The notebook’s wording is analytically sharper than Warren’s juxtaposition. Lovejoy sets up the practical-agnoticism reading as an initial appearance:

> at first sight, philosophical agnosticism of a purely practical teacher.

He then says closer scrutiny shows this is not a necessary interpretation; the refusals rest on an objection to the **form of the questions**, `not merely` the attitude of a common-sense moralist. Buddhism itself is a doctrine of salvation based on **insight** and must understand the origin of suffering/existence before prescribing its removal.

The operation is therefore:

- Warren: **false presupposition + practical anti-metaphysics** as coexisting reasons;
- Lovejoy: **false/question-form problem is analytically prior**, because Buddhism cannot be reduced to practical indifference; it has a theoretical psychology/causal analysis required by its soteriology.

This matters because it connects the unanswered-question passage directly to the next notebook move: the programmatic analysis of the Three Characteristics and dependent origination on PDF p.20.

The p.18–20 transition now reads as one argument:

> Buddhist silence is not absence of theory → its theoretical content must be reconstructed in the form appropriate to the system → the Three Characteristics and `paticca-samuppāda` provide that general position and technical working-out.

## 4. Rhys Davids supplies a second upstream layer

Rhys Davids’s _Buddhism: Its History and Literature_ (1896), Lecture I, also provides part of the intellectual substrate. After classifying the pre-Buddhist/Sāṁkhya environment, he says the central Buddhist alternative ignores/rejects soul-theory questions as useless and inimical to the practical ideal. He then warns that this **apparently negative position is not the Dharma itself**; it is Buddhism’s answer to earlier systems. The positive side still has to be reconstructed.

Rhys Davids also moves from that negative clearing operation to a causal account: Buddhism rejects ultimate First-Cause explanation but insists that things have causes and traces one cause back to another.

Thus p.18 is best placed at the intersection of two recent English-language syntheses:

- **Rhys Davids:** the negative anti-soul position is not the whole positive Dharma;
- **Warren:** unanswered questions contain denied presuppositions, while Buddhism also has practical/applied aims;
- **Lovejoy:** the question-form/presupposition issue is used to resist reducing Buddhism to mere practical agnosticism, then connected to the positive theoretical structure reconstructed in dependent origination.

## 5. Why this matters for the 1898 article

The 1898 JAOS paper states securely in Lovejoy’s own voice that Buddhism is a **system of spiritual discipline based on psychological analysis**, not an ontological metaphysics. It rejects Waddell/Hartmann-style readings that force the first nidāna into an ultimate-metaphysical framework. `Avijjā` concerns the theoretical insight relevant to salvation, and the middle section of dependent origination contains the psychological analysis through which that insight is articulated.

This is the mature published counterpart of the p.18–20 movement:

1. reject the wrong question/metaphysical frame;
2. identify the theoretical form actually proper to Buddhism;
3. reconstruct the internal causal/psychological machinery that performs its soteriological work.

The connection is methodological rather than verbal. The historian must first identify the **type of question a system is equipped to answer**, then reconstruct its concepts according to the functions they perform within that system.

## 6. Revised rule-of-inference set for 004

Batch 25 proposed several rules of inference. The Warren identification lets the second rule be stated more precisely:

1. **Genealogical discrimination:** resemblance must be doctrinally diagnostic before it supports historical dependence.
2. **Question-frame discrimination:** a refusal to answer may indicate rejection of a question’s presuppositions, not absence of theoretical commitment; the interpreter must reconstruct the system’s own problem-form.
3. **Functional discrimination:** technical terms are distinguished by the causal/semantic work they perform in the system.
4. **Compositional restraint:** historical borrowing does not entail loss of systemic intelligibility.
5. **System reconstruction:** distributed formulas can jointly articulate a general philosophical position.

These rules are not a mature history-of-ideas manifesto. They are observable operations produced while Lovejoy worked through a specific contemporary Buddhist source ecology.
