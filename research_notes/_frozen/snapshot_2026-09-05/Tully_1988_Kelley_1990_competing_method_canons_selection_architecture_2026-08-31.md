# Tully 1988 vs Kelley 1990: competing anthology canons and the selection architecture of methodological memory

Date: 2026-08-31
Status: **DIRECT/PUBLISHER CONTENTS CONTROL + HIGH-GRADE BIBLIOGRAPHIC CONTROL / EDITORIAL MOTIVE NOT INFERRED / FULL PREFACES STILL DESIRABLE**

## Core result

The late-1980s/early-1990s methodological field did not produce one canon. It produced at least two materially different anthology canons within two years:

1. James Tully, ed., *Meaning and Context: Quentin Skinner and His Critics* (1988);
2. Donald R. Kelley, ed., *The History of Ideas: Canon and Variations* (1990), volume 1 of the Library of the History of Ideas.

Both volumes make scattered methodological writing portable. But they solve different canonization problems.

Tully builds a **Skinner-centered controversy package**:

`SKINNER'S MOST DISCUSSED METHODOLOGICAL ESSAYS`
+
`SELECTED CRITICS`
+
`NEW REPLY`.

Kelley builds a **JHI/history-of-ideas lineage package**:

`FOUNDERS`
-> `FOLLOWERS`
-> `QUESTIONS`.

The strongest new interpretation is therefore:

> **The later memory of intellectual-history methodology was assembled through competing canon infrastructures rather than inherited from one settled rupture. Tully selected texts to make a Skinner-centered controversy portable; Kelley selected texts to make a JHI-centered genealogy portable. Their omissions differ accordingly.**

New governing formula:

`CANONIZATION BY PROBLEM SELECTION`
!=
`CANONIZATION BY PERSON IMPORTANCE ALONE`.

No omission below is evidence of deliberate suppression. Selection has consequences for visibility even when it is editorially ordinary and intellectually defensible.

# I. Tully 1988: portable controversy around Skinner

Public publisher/Google Books descriptions of *Meaning and Context* state that Skinner's most valuable methodological essays and the best critical articles concerning his work had been dispersed through journals and difficult to obtain. The volume was designed to gather the debate.

The public description specifies:

- five of Skinner's most widely discussed methodological articles;
- seven critical articles;
- five of the critical pieces reprinted;
- two commissioned essays, by John Keane and Charles Taylor;
- a new fifty-seven-page `Reply to My Critics` by Skinner.

The visible contents include Skinner's:

- `Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas`;
- `Motives, Intentions and the Interpretation of Texts`;
- `Social Meaning and the Explanation of Social Action`;
- `Some Problems in the Analysis of Political Thought and Action`;
- `Language and Social Change`;
- `A Reply to My Critics`.

The critic roster includes Martin Hollis, Keith Graham, Joseph V. Femia, Nathan Tarcov, Kenneth Minogue, John Keane and Charles Taylor.

This makes the anthology's basic object:

`SKINNER + A SELECTED SET OF OBJECTIONS TO SKINNER`.

The publication is therefore an obvious **canon infrastructure for a controversy**.

Evidence ceiling:

- the selection architecture and contents are direct/high-grade bibliographic controls;
- the formulation `most widely discussed` / `best critical articles` currently comes from public publisher/Google Books description rather than a directly read Tully preface;
- Tully's own detailed selection rationale remains a live direct-text target.

# II. Tully's selection asymmetry: Skinner 1974 enters, Parekh/Berki do not

Skinner's 1974 `Some Problems in the Analysis of Political Thought and Action` is reprinted in the anthology.

The repo's direct reconstruction of that article shows that large parts of it answer Bhikhu Parekh and R. N. Berki's JHI 1973 critique:

`The History of Political Ideas: A Critique of Q. Skinner's Methodology`.

Skinner 1974 explicitly addresses their claims about:

- one definite intention in complex works;
- whether intention recovery is sufficient;
- whether context is universally necessary;
- the possibility of transhistorical audiences;
- Hobbes's *Leviathan* as a case allegedly intelligible without specific context;
- traditions/paradigms and political action.

Yet Tully 1988 does not reprint:

- Parekh/Berki 1973 JHI;
- Berki/Parekh 1976 *Political Theory* rejoinder.

Therefore the anthology produces a concrete selection asymmetry:

`SKINNER'S RESPONSE TO PAREKH/BERKI`
= INCLUDED

while

`PAREKH/BERKI'S ORIGINAL JHI CRITIQUE + LATER REJOINDER`
= NOT INCLUDED.

The correct historical statement is:

> **The canonical package preserves Parekh and Berki intertextually inside Skinner's development more strongly than it preserves them as autonomous counter-texts.**

Do not infer intentional erasure without editorial correspondence or explicit selection rationale.

# III. Kelley 1990: a competing JHI-centered canon

Donald R. Kelley's *The History of Ideas: Canon and Variations* appeared in 1990, two years after Tully's anthology, as volume 1 of the `Library of the History of Ideas`.

Public bibliographic descriptions say the collection gathers important articles on the theory and practice of intellectual history from JHI and emphasizes the ways the field expanded beyond and departed from Lovejoy's original conception.

The table of contents is itself an historiographical argument.

## Part One — `Founders`

Includes:

- Arthur O. Lovejoy, `Reflections on the History of Ideas`;
- Frederick J. Teggart, `A Problem in the History of Ideas`;
- Leo Spitzer, `Geistesgeschichte vs. History of Ideas as Applied to Hitlerism`;
- Lovejoy's reply to Spitzer;
- Theodore Spencer on Lovejoy's *Essays in the History of Ideas*;
- Lovejoy, `Historiography and Evaluation: A Disclaimer`.

The foundational canon is therefore not `Lovejoy alone`. It already contains challenge and reply.

## Part Two — `Followers`

Includes, among others:

- Abraham Edel;
- Paul O. Kristeller;
- Philip P. Wiener, `Logical Significance of the History of Thought`;
- Joseph Mazzeo;
- Leonard Krieger;
- Daniel J. Wilson;
- Kathleen Duffin;
- Daniel J. Wilson's 1987 fifty-year reassessment;
- Edward P. Mahoney's 1987 Lovejoy article;
- Francis Oakley's 1987 `Lovejoy's Unexplored Option`.

## Part Three — `Questions`

Includes methodological/problem pieces by figures including:

- Johan Huizinga;
- a response by Jacob Katz;
- Calvin G. Rand;
- Nils Kvastad;
- Paul O. Kristeller;
- Lester Crocker;
- Patrick Hutton;
- Donald R. Kelley, `Horizons of Intellectual History`.

The volume therefore constructs:

`FOUNDING PROGRAMME + EARLY INTERNAL ARGUMENT`
-> `SUCCESSOR PRACTICE / MODIFICATION`
-> `OPEN QUESTIONS`.

This is a very different canon architecture from Tully's `Skinner + critics`.

# IV. Kelley's selection is especially revealing because of what it does not pair

Three asymmetries are currently high-value.

## A. Philip Wiener: earlier method essay included, 1961 influence essay omitted

Kelley includes Wiener's `Logical Significance of the History of Thought`.

But the contents do not include Wiener's 1961 JHI article:

`Some Problems and Methods in the History of Ideas`.

That 1961 article is precisely the one Quentin Skinner explicitly cites in 1969 for influence condition (ii), alternative-source discrimination.

Thus:

`WIENER AS JHI METHOD SUCCESSOR`
= INCLUDED

but

`WIENER AS PRIOR ART IN THE LATER SKINNER INFLUENCE DEBATE`
= NOT MADE VISIBLE THROUGH THIS ANTHOLOGY.

This is strong evidence that anthology selection follows a problem architecture rather than simply collecting each major figure's later historiographically consequential text.

## B. 1987 Lovejoy cluster: Wilson/Mahoney/Oakley included, Richter omitted

JHI 48.2 (1987) opened with five Lovejoy/*Great Chain* reassessment pieces:

- Wilson;
- Gordon-Bournique;
- Mahoney;
- Oakley;
- Richter.

Kelley 1990 reprints:

- Wilson;
- Mahoney;
- Oakley.

It does not reprint Melvin Richter's `Begriffsgeschichte and the History of Ideas`, the article that explicitly compares founders' programmes with actual performances, rejects lexical identity as proof of semantic identity, and places Skinner 1969 alongside historical semantics as a critique of unchanging unit-ideas.

Therefore the anthology's 1987 usable past emphasizes internal reassessment of Lovejoy more than a direct comparative bridge to *Begriffsgeschichte* and Skinner.

Again, selection consequence != editorial motive.

## C. Parekh/Berki 1973 omitted

Kelley's visible contents do not include Parekh/Berki's 1973 JHI critique of Skinner.

This is especially revealing in comparison with Tully:

- Tully includes Skinner 1974 but not the Parekh/Berki provocation;
- Kelley includes a broad JHI methodological lineage but not that JHI article either.

Thus the Parekh/Berki branch falls between two canon architectures:

`NOT CENTRAL TO SKINNER ANTHOLOGY'S SELECTED CRITIC ROSTER`
+
`NOT CENTRAL TO KELLEY'S JHI FOUNDERS/FOLLOWERS/QUESTIONS GENEALOGY`.

This is a plausible material mechanism for later low visibility without any claim of deliberate exclusion.

# V. Two anthologies create different historical objects

The most useful comparison is not `which anthology is more accurate?`

It is:

### Tully 1988

Object:
`THE SKINNER METHODOLOGICAL CONTROVERSY`.

Selection question:
`Which Skinner essays and which criticisms best make that controversy legible?`

Output:
`SKINNER + SELECTED CRITICS + SKINNER REPLY`.

### Kelley 1990

Object:
`THE THEORY AND PRACTICE / CANON AND VARIATIONS OF HISTORY OF IDEAS`.

Selection question:
`Which JHI texts make a lineage from founders through followers to open questions legible?`

Output:
`LOVEJOY/JHI FOUNDERS + INTERNAL DEBATES + SUCCESSORS + QUESTIONS`.

Therefore:

`SAME HISTORICAL FIELD`
+
`DIFFERENT EDITORIAL PROBLEM`
-> `DIFFERENT CANON`.

This is the strongest current formulation of **canonization by problem selection**.

# VI. Why this matters for `canonical compression`

Earlier notes used `canonical compression` to describe how a distributed, reciprocal methodological ecology can become portable as a smaller set of exemplary texts and oppositions.

The Tully/Kelley comparison now specifies the mechanism more precisely.

Compression does not require one dominant institution to erase alternatives.

It can arise because different anthologies:

1. choose different objects of memory;
2. impose different organizing taxonomies;
3. make different texts portable;
4. leave different argumentative links outside the package;
5. are subsequently taught/cited as coherent wholes.

Thus:

`LIVE ARGUMENT ECOLOGY`
-> `MULTIPLE SELECTIVE PACKAGES`
-> `DIFFERENTIAL PORTABILITY`
-> `LATER SIMPLIFIED FIELD MEMORY`.

The clean Lovejoy/Skinner contrast may therefore be an **emergent effect of overlapping selective packages**, not the product of a single canonical decision.

---

# VII. This also explains why `forgotten` is too crude a category

Parekh/Berki, Wiener 1961, Richter 1987 and other branches are not literally lost.

They remain:

- indexed in journals;
- cited in some later scholarship;
- recoverable in bibliographies;
- occasionally taught in specialist courses;
- embedded in replies and retrospective discussions.

Their status is better described as:

`LOWER CANONICAL PORTABILITY`

rather than

`TOTAL FORGETTING`.

This distinction should be preserved in the article.

A text can be available yet structurally unlikely to enter the standard pedagogical/citation chain because it is absent from the most portable packages.

# IX. Article-level payoff

A strong later section can now be organized around **competing canon infrastructures** rather than a vague claim that disciplinary memory became simplified.

Possible paragraph sequence:

1. recover the reciprocal 1961–76 debate;
2. show 1987 JHI modular reassessment of Lovejoy;
3. show Tully 1988 packaging a Skinner-centered controversy;
4. show Kelley 1990 packaging a JHI-centered founders/followers/questions genealogy;
5. show that the same methodological links — especially Parekh/Berki, Wiener 1961 and Richter 1987 — fall outside one or both portable packages;
6. trace how later field histories inherit the compressed contrast.

Strongest current proposition:

> **The canonical opposition between Lovejoyian history of ideas and Cambridge contextualism was not simply announced after a methodological victory. It emerged amid competing late-twentieth-century efforts to package a far more distributed controversy into portable canons. Tully's Skinner-centered controversy and Kelley's JHI-centered genealogy selected different texts for different problems; their overlap made some actors exceptionally portable, while other argumentative links remained accessible but structurally peripheral.**
