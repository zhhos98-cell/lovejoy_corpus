## Central correction

The twenty-first-century afterlife should no longer be described as a single genealogy from Martin Guerre to digital identity. The evidence supports **two partially independent transmission histories that repeatedly intersect**:

### A. Concept transmission

`state information / passport / identification at distance`

→ `identification as power relation`

→ `plural historical regimes of identification`

→ `policy/legal distinctions among identification, authentication, biometric verification and identity matching`.

### B. Case portability

`Davis's Martin Guerre`

→ `imposture as identification failure`

→ `benchmark / anomaly / teaching object`

→ `retrofit into contemporary verification taxonomies`

→ `reinserted into policy, digital-ID, biometric and public-history discourse`.

The two histories intersect, but the case does not need to travel continuously with the concept. This distinction prevents an otherwise tempting but unsupported genealogy.

## 1. Noiriel: from identity to identification as a power relation

Gérard Noiriel's 1998 article `Surveiller les déplacements ou identifier les personnes ?` reconstructs a transformation of the French passport from a permission to move toward an instrument that certifies identity and enables the state to act upon a nationally defined population. The key issue is already institutional rather than metaphysical: the state requires practical means for attaching persons to categories, rights, territory and administrative records.

The methodological position becomes explicit in the presentation of the 2007 collective volume *L'Identification. Genèse d'un travail d'État*. The object of inquiry is no longer the endless definition of `identity` in different cultures but concrete practices and technologies of **identification at distance**, analyzed as relations of power connecting those who can define the identities of others with those made objects of those operations.

This is a decisive history-of-knowledge move because it changes both the unit and the explanatory grammar:

`What is identity?`

becomes

`Who identifies whom, through which technique, at what distance, for which institutional purpose, and with what power to make the result consequential?`

The object is no longer primarily selfhood. It is an **operation with an authorized asymmetry**.

Sources:
- Gérard Noiriel, `Surveiller les déplacements ou identifier les personnes ? Contribution à l'histoire du passeport en France de la Ire à la IIIe République`, *Genèses* 30 (1998), 77–100, DOI 10.3406/genes.1998.1497.
- Gérard Noiriel, dir., *L'Identification. Genèse d'un travail d'État* (Belin, 2007).

## 2. Higgs, 2007/08: Martin Guerre at the threshold of an autonomous identification problem

Edward Higgs's keynote `Are State-Mediated Forms of Identification a Reaction to Physical Mobility? The case of England, 1500–2007` was delivered into a striking institutional setting: the first IFIP working conference on **Policies and Research in Identity Management**, alongside papers on biometric encryption, multimodal biometrics, secure biometric authentication, privacy and strong authentication.

Higgs locates Davis's *The Return of Martin Guerre* beside work on Perkin Warbeck and the Tichborne Claimant and makes an exact distinction. In those histories, personal identification is important, but the works explain **how identification failed in particular cases of imposture**, rather than how different forms of identification developed over time.

That sentence catches Martin Guerre at an epistemic threshold. The case has already become legible to an emerging history of identification, but it has not yet become the field's explanatory object in its own right.

Higgs then questions the common equation:

`modernity = mobility = anonymity = identification`.

He argues that the history of state-mediated identification cannot be reduced to the collapse of intimate village recognition under urban mobility; changing relations among individuals, organizations and states have to be specified.

This matters because later Martin Guerre reuses often restore precisely the village/modern contrast that Higgs was trying to complicate.

Source:
- Edward Higgs, `Keynote Paper: Are State-Mediated Forms of Identification a Reaction to Physical Mobility? The case of England, 1500–2007`, in *Policies and Research in Identity Management*, IFIP 261 (Springer, 2008), 105–120.

## 3. About and Denis, 2010: disciplinary recoding into `régimes d'identification`

Ilsen About and Vincent Denis explicitly state that older works had addressed the problem without making `identification` an explicit category; Davis's Martin Guerre is one of their examples. Since the 1990s, they argue, identification has become an autonomous object across history and neighboring social sciences.

They decompose the operation into three functions:

- **singularisation** — establishing a person's distinctive uniqueness;
- **différenciation** — distinguishing one individual from another;
- **reconnaissance** — comparing known data/characteristics to establish that someone is the same person across time or space.

They then formulate `régime d'identification`: a historically specific configuration formed by the relations among naming, signs, dress, written/iconographic documents, recognition practices, actors, procedures and forms of authority. They explicitly adapt and complicate Noiriel's contrast between face-to-face identification and identification at distance.

This produces the clean conceptual chain:

`Noiriel: face-to-face / distance + power relation`

→ `About–Denis: plural configurations / regimes + changing actors, tools, procedures, resistance`.

Martin Guerre is reclassified inside this field as a precursor, but the concept of `régime d'identification` is not derived from Martin Guerre. The **case history and concept history remain distinguishable**.

Source:
- Ilsen About and Vincent Denis, *Histoire de l'identification des personnes* (La Découverte, 2010), Introduction, especially pp. 3–7.

## 4. The surprising technical retrofit: Gelb and Diofasi Metz, 2018

Alan Gelb and Anna Diofasi Metz's *Identification Revolution: Can Digital ID Be Harnessed for Development?* (Center for Global Development, 2018) gives the clearest evidence yet that Martin Guerre was not merely remembered in digital-ID discourse but **re-encoded in its technical vocabulary**.

Their introduction first uses the case as an example of the problem of validating a claimant against a known identity. Later it maps the evidence in the Guerre affair onto the familiar authentication-factor triad:

- appearance → `something you are`;
- testimony from Bertrande → `something you have`;
- detailed personal knowledge → `something you know`.

The authors' conclusion is that even multiple factors cannot entirely eliminate fraud or error. Immediately afterward, the book proposes three phases of identification systems:

`village` → `centralized national identification` → `federated / e-village`.

In the village phase, identification is established through personal connections, `who you know and who knows you`; centralized systems move the reference state into nationally administered records and credentials; the `e-village` draws authentication evidence from distributed official and private databases and online participation.

This is a remarkable epistemic operation: **a sixteenth-century evidentiary bundle is retrospectively parsed as if it were a modern authentication stack**.

That parsing is useful precisely because it is historically non-neutral. Bertrande's testimony is not literally a possession factor in the technical sense. Treating it as `have` flattens a socially situated witness relation into a standardized factor class. The case is made portable by reducing heterogeneous historical evidence to interoperable categories.

This gives us a direct example of what could be called **retrospective protocolization**:

`historically heterogeneous signs and relations`

→ `modern verifier categories`

→ `cross-period comparability`.

The operation creates analytical leverage while also producing historical loss.

Source:
- Alan Gelb and Anna Diofasi Metz, *Identification Revolution: Can Digital ID Be Harnessed for Development?* (Center for Global Development, 2018), Introduction.

## 5. A genuine historiographical fork: Higgs versus Gelb–Diofasi

The two works make Martin Guerre do almost opposite explanatory jobs.

### Higgs
Martin Guerre is an **identification failure case**. Higgs warns against the overly smooth modernization equation in which village interknowledge collapses under mobility and therefore produces state identification.

### Gelb–Diofasi
Martin Guerre becomes a **benchmark for staged identity-system development**, and the book explicitly organizes its model as village → centralized system → digital/e-village.

Thus Martin Guerre does not carry one settled lesson into the twenty-first century. It is an object through which competing models of historical change can be articulated.

This is important methodologically. A reception-history narrative might record both citations as evidence of continuing interest. A history of knowledge asks instead:

- what is the receiving discipline trying to make comparable?
- which variables are preserved?
- which relations are erased?
- which historical sequence does the case authorize?

The answer shows that **portability is compatible with contradictory inferential use**.

## 6. Daston, 2019: anti-teleological reuse

Lorraine Daston's `Classic Natalie` provides a third use. She assigns Davis to students habituated to documents, phones, biometrics, mirrors, selfies and social-media records in order to make the infrastructure of modern personal identity visible by subtraction.

Here Martin Guerre does not prove that modern identification is superior. It makes modern certainty strange.

Daston's operation is:

`remove continuous identification infrastructure`

→ `recover the contingency of what now feels self-evident`.

This is the opposite of reading the affair simply as a primitive technical deficit. The anomaly exposes the hidden supports of ordinary re-identification.

Combined with Higgs and Gelb–Diofasi, the same case now supports three distinguishable epistemic uses:

1. **failure case**;
2. **developmental benchmark**;
3. **defamiliarizing anomaly**.

## 7. 2020 French National Assembly: the two transmission histories intersect

The French National Assembly's 2020 report on digital identity is the clearest policy-level intersection.

Its historical periodization explicitly cites About and Denis. It moves from face-to-face recognition and signs, through written registration and state identification, toward mass/computerized identification. It separately cites Davis and inserts Martin Guerre at the point where the report turns from historically fallible interpersonal identification to identity usurpation.

The report then differentiates digital **identification** and **authentication**, discusses biometric comparison, remote enrolment and liveness proof.

The conceptual architecture is therefore braided:

`Noiriel → About/Denis → regime periodization`

plus

`Davis → Martin Guerre → memorable failure case`

→ **policy synthesis** → `digital identification / authentication / biometrics / liveness`.

The report is not evidence that one continuous textual chain carried Martin Guerre from Noiriel into parliament. It is stronger and more interesting evidence of **recombination**: a concept lineage and a case lineage become components in a new policy object.

## 8. Noiriel, 2021: the case is reinserted after the field has formed

A useful postscript comes from Gérard Noiriel himself. On 10 December 2021, his France Culture chronicle `Connaissez-vous Martin Guerre, l'homme qui avait perdu son identité ?` publicly retold the case, foregrounding the uncertainty over whether the returning claimant was the real Martin.

This does not prove that Martin Guerre originally generated Noiriel's identification programme. Chronology and source ownership point the other way: Noiriel's state-identification work was already established, About and Denis had already systematized the field, and the 2020 policy report had already integrated Martin Guerre into a digital-identity genealogy.

Instead, the 2021 broadcast documents **re-entry**. Once `identification` has become a mature analytical object, the famous case can be pulled back into the vocabulary of the field and presented publicly as a problem of losing/establishing identity.

This is exactly why the two-history model is preferable to a simple genealogy.

Source:
- Gérard Noiriel, `Connaissez-vous Martin Guerre, l'homme qui avait perdu son identité ?`, *Le Pourquoi du comment : histoire*, France Culture, 10 December 2021.

## 9. 2024: technical and legal categories become formally separated

By 2024, EU law supplies a highly explicit taxonomy of the operations that the earlier history-of-identification literature had taught us not to collapse.

The revised eIDAS framework, Regulation (EU) 2024/1183, defines:

- person identification data;
- electronic identification means;
- authentication as electronic confirmation of an electronic identification;
- identity matching as linking person-identification data/eID means to an existing account belonging to the same person;
- strong user authentication through at least two independent factors drawn from knowledge, possession and inherence.

The EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, separately distinguishes **biometric identification** from **biometric verification/authentication**. Identification compares biometric data against stored biometric data of individuals in a reference database to establish identity; verification confirms that a specific natural person is the person they claim to be for a bounded purpose such as access.

Neither regulation invokes Martin Guerre. That absence is analytically useful. It shows the concept lineage arriving at a formal legal taxonomy **without the historical case**.

The case remains portable in surrounding public, scholarly and policy discourse, while the legal apparatus stabilizes operational distinctions in actor vocabulary.

Sources:
- Regulation (EU) 2024/1183, European Digital Identity Framework.
- Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Artificial Intelligence Act.

## 10. The central history-of-knowledge finding: braided transmission

We should therefore replace the loose metaphor of `afterlife` with a more exact model.

### Concept genealogy

`passport / state information`

→ `identification at distance`

→ `power relation`

→ `régime d'identification`

→ `identification / authentication / verification / identity matching` as increasingly formal institutional categories.

### Case itinerary

`Davis's microhistorical affair`

→ `failure of recognition`

→ `precursor reclassification`

→ `technical benchmark`

→ `pedagogical anomaly`

→ `policy exemplum`

→ `public-history re-entry`.

### Points of intersection

- Higgs 2007/08: historical case placed inside an identity-management conference while remaining a failure case rather than a developmental theory.
- About–Denis 2010: case explicitly recoded as a precursor to an autonomous history of identification.
- Gelb–Diofasi 2018: case translated directly into technical authentication-factor vocabulary.
- French National Assembly 2020: regime history and Martin Guerre recombined inside digital-identity policy.

This is a **braided history of knowledge**, not a single lineage.

## 11. Retrospective protocolization as a new operator

The Gelb–Diofasi case suggests a reusable analytical operator.

### Definition

**Retrospective protocolization** = the re-description of historically heterogeneous evidence, practices or relations in terms of a later standardized decision protocol so that the old case can be compared with a contemporary system.

Martin Guerre provides an unusually visible instance:

`appearance + spouse testimony + intimate memory`

becomes

`inherence + possession + knowledge`.

The gain is portability. The loss is the historical specificity of witness authority, marriage, household memory, embodied familiarity and juridical proof.

This gives a sharper vocabulary than saying simply that later authors `modernize` or `anachronistically read` the case. Protocolization describes the exact epistemic operation: **heterogeneous relations are reassigned to a finite set of slots in a later verification architecture**.

## 12. Connection to the active `primitive` work: methodological only

This is particularly useful as a comparative control for the Lovejoy/`primitive` project because it demonstrates, in a very different archive, how non-isomorphic materials can be made portable by assigning them to a common relational schema.

The comparison is structural only:

`historically specific item`

→ `decomposition into relation-specific features`

→ `reassignment into a portable analytical protocol`

→ `reaggregation across heterogeneous cases`.

The Martin Guerre example also shows the danger: once reassignment succeeds, the new grid can make the old object appear naturally composed of the analyst's categories. That is precisely where source-level controls are required.

No influence, shared genealogy, or historical relation to Lovejoy is claimed.

## 14. Strongest compressed formulation

**Martin Guerre's twenty-first-century reappearance has two histories. One is a concept genealogy in which Noiriel's identification at distance and its power asymmetries are expanded by About and Denis into plural `régimes d'identification` and eventually intersect with policy and legal taxonomies that separate identification, authentication, biometric verification and identity matching. The other is a case itinerary in which Davis's microhistory is repeatedly reclassified: Higgs treats it as a failure case at the boundary of a new history of identification; About and Denis make it a precursor; Gelb and Diofasi Metz retrospectively protocolize its witnesses, bodily resemblance and intimate knowledge as modern authentication factors; Daston uses it to defamiliarize contemporary biometric certainty; and the French National Assembly recombines the famous failure case with the regime history inside digital-identity policy. The case and the concept therefore do not travel as one package. They separate, acquire different disciplinary meanings, and repeatedly rejoin. That braid is the actual twenty-first-century history of knowledge.**
