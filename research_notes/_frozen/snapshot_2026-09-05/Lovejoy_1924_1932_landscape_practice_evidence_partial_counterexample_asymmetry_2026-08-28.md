# Lovejoy 1924–1932 — landscape practice as historical evidence: partial counterexample to evidentiary asymmetry

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: **COUNTEREXAMPLE CONTROL / ASYMMETRY HYPOTHESIS WEAKENED BUT NOT FALSIFIED**

Primary controls:

- Arthur O. Lovejoy, `On the Discrimination of Romanticisms`, *PMLA* 39.2 (1924), 229–253;
- Arthur O. Lovejoy, `The First Gothic Revival and the Return to Nature`, *Modern Language Notes* 47.7 (1932), 419–446; reprinted in *Essays in the History of Ideas* (1948), 136–165.

Direct 1924 text is available through a public transcription/rehost; the 1932 article's exact argument is currently controlled through JSTOR bibliographic metadata, Wilson's annotated bibliography, and independently quoted passages in later architectural/literary scholarship. Publication-grade use of longer 1932 quotations should still be locked against the article/facsimile.

## Why this matters

The current critical hypothesis asks whether Lovejoy's causal ontology is wider than his evidentiary regime: perhaps textual/intellectual causes are easier for him to prove because they leave the kinds of explicit carriers his method prefers, while diffuse practice/social/institutional causes are harder to establish.

The landscape/Gothic sequence supplies an important counterexample.

Lovejoy does not confine the history of ideas to verbal doctrine. He explicitly treats:

- garden design;
- landscape taste;
- architecture;
- built/artificial landscapes;
- aesthetic practice

as historically probative provinces in which conceptual change becomes visible.

But the counterexample is only partial. Lovejoy still frequently redescribes the causal mechanism as the migration/application of **ideas** across those practices.

Thus:

> **practice is admissible evidence and a historical carrier in Lovejoy's method, but it is not yet clear that practice is granted an autonomous causal status independent of an idea-centered explanatory grammar.**

---

# I. 1924 — the first anti-classical revolt occurs in gardening, in theory and practice

In `On the Discrimination of Romanticisms`, Lovejoy says the aesthetic principle later visible in literature had already been applied to an art in whose **theory and practice** eighteenth-century Englishmen were deeply interested: landscape design.

He then makes the chronological claim:

> the first great revolt against neo-classical aesthetics was not in literature but in gardening; the second was in architectural taste.

The garden movement is represented through both discourse and practice:

- Addison's account of `artificial rudeness` and natural irregularity;
- Temple, Pope, Walpole, Batty Langley and others as advocates;
- Kent, Brown and Bridgeman as practitioners whose works **exemplified** the new horticultural aesthetic.

Thus the evidentiary set is not only:

`texts about gardening`.

It also includes:

`designed landscapes / practical exemplifications of taste`.

### Direct methodological consequence

Lovejoy's historical chronology can be established through an art/practice domain prior to literary articulation.

This blocks the strongest version of the evidentiary-asymmetry hypothesis in which only textual propositions can become historically probative.

---

# II. But Lovejoy still describes the cross-domain change as idea-driven

The same 1924 passage says gardening, architecture and literature were **inspired by the same ideas**.

The explanatory syntax is therefore approximately:

`idea(s) about nature / irregularity / artifice`
→ early application in garden theory/practice
→ architectural taste
→ literary form/taste.

Practice reveals and instantiates the conceptual transformation, but Lovejoy does not clearly say:

`material garden practice itself generated the idea`.

This is an important limit.

The case demonstrates **evidentiary openness to practice**, not necessarily a non-ideational causal ontology of practice.

---

# III. 1932 — artificial landscape design becomes herald/precursor of Gothic revival

Wilson's summary of `The First Gothic Revival and the Return to Nature` records Lovejoy's central thesis:

- early-eighteenth-century Gothic architecture was disparaged because prevailing aesthetic standards identified `natural` form with simplicity, regularity and symmetry;
- by roughly 1740 a Gothic revival was established;
- this reversal was made possible by a changed conception of what counted as natural;
- **the beginnings of the change occurred in landscape architecture**, before the new conception of nature was gradually applied to architecture.

A passage independently quoted in later scholarship gives Lovejoy's formulation that the earliest Gothic Revival had as its herald/precursor:

- the new fashion in designing artificial landscapes;
- the new liking for wilderness, boldness, broken contours and boundless natural prospects.

### Evidentiary significance

The chronological and explanatory sequence uses changes in designed landscapes and taste as historical evidence for the prior emergence of a new aesthetic norm.

The physical/design field is not decorative context. It helps date and locate the transformation.

---

# IV. The causal relation is nevertheless hybrid

The 1932 argument should not be reduced to either:

`ideas cause practices`

or

`practices cause ideas`.

The visible architecture is closer to:

`semantic shift in NATURE / aesthetic norm`
↔ `garden theory and landscape design practice`
→ `reclassification of Gothic architecture as natural`
→ `new architectural taste / revival`.

There is reciprocal reinforcement among:

- semantic/conceptual change;
- aesthetic judgment;
- design practice;
- built examples;
- verbal theories and descriptions.

Lovejoy's own prose, however, appears to privilege the semantic/conceptual level when explaining what made the Gothic revaluation possible.

Thus this is a **hybrid carrier/configuration case**, not strong evidence of autonomous material causation.

---

# V. Great Chain methodological self-description confirms the admissibility of practice

Later discussion of Lovejoy's methodological introduction repeatedly cites his point that landscape gardening may look remote from philosophy, yet at one point its history becomes part of a genuinely philosophical history of modern thought.

This matters because it identifies a non-canonical archive of ideas:

- gardens;
- landscape forms;
- changing design conventions;
- aesthetic practices.

The historian of ideas is therefore not confined to philosophical treatises.

But the rationale remains that such practices register or embody transformations relevant to philosophical/aesthetic ideas.

---

# VI. Revised evidentiary-accessibility hypothesis

The landscape case requires a correction to the earlier asymmetry hypothesis.

### Too strong

`Lovejoy's method privileges textual carriers so strongly that non-textual practice lacks a viable route into historical explanation.`

### Replace with

> **Lovejoy admits practices and designed environments as evidence, chronological markers and carriers/embodiments of conceptual change. The remaining question is whether he allows such practices to function as autonomous causal mechanisms, or mainly translates their historical significance back into an idea-centered vocabulary of semantic change, application and exemplification.**

This is a much narrower and more testable hypothesis.

---

# VII. New distinction: evidentiary carrier vs causal engine

The case suggests separating two questions previously blurred together.

## EVIDENTIARY CARRIER

What kind of historical object can show that a transformation has occurred?

Lovejoy clearly admits:

- texts;
- words;
- arguments;
- garden designs;
- architecture;
- aesthetic practices.

## CAUSAL ENGINE

What is said to produce the transformation?

Possible answers include:

- ideas/semantic shifts;
- psychological association;
- social structure;
- institutional incentives;
- material practice;
- reciprocal interaction among them.

In the landscape/Gothic case, non-textual practice is a strong carrier, while the causal engine remains at least partly ideational.

This distinction should govern future counterexample searches.

---

# VIII. Implication for the two-axis matrix

The existing evidence matrix should now distinguish **carrier** from **cause**.

A garden can be:

- `PRACT` evidence that an aesthetic norm is operative;
- a carrier through which the norm becomes publicly instantiated;
- a site of downstream diffusion;
- without necessarily being the ultimate causal origin of that norm.

Thus do not infer:

`non-textual source used`
→ `non-ideational causal explanation`.

The relevant coding becomes:

`relation type` × `evidence/carrier type` × `claimed causal engine`.

---

# IX. Counterexample status

### What this case falsifies

- Lovejoy only treats written texts as historical evidence;
- Lovejoy cannot place practical/design change prior to literary change;
- material/built artifacts are absent from his history-of-ideas archive.

### What it does not yet falsify

- Lovejoy may still privilege ideas/semantic change as the ultimate explanatory engine;
- non-textual causes may still face higher mediation burdens than intellectual causes;
- institutional/social/material structures may remain harder to convert into causal explanation than designed artifacts understood as manifestations of taste.

---

# X. Next counterexample tests

1. Search Lovejoy for cases where **institutional structure**, not merely aesthetic practice, positively causes an intellectual configuration.
2. Search for positive explanations from **professional organization / education / social position / economic practice** without actor-explicit causal testimony.
3. Compare garden/architecture cases with the 1937 Trinkhaus class-structure dispute.
4. Distinguish `practice as embodiment/evidence` from `practice as causal generator` sentence by sentence.
5. Search `Herder and the Enlightenment Philosophy of History`, `Nature as Aesthetic Norm`, and Lovejoy's academic-freedom/institutional writings for explicit institutional causation.

## Current shorthand

> **Landscape gardening is a real counterexample to a text-only Lovejoy: practical and built forms can date, embody and transmit aesthetic change. But Lovejoy often explains their significance as applications of a changing idea of nature. The open asymmetry is therefore not text versus practice; it is whether different causal engines receive equally usable historical warrants.**
