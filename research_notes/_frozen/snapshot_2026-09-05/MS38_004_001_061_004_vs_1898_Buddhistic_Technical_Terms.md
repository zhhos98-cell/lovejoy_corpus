# MS38_004_001_061_004 and Lovejoy's 1898 Buddhism article

## Identification

The direct published counterpart to notebook `MS38_004_001_061_004` is Arthur Oncken Lovejoy, **“The Buddhistic Technical Terms upādāna and upādisesa,”** *Journal of the American Oriental Society* 19 (Second Half, 1898), 126–136. The published article identifies Lovejoy with Harvard University. A full-text copy is already stored in this repository as `The Buddhistic technical terms upadana and upadisesa..html`.

The relationship is extremely close. The notebook is broader than the article, but its later sections contain the reading apparatus, source comparisons, technical problems, and in several cases the argumentative sequence that become the 1898 publication. The safest present description is **preparatory or contemporaneous working notebook for the 1898 article and for a broader unrealized study of Sāṁkhya/Buddhism**. Exact notebook dating has not yet been established from archival metadata, so “draft notebook for the article” should remain a hypothesis rather than a cataloguing assertion.

The dating window is nevertheless narrow on internal evidence: the notebook and article engage Émile Senart's 1896 essay on the twelve nidānas and Henry Clarke Warren's *Buddhism in Translations* (1896), while the article appeared in 1898.

## 1. What passes directly from notebook into article

### A. Upādāna is not an abbreviation for the five skandhas

The notebook's pp. 47–54 and 55–71 repeatedly tests Senart's claim that `upādāna` is effectively a collective designation or abbreviation for `upādānakkhandha`. Lovejoy works through the resulting problem of apparent duplication: the skandhas already appear under several nidānas, so identifying upādāna with the skandhas would make the formula repetitious.

The published article makes this dispute its opening polemical target. It cites Senart's 1896 essay, reconstructs the argument from `upādānakkhandha`, then rejects the identification through passages from the *Dhamma-cakkappavattana Sutta* and especially the *Khandha Saṁyutta*. The article's distinction is the mature version of the notebook's working problem: the skandhas are **objects/conditions subject to attachment**, whereas upādāna is the **attachment itself**.

### B. The notebook's khandha–nidāna mapping becomes the article's causal interpretation

Notebook pp. 19–54 painstakingly map the twelve nidānas, then overlay them with the five khandhas. The notebook explicitly notices that nāmarūpa, viññāṇa, vedanā, saṅkhāra and rūpa belong to overlapping classificatory systems and asks whether the chain is temporal, logical, psychological, karmic, or some mixture of these.

The article condenses this into a three-part interpretation of `paticca-samuppāda`: links 1–2 are associated with past existence, 3–9 with present existence, and 10–12 with future existence. It explicitly says the sequence is **not a strict temporal sequence**. The published argument then assigns upādāna a precise function at the transition: karma explains the particular condition of rebirth, while upādāna explains the fact of rebirth *as such*.

This is a major transformation from notebook to article: the sprawling classificatory work is compressed into a single intelligibility claim about the causal formula.

### C. “Composite” does not mean “meaningless”

One of the clearest continuities is Lovejoy's response to Senart. In the notebook he repeatedly accepts that the nidāna formula contains heterogeneous, historically inherited categories and may not be homogeneous as a temporal sequence, while resisting the inference that its order is accidental or unintelligible.

The published article sharpens this into a direct rebuttal of Senart's “shreds and patches” interpretation. Lovejoy grants that the terminology, and perhaps even some ideas, are largely borrowed, but argues that their **arrangement and application are original and characteristic**. He insists that the formula retains a distinguishable and important meaning.

This is one of the strongest methodological links between the notebook and Lovejoy's later history-of-ideas practice: historical compositeness and analytical intelligibility are treated as compatible rather than mutually exclusive.

### D. Flux / Three Characteristics survive as the interpretive key

The notebook ends with its own historical-structural outline:

- Original elements: conception of flux as the reason for suffering (= Three Characteristics); formula of Dependent Origination.
- Derived elements: theory of sense-perception; psychology; khandhas.

The article does not reproduce this original/derived list, but it preserves its core hierarchy. Lovejoy argues that impermanence is the fact which explains the failure of upādāna to secure stable satisfaction, and that the Three Characteristics provide the complementary commentary on the middle section of dependent origination. He goes so far as to say that `paticca-samuppāda` plus `tilakkhaṇa` constitute nearly all of Buddhism's indispensable theoretical equipment.

Thus the notebook's final outline is not an abandoned tangent. Its first half survives almost verbatim as the conceptual center of the article; the “derived elements” half is mostly suppressed.

### E. Upādisesa as ethical residue, not physical survival of the skandhas

Notebook pp. 59–71 become the second half of the article with striking fidelity. The notebook tests `upādi/upādhi/upādisesa/anupādisesa` through Oldenberg, Childers, Rhys Davids, Sutta-Nipāta, Saṁyutta-Nikāya and Buddhaghosa. Its key move is to reject a purely physical reading in which `upādisesa` means “the five skandhas remaining.” Instead it increasingly treats the residue as ethical: remaining attachment, fetter, or impurity.

The article adopts exactly this line. It argues that in Sutta-Nipāta passages `upādisesa` distinguishes the Anāgāmin from the Arahat and that `anupādisesa` denotes the moral condition of complete freedom from attachment rather than physical death. The notebook sentence that treating death itself as the *summum bonum* would be profoundly un-Buddhist reappears in the published argument in essentially the same argumentative position.

The publication does, however, end cautiously: Lovejoy acknowledges passages favoring the conventional interpretation and explicitly says the matter is not finally settled. The notebook's much longer etymological and textual apparatus is therefore converted into a narrower provisional thesis.

## 2. What the 1898 article leaves out

The omissions are as revealing as the continuities.

### A. Most of the Sāṁkhya/Buddhism chronology disappears

The notebook begins with Sāṁkhya epistemology, pramāṇa, Buddhist agnosticism, the chronology of Sāṁkhya and Buddhism, Brahma-jāla heresies, and repeated warnings against projecting a mature Sāṁkhya system backward from later texts. Almost all of this disappears from the 1898 article.

Sāṁkhya survives only locally, especially in the discussion of Sanskrit `upādhi` and in contrasts with Vedānta. The published object is therefore much narrower than the notebook label “Sankhya + Buddhism” suggests.

### B. The large psychological taxonomy is compressed away

The notebook devotes substantial space to rūpa, vedanā, saññā, saṅkhāra, viññāṇa, the six sensory channels, the 52 saṅkhāras, and diagrams connecting khandhas to nidānas. The article refers to Buddhist psychology and to the obscure presence of the skandhas within nidānas 1–8, but explicitly says it cannot review that analysis there.

This is direct evidence that the notebook preserves an analytical layer excluded for publication rather than material irrelevant to the paper.

### C. The “original versus derived elements” historical model is muted

The notebook closes by distinguishing original from derived components. The article instead presents the system synchronically enough to resolve the two technical terms, while allowing only controlled historical remarks such as “pre-Buddhistic” karma terminology, borrowed terms, and Vedāntic ideas.

This difference matters. The notebook is already asking a history-of-concepts question about **stratification and accretion**; the article is a philological intervention whose published burden of proof is narrower.

### D. The notebook's stronger Nirvāṇa conclusions are softened

The notebook at one point states that the final Buddhist goal is absolute cessation of existence, while simultaneously distinguishing this question from the technical meaning of `upādisesa`. The article concentrates on the latter and avoids making the stronger metaphysical thesis its main conclusion. This looks like deliberate argumentative pruning: the technical philological claim is publishable without settling the entire Nirvāṇa controversy.

## 3. Manuscript → publication transformation

| Notebook operation | Published operation |
|---|---|
| Broad Sāṁkhya/Buddhism comparison | Narrow technical article on two terms |
| Collect competing definitions and source passages | Turn them into a polemic against Senart / conventional readings |
| Map nidānas onto khandhas | Condense into a three-stage causal interpretation |
| Ask whether heterogeneous categories make the chain incoherent | Argue that borrowed materials can have an original arrangement |
| Distinguish original and derived doctrinal elements | Retain flux + dependent origination as theoretical core, suppress much of the derivation thesis |
| Explore Nirvāṇa, parinirvāṇa, residue, death, aggregates | Limit conclusion chiefly to ethical meaning of upādisesa/anupādisesa |
| Keep contradictory passages and etymologies in view | End with an explicitly provisional conclusion |

## 4. Why this matters for Lovejoy's intellectual history

The direct notebook–article comparison gives a much firmer basis for discussing an early Lovejoyan method than comparison with *The Great Chain of Being* alone.

Three operations are already visible in 1898:

1. **Decomposition.** A philosophical system is not taken as an indivisible whole. Lovejoy separates terms, classifications, historical strata, and causal functions.
2. **Migration / borrowing without identity.** Concepts and technical vocabulary may be inherited from pre-Buddhist, Sāṁkhya, Vedāntic, or general Indic contexts without making Buddhism reducible to those systems.
3. **Recomposition into intelligibility.** The fact that a system is historically composite does not make it a meaningless heap. The historian/analyst must recover what is distinctive in the arrangement and application of inherited materials.

These are not yet the fully articulated method of “unit-ideas.” The notebook–article pair nevertheless makes it possible to argue, with primary evidence, that a recognizable **analytical habit of decomposition and recomposition** was present in Lovejoy's Harvard-period scholarship decades before the canonical formulations of the history of ideas.

## 5. Dating and evidential status

**Secure:**
- Article published in 1898, *JAOS* 19, pp. 126–136, under Lovejoy's Harvard affiliation.
- Notebook contains extensive material that appears in the article's exact source constellation and argumentative structure.
- Both engage Senart (1896) and Warren (1896).

**Strong inference:**
- At least the later portions of `MS38_004_001_061_004` are preparatory or contemporaneous working notes for the 1898 article.

**Not yet secure:**
- Exact dates of individual notebook entries.
- Whether the whole notebook was compiled specifically for the article, or whether the article was extracted from a broader Sāṁkhya/Buddhism project.
- Whether Lovejoy intended a larger publication based on the material omitted from the article.

The second possibility currently fits the evidence best: the article looks like a sharply delimited extraction from a broader research notebook.
