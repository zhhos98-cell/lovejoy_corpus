## 1. Direct source: R. Spence Hardy 1853, pp.394–396

Primary object: R. Spence Hardy, *A Manual of Budhism, in its Modern Development* (London: Partridge and Oakey, 1853), printed pp.394–396.

Hardy defines `upādāna` as cleaving to existing objects and treats two factors in post-mortem continuity: `upādāna` and karma.

The decisive p.394 formulation is that by `upādāna` a new existence is produced, while **the manner of its operation is controlled by the karma with which it is connected**. Destruction of `upādāna` secures cessation. Hardy also acknowledges instability in causal wording: some passages make `upādāna` look like the efficient cause of reproduction, others karma.

The practical division of labour is nevertheless clear:

- `upādāna` → renewed existence / continuation;
- karma → specification of the renewed state.

On p.394 Hardy lists broad classes of renewed existence; p.395 extends karma's specification to sex, status, appearance and condition within human rebirth; p.396 restates the relation as karma producing new existence in conjunction with `upādāna`.

Hardy therefore already has a two-factor causal architecture. Lovejoy does not invent the bifurcation.

## 2. Notebook 004 transforms Hardy into an analytic question

Notebook 004 PDF p.33 / manuscript p.97 is headed `Upādāna, Relation to Karma` and explicitly cites `Hardy, p.394` after a question that can be securely reconstructed at the level of opposition:

- `upādāna` as cause of the **fact** of continued existence;
- karma as cause of the **manner** of continued existence.

The exact grammar is transcriptionally less secure than the source citation and conceptual opposition, but `fact` versus `manner` is clear.

This is not merely copying Hardy. Hardy's own causal description remains partly unstable; the notebook converts it into a sharper allocation problem.

Hardy is also cited elsewhere in 004, including p.386 in the `saṅkhāra = karma` discussion, confirming that the *Manual* belongs to Lovejoy's active working apparatus.

The observable transformation is:

`Hardy descriptive two-factor model` → `Lovejoy fact/manner analytic distinction`.

## 3. The best historical comparison is branching from a shared source

### Hardy → Jacobi 1898: assimilation

Jacobi quotes Hardy p.394 and uses the passage toward a Sāṃkhya-Yoga / `adṛṣṭa` construction. He treats `upādāna` as containing passions or sinful dispositions through which karmic effects operate and new karma is generated.

The shared Hardy passage is therefore used to bring `upādāna` toward karma / `adṛṣṭa` and support a systematic-genealogical analogy.

### Hardy → 004 → Lovejoy 1898: differentiation

Lovejoy moves in the opposite direction. The notebook turns Hardy into `fact` versus `manner`; the published article stabilizes the distinction as:

- karma → the **particular condition** in which rebirth occurs;
- `upādāna` → the **fact of rebirth as such**.

Lovejoy then tests `upādāna` independently through Buddhist textual usage and defines it as attachment / a settled condition of will and interest toward the skandhas. Thus two causes of rebirth are assigned different semantic and explanatory jobs.

### Oldenberg 1898: lexical-functional control against assimilation

Oldenberg rejects Jacobi's `adṛṣṭa` construction and reconstructs `upādāna` from Buddhist usage. His `phassa → vedanā → taṇhā → upādāna` sequence treats contact, feeling, desire and grasping as distinct stages rather than synonyms.

The resulting source field is not a priority contest but a branch:

`Hardy p.394`

→ Jacobi: systematic/genealogical assimilation toward karma / `adṛṣṭa`

→ Lovejoy: differentiated causal allocation

while Oldenberg independently supplies a strong lexical-functional objection to the assimilative route.

## 4. Lovejoy's stronger 1898 increment is historical-functional recomposition

The published article adds more than a clean restatement of Hardy.

### A. Preserve the inherited two-factor architecture

The notebook's `fact/manner` distinction becomes `fact of rebirth` versus `particular condition of rebirth`.

### B. Separate semantic identity from causal co-participation

`upādāna` and karma can both bear on rebirth without being conceptually identical. Lovejoy's textual argument defines attachment through its relation to the skandhas and distinguishes it from karma.

### C. Explain historically why the two terms have different jobs

This is the strongest increment beyond Hardy. Lovejoy argues that karma entered Buddhism with an older moral-causal function: it explained better or worse future states while presupposing continued rebirth. Buddhism's distinctive problem is that rebirth itself can cease. Once continued existence becomes contingent, the **fact of rebirth as such** needs a causal explanation separate from the category that distributes the condition of a future state.

The resulting configuration is:

> inherited `karma` explains **which** rebirth; `upādāna` explains **whether** renewed existence occurs at all.

Project shorthand: **historical-functional recomposition**. This is analytical vocabulary, not Lovejoy's phrase.

## 6. Relation to 1902

`Religion and the Time-Process` (1902) explicitly cites Lovejoy's 1898 Buddhist paper and moves analogous evidentiary separation to a larger scale.

Two controls matter:

- conceptual resemblance between Aristotle and Vedānta does not establish eastern borrowing when an internal Greek genealogy suffices;
- recurrent categories/presuppositions can be tracked while their provenance, logical role and combinations remain analytically distinct.

The continuity is therefore not that Hardy's causal doctrine survives into 1902. It is an explanatory architecture:

`1898: provenance of a category ≠ its current causal function`

→

`1902: conceptual affinity ≠ demonstrated historical transmission; recurrent category ≠ identical historical configuration`.

This supports the project's broader division between genealogy/transmission and function/configuration without treating 004 as proto-`unit-ideas`.

## 7. Important negative correction: `Home Library Group 32`

Wilson's shorthand `A. O. Lovejoy H[ome] L[ibrary] Group 32` refers to Lovejoy's work in the Boston Children's Aid Society Home Library program, not a private book inventory.

It has no evidentiary value for ownership of Hardy, Oldenberg, Warren, Senart or other Indological books. Do not reactivate the earlier personal-library interpretation.
