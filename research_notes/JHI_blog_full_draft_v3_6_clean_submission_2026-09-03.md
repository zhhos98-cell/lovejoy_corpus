The bibliography appended to Arthur O. Lovejoy’s [*Essays in the History of Ideas*](https://www.press.jhu.edu/books/title/5183/essays-history-ideas) gives him an unlikely beginning.[^1] Its first entry is an [1898 article on two Buddhist technical terms](https://dlbs.liberal.ntu.edu.tw/FULLTEXT/JR-ENG/love.htm)—not a precursor to *The Great Chain of Being*, not a statement of the unit-idea program, but a piece of specialist Buddhist scholarship. The impression is false: Lovejoy had already published an essay on Lord Monboddo in 1895.[^1] Yet the false beginning is useful. Before we have learned much about the young Lovejoy, a later bibliography has already arranged his career for us.

Nor was this Buddhist episode simply forgotten. Daniel J. Wilson documented Lovejoy’s comparative-religion training, Sanskrit and Buddhist studies, and later work in Paris; Simon Schaffer could still [note in 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/007327531004800309) that Lovejoy studied Pāli and Sanskrit there (484). Wilson’s archival transcriptions, however, preserve something more useful than the biographical fact. Writing from Auxerre on 20 October 1898, Lovejoy proposed “to elucidate the theoretical presuppositions and doctrines of Buddhism from a philosophical point of view”: philologists, he complained, had expounded Buddhist literature while caring little for philosophy, whereas philosophers had reconstructed Buddhist conceptions “more or less out of their own heads” while neglecting “the technicalities of the texts.” He hoped to study with Sylvain Lévi for precisely this purpose; by December, when no Pāli Buddhism course was being offered, he expected instead to read Buddhist texts with Lévi privately.[^2] This was not a mature methodological manifesto. It was a young scholar naming a practical jurisdictional problem—textual control on one side, philosophical reconstruction on the other—while trying to work across it.

Among Lovejoy’s papers at Johns Hopkins is a Harvard Cooperative Society notebook labeled in his hand “Sankhya + Buddhism.”[^3] It does not preserve a lost version of his mature method, still less a Buddhist origin for the history of ideas. What it preserves is more concrete: repeated disputes over what particular evidence could establish about a term, a sequence, a cause, a borrowing, or a historical connection. The October letter names a gap already visible in Lovejoy’s pre-Paris Buddhist work; the notebook lets us inspect the local operations by which he worked within it.

One of the clearest begins with [Henry Clarke Warren](https://buddhadust.net/resources/pdf/warren/warren-on-p-s.pdf). Warren had warned that the Buddhist formula of dependent origination should not be treated as a simple “chain of causation”: its links expressed different kinds of dependence, and each had to be examined separately (xxvii). Yet when he reached *taṇhā* (desire) and *upādāna* (attachment), he treated their relation as one of identity. Lovejoy copied the judgment and interrupted it: “But not so if we are to follow the present text” (“Sankhya + Buddhism,” MS p. 75). He then compared Buddhist classifications—four kinds of attachment, six forms of desire—to ask whether the textual evidence could sustain Warren’s equation. There is no invention here of a general principle; Warren had already supplied the problem. Lovejoy’s disagreement is narrower, and more revealing for that reason: one proposed relation has to earn its warrant from the evidence appropriate to it.

With Émile Senart, the scale expands from two terms to the historical composition of a Buddhist formula. In [“À propos de la théorie bouddhique des douze nidânas”](https://play.google.com/store/books/details/M%C3%A9langes_Charles_de_Harlez?id=g849OKp_GrsC), Senart argued that the twelve *nidānas* contained heterogeneous categories assembled from different sources; repetition and mixture could therefore indicate secondary construction (284–88). Lovejoy concedes much of the premise: the terms need not all be originally Buddhist, the series is not one homogeneous temporal sequence, and materials may have been borrowed. What he resists is the further inference that the resulting arrangement has thereby been shown accidental or unintelligible. Hermann Oldenberg had already challenged important parts of Senart’s semantic case in the third edition of [*Buddha*](https://books.google.com/books?id=ta7rJ6uWLfwC) (272–73, 443–55), so Lovejoy’s significance is not priority. It lies instead in the local redistribution of burden: evidence about where components came from does not, by itself, settle what they do once combined.

The path into print now makes that redistribution visible at more than one scale. On 10 April 1897 an American Oriental Society circular announced Lovejoy’s paper as a “Critical summary of the argument of the Milinda-pañha”; the final record of the Baltimore meeting, where the absent Lovejoy’s paper was read by title, listed instead “On the meaning of the Buddhist technical terms ...”; in 1898 the article appeared as “The Buddhistic Technical Terms Upadana and Upadisesa.”[^4] The sequence does not prove that one unchanged manuscript was renamed twice, but it does document a public-facing contraction from a whole-text argument to technical-term meaning before the final two-term article. In print Lovejoy still allows that Senart’s terms, “perhaps even the ideas,” may be largely borrowed while their “arrangement and application” remain original and characteristic (127, 132). Publication, then, did not simply polish the notebook: it selected a smaller object and asked fewer relations to bear the weight of the argument.

Across the manuscript, the same materials are repeatedly reopened under different questions. Lovejoy asks whether two terms are identical in use, what causal work a term performs, what comes before what in time, what belongs inside what as a classification, where a doctrine or term came from, and what a borrowed element does once placed in a larger arrangement. An answer to one question does not automatically answer the others. One page makes this especially visible. On manuscript p. 123, Lovejoy places *viññāṇa* within the *nāma* side of a classification while writing beside it that “Viññāṇa is temporally (?) an antecedent of nāmarūpa, and logically a subdivision of it.” Forced onto one axis, the statements look awkward; on the page they answer different questions. The same term can occupy one position in a classification and another in a sequence. The point is modest but consequential: a working sheet can preserve non-identical relations without requiring one to collapse into the other.

The treatment of *upādisesa* shows what happened when such working problems entered print. Lovejoy moves among competing etymologies, textual passages, and stronger possibilities about the term’s relation to death and Nirvāṇa, but the published article explicitly limits what etymology can establish: the meaning of *upādi*, he writes, must be settled “rather by an examination of its use than by etymological arguments” (134). Contrary passages remain; some evidence is “relevant but not necessarily conclusive”; the question, he concludes, “cannot be regarded as finally settled” (136). A local semantic claim can therefore close while a larger historical or etymological claim remains open. The printed paper is not merely cleaner than the notes; it is narrower in what it undertakes to prove.

Harvard helps explain why one student was moving so easily between close Buddhist textual work and broader comparison. Lovejoy’s individual graduate record shows that in 1896–97 he took `Sanskrit 5` and `Semitic 13`, receiving an A in both; the contemporaneous course announcements identify course 5 as Charles Rockwell Lanman’s advanced Pāli course, “The Sacred Books of Buddhism,” reading the *Dhammapada*, *Sutta Nipāta*, and *Majjhima Nikāya*, and Semitic 13 as Crawford H. Toy’s “History of the Hebrew Religion, with comparison of other Semitic religions.” Charles Carroll Everett’s comparative study of religion, including Buddhism and Hindu philosophies, occupied the same graduate environment.[^5] Harvard did not transmit one unified “method.” It juxtaposed distinct jurisdictions—philological source work, comparison among related traditions, philosophy and comparative religion—which Lovejoy traversed and, by 1898, could himself describe as uneasily divided.

A second Lovejoy notebook changes the material register. In notebook 005, with a strong Paris 1898–99 core but later reuse as well, the objects are no longer primarily Pāli terms but death, ritual, afterlife, sacrifice, and social practice.[^6] A differentiated fate after death cannot simply be called moral reward or punishment because it looks like one: the notebook tests manner of death, ritual provision, social status, kinship or group relation, taboo, imported moralization, and moral conduct; sacrifice, blood, fetish, charm, cannibalism, and agrarian rites are likewise repeatedly reopened under rival mechanisms and genealogies. Much of this conceptual apparatus is source-owned—Marillier, Robertson Smith, Frazer, Tylor, and others already supplied many of the component classifications—but the surviving Lovejoy-local work lies in ranking sources, reclassifying cases, mapping mechanisms to social bearers, rejecting rival genealogies, testing historical pathways, and sometimes leaving alternatives unresolved. Inserted slips, branching schemes, and local revisions make that work visible on the page. The point is again not originality but inference: what does a visible form actually warrant us to say about cause, function, history, or bearer?

Lovejoy carried related distinctions beyond the Buddhist case. In [“Religion and the Time-Process”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3153919) (1902), he sent readers back to his earlier Buddhist article while discussing wider comparisons. The affinity between Aristotle’s theology and Vedānta, he wrote, was “obvious”; the theory that Aristotle therefore came under eastern influence was nevertheless “entirely gratuitous,” because the internal development of the Platonic dialectic supplied an account of the Greek position (446–47). Resemblance did not have to become genealogy. This is not evidence that a Buddhist “method” migrated intact; it is evidence that comparison and historical transmission could remain different claims even when they concerned the same resemblance.

In 1906 the stakes of such distinctions became overtly political. Writing on “Democracy in the Twentieth Century,” Lovejoy described social-evolutionary stages and racial or national difference as having split Enlightenment “generic Man” “in two directions—longitudinally and cross-wise” (94). The axes were unequal: institutions fitted to one stage of culture were, he said, certainly unfitted to an earlier one, whereas racial or national difference might make institutions inappropriate elsewhere but did not have to; he also warned that race had become an overworked category (94–95). Yet the distinctions entered arguments about colonial policy and franchise. On p. 102 he regretted the previous generation’s “hasty” enfranchisement of Black Americans and sympathized with attempts to disfranchise illiterate Black voters, while withholding final judgment on those cases (101–2). Finer classification was therefore no guarantee of political innocence: a more differentiated account of humanity could become a grammar for deciding who was fitted for what institutions and when.

The rest of 1906 blocks any tidy story in which early philological caution simply gave way to later abstraction. In [“Kant’s Antithesis of Dogmatism and Criticism”](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/XV.58.191), Lovejoy tested a broad inherited contrast against local propositions and rejected the classification when it failed. Reviewing Paul Elmer More’s *Shelburne Essays*, he returned to the *Sutta Nipāta*—the same source he had used in 1898 to adjudicate Buddhist technical meaning—to resist an over-positive account of Nirvāṇa. Yet in [“The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy”](https://doi.org/10.5840/monist190616324) he moved in the opposite scalar direction: heterogeneous ethnographic and lexical materials were separated from spirit-centered interpretations; an impersonal, communicable, quasi-mechanical efficacy was abstracted across historically independent fields; and that analyst-constructed relation, now supplied with lexical warrant, was returned at a larger address as a “fundamental concept” of “primitive philosophy,” even “savage thought,” while Lovejoy simultaneously acknowledged that his evidence was incomplete and his initial statement overly categorical. Decomposition and totalization were thus not successive stages. Relation-sensitive checking could therefore support opposite scalar outcomes: block one aggregate category, or isolate a relation portable enough to build another.

By the time Lovejoy published [*The Great Chain of Being*](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL7692618M/The_Great_Chain_of_Being), Asian materials had not disappeared, but they occupied a different place. The preface defines the book’s principal historical object through “Occidental philosophy” and persistent presuppositions of “Western thought”; Buddhism and Vedānta remain visible in its account of otherworldliness (30), “primitive Buddhism” recurs as a comparative form (97), and at p. 35 Plato becomes the principal source of the “indigenous” Occidental strain of otherworldliness, distinguished from “imported Oriental varieties.” The narrative center is therefore Western without the Asian material vanishing. In the notebooks of the 1890s, Buddhist and Indian materials had themselves been local textual, semantic, causal, and genealogical problems; in *Great Chain*, they more often serve as comparative types or boundaries inside a predominantly Occidental genealogy. The change is one of historical function and address, not simple presence or absence.

Returning from the mature book to the notebooks therefore does not reveal a forgotten Buddhist origin of the history of ideas. Wilson had already preserved the early training, the 1898 article, and Paris; Schaffer could preserve the Pāli and Sanskrit fact while telling a different story through evolution, hierarchy, seriality, and *Great Chain*. The formation was known but differently classified. What the notebooks alter is its evidentiary status: they let us reconstruct the scholarly work performed inside a biographical episode that later histories could mention without making analytically central, while the newly recovered Wilson witness shows that the gap between textual expertise and philosophical reconstruction was also one Lovejoy himself articulated while the work was under way.

The odd first item in the *Essays* bibliography thus looks different at the end. A scholarly career is arranged more than once: notes break materials into working problems; institutional records change the scale at which those problems become public; publication selects claims an author is prepared to defend; later essays can move the same inferential habits across new objects and political stakes; mature books rearrange earlier materials around new narrative centers; bibliographies and disciplinary histories, in turn, make some of these arrangements easier to see than others. The question raised by these notebooks is therefore not simply whether Lovejoy once studied Buddhism. It is what kind of historical work he did when he did so, how relations that were separated at one scale could be recombined at another, and how those changing objects were later classified as other versions of Lovejoy became canonical.
