# Lovejoy as Orientalist / comparative religion — web sweep batch 49

Date: 2026-08-18  
Focus: secondary historiography for the `004 → 1898 → 1901 → 1902` route, with an eye to a first-form JHI Blog piece and a later journal article. This note extends batch 29 rather than replacing it.

## Core result

The secondary literature now falls into three useful but only partly overlapping historiographies:

1. **Lovejoy’s intellectual development and historiographical practice**: Daniel J. Wilson, Leo Catana, Daniel Wickberg, Darrin McMahon, Anthony Grafton, and Nico Mouton already make it impossible to claim simply that Lovejoy’s mature history of ideas had an unnoticed early prehistory. Wilson explicitly links his early religion/history-of-philosophy writings to later history-of-ideas practice; Catana shows that Lovejoy’s actual historical practice can differ from his mature methodological rhetoric; Wickberg/McMahon/Mouton reopen the mature method against older caricatures.
2. **The nineteenth-century Western/American making of “Buddhism” as an object of knowledge**: Philip C. Almond and Thomas A. Tweed provide the indispensable large frames. Almond emphasizes the nineteenth-century textualization/reification of Buddhism in European scholarship; Tweed reconstructs the American encounter with Buddhism from 1844 to 1912 and the ways Victorian American categories shaped interpretation. Sebastian Lecourt’s recent work on reading “world religions” in the 1890s adds a useful fin-de-siècle frame and cautions against reducing all such reading to a single Orientalist logic.
3. **The institutional/documentary history of Oriental and comparative-religion scholarship**: the present project has strong primary evidence for the Harvard Oriental Series / Lanman / Warren environment, but the targeted sweep has not yet produced a comparably strong modern secondary study that directly joins this Harvard scholarly infrastructure to Lovejoy’s notebook practice.

The productive intersection is therefore narrower than “Buddhism influenced Lovejoy’s history of ideas”:

> **Notebook 004 lets us watch a young American philosopher learning to perform historical-philosophical discrimination inside a late-nineteenth-century textual and philological apparatus that was itself producing “Buddhism” as a stable comparative object.**

This is a documentary/workshop argument, not a claim that Buddhism caused the later unit-idea method.

## 1. Lovejoy historiography: what is already occupied

### Daniel J. Wilson

Wilson remains the baseline developmental account. His biography and annotated bibliography already treat Lovejoy’s early work in religion and history of philosophy as part of the genealogy of his mature historical practice. `Religion and the Time-Process` (1902) is especially important in Wilson’s narrative because it joins religious history, temporality, value and the later historical study of large conceptual complexes.

**Consequence:** the article/blog should not announce the discovery of an “early Lovejoy” doing history of ideas before the history of ideas. That bridge is already in the literature.

### Leo Catana, 2010

Catana’s `Lovejoy’s Readings of Bruno: Or How Nineteenth-Century History of Philosophy Was “Transformed” into the History of Ideas` (_JHI_ 71.1, 91–112; DOI 10.1353/jhi.0.0068) argues that Lovejoy’s grand methodological declarations in _The Great Chain of Being_ were partly disciplinary rhetoric and do not fully describe his actual historical practice. Catana places the 1904 and 1936 Bruno studies in continuity with nineteenth-century general histories of philosophy.

**Use for 004:** method should be reconstructed from practice rather than read backwards from the 1936 manifesto. 004 adds something Catana does not have: a pre-publication notebook in which copied authorities, supplementation, reclassification and eventual publication can be separated.

### Wickberg / McMahon / Grafton / Mouton

Daniel Wickberg’s `In the Environment of Ideas` (_Modern Intellectual History_ 11.2, 2014, 439–464; DOI 10.1017/S1479244314000080) repositions Lovejoy as a form of cultural historian rather than a simple foil for contextualism. Anthony Grafton’s 2006 JHI survey is a key disciplinary history of the rise, criticism and transformations of history of ideas. Darrin McMahon likewise argues that standard dismissals of Lovejoy often flatten what he actually did. Nico Mouton’s 2023 `Apologia` (_History and Theory_ 62.2, 272–295; DOI 10.1111/hith.12298) directly challenges the canonical list of accusations that Lovejoy treated ideas as timeless, contextless essences incapable of change.

**Consequence:** avoid a straw-Lovejoy setup. The notebook’s value is positive and archival: it shows operations, not merely that mature Lovejoy was “more contextual” than Skinnerian caricatures suggest.

## 2. Buddhism / Orientalism historiography: the most useful new frame

### Philip C. Almond

_The British Discovery of Buddhism_ (1988) argues that nineteenth-century European scholarship made Buddhism into an increasingly textualized and delimited object. By the middle Victorian period, an “ideal Buddhism” reconstructed through texts and Western scholarly institutions could function as the standard against which lived Asian forms were judged. Almond’s conclusion describes the reification of Buddhism as a textual object and links this process to the emergence of the historical Buddha.

**Use:** 004 is full of precisely the kind of mediated textual Buddhism Almond makes visible: translated canons, philological authorities, comparative classifications, disputed technical terms, attempts to reconstruct an original system from layered textual evidence.

**Limit:** Almond’s principal object is Britain/Europe. Lovejoy’s Harvard notebook should be treated as a possible American extension/transplantation of this scholarly machinery, not folded uncritically into a British story.

### Thomas A. Tweed

_The American Encounter with Buddhism, 1844–1912_ (first published 1992; later UNC edition) is the strongest American-scale contextual history. Tweed starts the American encounter in 1844 and studies the interpretive debate through which Americans made Buddhism intelligible within Victorian cultural constraints. The book is especially useful for distinguishing scholarly, religious, dissenting and popular forms of reception without assuming that interest in Buddhism amounted to conversion or doctrinal sympathy.

**Use:** Lovejoy’s 1898 technical article and notebook sit chronologically inside Tweed’s American encounter, but at the specialist/academic end of the spectrum. A targeted search has not yet surfaced evidence that Tweed treats Lovejoy directly. Until the book/index is checked, use Tweed as contextual historiography, not as a Lovejoy secondary source.

### Sebastian Lecourt, 2023

`Reading World Religions in the 1890s` in _Nineteenth-Century Literature in Transition: The 1890s_ (pp. 97–111; DOI 10.1017/9781009063852.006) surveys the expanding 1890s market for works on Buddhism, Islam, Zoroastrianism and Hinduism. Lecourt notes that scholarship on figures such as Max Müller often emphasizes Orientalism and imperial power-knowledge, while liberal, missionary, middlebrow and occult readers pursued heterogeneous projects that cannot always be reduced to a single imperial logic.

**Use:** good safeguard against making the JHI Blog an automatic “Lovejoy as Orientalist” indictment. The archive is more interesting if we reconstruct the specific scholarly operations and institutional routes first.

## 3. A more precise historiographical gap

After the targeted searches so far, I have **not located a modern secondary study that uses MS38 notebook 004 to reconstruct Lovejoy’s 1890s Buddhist/Indological working practice and then connects that practice to the broader scholarly production of Buddhism as a comparative philosophical object**.

This remains a provisional negative search result. It is not yet an exhaustive novelty claim.

The gap sits between two established literatures:

- Lovejoy scholarship knows that early religion/history-of-philosophy mattered to his development.
- Buddhism/Orientalism scholarship knows that nineteenth-century Buddhism was produced through textual, philological and comparative institutions.

004 gives a rare point where the two histories can be observed in the same working notebook.

## 4. What the JHI Blog can safely claim

A strong first-form claim:

> Existing Lovejoy scholarship already recognizes his early work on religion as part of the prehistory of his history of ideas, while histories of nineteenth-century Buddhism have shown how “Buddhism” became a textual and comparative scholarly object. Lovejoy’s `Sankhya + Buddhism` notebook brings those two histories together at the level of practice. It preserves the moment-by-moment work by which translated and philological authorities were excerpted, compared, supplemented and reorganized into a historical reconstruction.

The blog can therefore be about **the workshop**, not about proving a final genealogy from Buddhism to _The Great Chain of Being_. The later journal article can ask the larger question once Harvard courses, Lanman/Warren networks, bibliography, teaching and later reuse are more completely reconstructed.

## 5. Claims to avoid

- `Lovejoy invented the history-of-ideas method while studying Buddhism.`
- `Historians have overlooked the connection between Lovejoy’s early religion work and his later method.` Wilson already occupies this ground.
- `The notebook proves that Buddhism caused the unit-idea method.`
- `Lovejoy was a Buddhologist throughout his career.` Better: specialist Buddhology was brief, but Buddhist/Indian-philosophy work continued through teaching and comparative reuse.
- `Lovejoy’s notebook simply reproduces Orientalist ideology.` The stronger historical question is what particular textual and inferential operations he inherited, modified and used.
- `Almond’s British story directly describes Harvard.` Treat transatlantic extension as a hypothesis to be demonstrated.
