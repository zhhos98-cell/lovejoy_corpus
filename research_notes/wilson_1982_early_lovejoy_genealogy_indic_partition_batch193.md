# Batch 193 — Wilson 1982 and the partition of early Lovejoy: Buddhist philology separated from the genealogy of the history of ideas

Date: 2026-08-26  
Status: synced  
Scope: close reading of Daniel J. Wilson, *Arthur O. Lovejoy: An Annotated Bibliography* (1982), especially the introduction, primary-source entries 17–26, the index, and Wilson’s annotation of his own 1980 JHI article on *The Great Chain of Being*. Cross-control is the full 1904 OCR witness of Lovejoy’s “The Dialectic of Bruno and Spinoza.” This is a historiographical batch: Wilson is treated as a secondary interpreter and bibliographer, not as evidence for Lovejoy’s own intentions where the primary text is available.

## Core result

Wilson’s bibliography preserves a great deal of early Lovejoy evidence but organizes it in a way that helps explain a later historiographical blind spot.

In the primary-source sequence, the **1898 Buddhist article** is represented as a highly technical intervention in the correct interpretation of two Buddhist terms. By contrast, Wilson’s narrative genealogy of Lovejoy’s later history of ideas starts principally from **“Religion and the Time-Process” (1902)** and **“The Dialectic of Bruno and Spinoza” (1904)**, which he describes as introducing Lovejoy to the Platonic complex later embodied in the chain of being. In entry 22, and still more explicitly in Wilson’s 1980 JHI article (entry 435), the 1904 essay becomes the early framework of the later *Great Chain of Being*.

The full 1904 primary text complicates this retrospective partition. Lovejoy’s Bruno–Spinoza article itself uses **Vedānta, the Mundaka Upanishad, Schopenhauer as “modern Vedāntism,” Deussen, and Māyā** inside its central analytical argument. Wilson’s genealogy is therefore not wrong about the Platonic/Neo-Platonic line, but it is **selective**: the non-Western material functioning within that early conceptual apparatus becomes hard to see once the work is retrospectively narrated chiefly as the prehistory of a Western chain-of-being project.

## 1. Wilson’s introduction gives the early religious essays a privileged genealogical role

Wilson says Lovejoy’s earliest history-of-ideas essays focused on the history of religious thought and formed part of his attempt to reconcile a temporal world with the eternal by tracing the reasons for religious rejection of temporality.

He then singles out **“Religion and the Time-Process” (1902)** and **“The Dialectic of Bruno and Spinoza” (1904)** as works that introduced Lovejoy to the complex of Platonic ideas later embodied in the chain of being. The next step in Wilson’s narrative is “The Obsolescence of the Eternal,” followed by the mature history-of-ideas work.

This is a strong and useful secondary genealogy. It is also a selective one: the 1898 Buddhist philological article is absent from this particular developmental sentence.

## 2. Entry 19 isolates the 1898 Buddhist paper as technical philology

Wilson’s annotation of **“The Buddhistic Technical Terms upādāna and upādisesa”** says that Lovejoy offers what he regards as the correct interpretation of two terms essential to understanding the philosophical system of Buddhism and attempts to correct earlier misinterpretations through a very technical analysis.

That description is accurate as far as it goes. But bibliographically the entry is comparatively isolated: unlike the adjacent 1902 and 1904 entries, it is not supplied with a “See” chain tying it to Wilson’s later interpretation of the genealogy of Lovejoy’s history of ideas.

Wilson control: primary-source entry 19, printed p. 12.

Evidence limit: absence of a cross-reference is an editorial fact about the bibliography, not proof that Wilson consciously intended to marginalize the article.

---

## 3. Entry 21 converts the 1902 article into a major bridge

Wilson’s annotation of **“Religion and the Time-Process”** treats it as a large history of religious attitudes toward temporality, moving from ancient Western and Indian civilizations through Platonism, Christianity, deism, the decline of the chain of being, evolution, and a new valuation of process.

The entry cross-references *The Great Chain of Being*, the 1904 Bruno–Spinoza article, “The Obsolescence of the Eternal,” and Wilson’s own later interpretation (435).

The important historiographical point is that **Indian material is visible inside Wilson’s summary of the 1902 article**, but the developmental relation is indexed through the later chain-of-being/eternal-temporal story rather than through the earlier 1898 Buddhist philological paper.

Wilson control: primary-source entry 21, printed pp. 13–14.

---

## 4. Entry 22 makes the 1904 article a direct precursor of *The Great Chain of Being*

Wilson summarizes the 1904 Bruno–Spinoza essay as an inquiry into historical sources and affinities, a tracing of Neo-Platonism through Plotinus, the Middle Ages, Bruno, and Spinoza, and a study of **otherworldliness** and **plenitude**.

He concludes that “in the structure of the argument, in the examples provided, and in the moral deduced,” the article anticipates *The Great Chain of Being*.

That is a high-value secondary observation and is supported by the 1904 primary text. But Wilson’s short entry does not register the article’s explicit Vedānta/Mundaka/Deussen/Māyā comparison, even though those materials help Lovejoy define the central `that/what` distinction early in the argument.

Wilson control: primary-source entry 22, printed p. 14.  
Primary control: Lovejoy 1904, pp. 144–148.

---

## 5. Wilson’s 1980 JHI article sharpens the retrospective Western framing

Entry 435 summarizes Wilson’s article **“Arthur O. Lovejoy and the Moral of The Great Chain of Being”** (*JHI* 41 [1980]: 249–265).

Wilson argues that by 1904 Lovejoy had already outlined the **framework of western thought** later traced in greater detail in *The Great Chain of Being*, and that the structure and moral of the argument remained essentially stable across the following thirty years.

This formulation captures an important continuity in Lovejoy’s attack on an Absolute that is both total and inclusive of manifold becoming. But read against the full 1904 article, the phrase “framework of western thought” can hide a methodological fact: Lovejoy is using Vedānta and modern Vedāntism as part of the comparative equipment with which that framework is analyzed.

Wilson control: entry 435, scan JSON p. 189.

## 6. The mismatch is best described as a historiographical partition, not an error

The evidence supports a narrower claim than “Wilson ignored India.” He did not. His summary of the 1902 article explicitly mentions Indian civilization, and his bibliography indexes Buddhism and Deussen.

The stronger and more precise finding is about **where different materials are placed in the explanatory architecture**:

- 1898 Buddhism → technical philology;
- 1902 religion/time → major temporal-otherworldliness bridge;
- 1904 Bruno–Spinoza → prehistory of *The Great Chain of Being* and Western intellectual framework.

The direct primary text shows more overlap among these compartments than Wilson’s retrospective organization suggests. Indic materials cross from the “Orientalist” or Buddhist domain into Lovejoy’s general metaphysical method by 1904.

Thus the historiographical gap is produced less by factual omission than by **disciplinary partitioning**.

---

## 7. Why this matters for the current Lovejoy project

This helps explain why Lovejoy’s non-Western formation can disappear even in careful scholarship that has the relevant bibliography in hand.

A researcher following Wilson’s cross-references naturally moves:

`1902 Time-Process → 1904 Bruno–Spinoza → 1936 Great Chain`

while the 1898 Buddhist paper remains a specialist philological item.

But the primary-text route now available is:

`1898 Buddhist technical analysis`

→ `1902 Vedānta + Buddhism used to analyze otherworldliness and temporality`

→ `1904 Vedānta/Deussen/Māyā used inside a general metaphysical classification`

→ later Platonic/chain-of-being synthesis.

The last sequence should still be treated as a research reconstruction, not as Lovejoy’s own retrospective autobiography. Its advantage is evidentiary: each transition can be tested against signed primary texts.

---

## 8. Adjacent entries make 1904 look less like a single-track “history of philosophy” year

Wilson’s entries 22–26 place, in the same year:

- Bruno/Spinoza and historical metaphysics;
- international ethics and intervention;
- current philosophy and ethics reviewing;
- religious instruction, non-sectarian universities, academic freedom, and free society;
- eighteenth-century evolution and the prehistory of descent theory.

This concentration matters because the 1904 metaphysical article should not be isolated from Lovejoy’s simultaneous activity in social ethics, institutional questions, current philosophy, and historical evolutionism. Chronological co-presence is not causal unity, but it provides the right corpus boundary for a person-specific 1904 reconstruction.

Wilson control: primary-source entries 22–26, printed pp. 14–16.
