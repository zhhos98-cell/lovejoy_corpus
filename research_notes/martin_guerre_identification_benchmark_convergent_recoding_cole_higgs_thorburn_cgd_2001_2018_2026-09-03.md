# Martin Guerre as an identification benchmark — convergent recoding from forensic history to digital-ID development, 2001–2018

Date: 2026-09-03  
Status: **BOUNDED SOURCE CONTROL / HISTORY-OF-KNOWLEDGE ADDENDUM / NON-GENEALOGICAL TO LOVEJOY**

## Result

A closer source-chain check changes the causal language again. Simon Cole's *Suspect Identities* (2001), Edward Higgs's identity-management keynote (2007/08), Malcolm Thorburn's legal-theoretical chapter on identification (2012), and Gelb–Diofasi Metz's *Identification Revolution* (2018) all use Martin Guerre to articulate problems of identification, but present evidence does **not** support one continuous citation chain from Cole to the 2018 digital-ID book.

The stronger model is **convergent recoding**:

> a highly structured historical case repeatedly affords the same modern decomposition — claimant, physical signs, social witnesses, remembered information, missing reference record, authorized decision — so independent disciplines can translate it into their own identification vocabulary without inheriting one stable interpretation.

This makes the case's portability less dependent on direct influence than previously assumed.

## 1. Simon A. Cole, 2001: from the missing physical record to scientific identification

Cole's *Suspect Identities: A History of Fingerprinting and Criminal Identification* opens Chapter 1, `Impostors and Incorrigible Rogues`, with Martin Guerre. The courts' problem is formulated as the difficult task of determining who a man was. Cole emphasizes that the closest thing to a physical record of Martin available to the court was the cobbler's wooden shoe lasts; their mismatch did not settle the case, so adjudication depended more heavily on testimony and remembered traits.

This placement matters architecturally. Martin Guerre is not a late illustrative anecdote attached to fingerprinting. It is the **opening negative condition** against which the later history of criminal identification becomes intelligible.

The book then moves through anthropometry, fingerprints, record systems, inter-institutional exchange and DNA. A later chapter is explicitly titled `Identification at a Distance`. The overall problem is how institutions produce durable, portable signs that can connect a body encountered now to a person/record established elsewhere or earlier.

The case therefore supplies a contrast:

`local memories + bodily descriptions + uncertain material traces`

versus

`standardized inscription + archive + retrieval + comparison`.

Cole's history is also anti-triumphalist: the point is not that scientific identifiers achieve infallibility, but that fingerprinting won institutional and public trust through historically contingent competition among technologies and claims to certainty.

Source:
- Simon A. Cole, *Suspect Identities: A History of Fingerprinting and Criminal Identification* (Harvard University Press, 2001), especially ch. 1 and ch. 9. JSTOR: https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvjz80ss

## 2. Higgs, 2007/08: the benchmark is classified, then the developmental story is challenged

Higgs places Davis's Martin Guerre, Perkin Warbeck and the Tichborne Claimant in a category of histories that reveal how identification failed in particular imposture cases. He explicitly distinguishes them from histories designed to explain changing forms of identification.

He then quotes Cole's model of premodern identification: village-scale personal acquaintance and collective memory provide an informal solution; industrialization and migration undermine that system, creating anonymity and the need for new identifiers such as fingerprints.

But Higgs's keynote is written to test and qualify that very model. He labels the broader equation `modernity = mobility = anonymity = identification` and argues that the evidence from England does not support mobility as a sufficient general cause. The changing relationships among individuals, commercial organizations and the state must be examined more directly.

This gives an important source-level asymmetry:

- Martin Guerre is already a **benchmark for failed recognition**;
- Cole's modernization account is a **theory of why formal identification expands**;
- Higgs accepts the former while disputing the sufficiency of the latter.

So the case does not determine the theory attached to it.

Source:
- Edward Higgs, `Are State-Mediated Forms of Identification a Reaction to Physical Mobility? The case of England, 1500–2007`, IFIP IDMAN 2007 / Springer 2008, pp. 105–120: https://opendl.ifip-tc6.org/db/conf/idman/idman2007/Higgs07.pdf

## 3. Thorburn, 2012: Martin Guerre inside a normative separation of identification from surveillance and profiling

Malcolm Thorburn's `Identification, Surveillance and Profiling: On the Use and Abuse of Citizen Data` (2012) moves the case into legal and political theory. Thorburn's primary conceptual intervention is to separate three practices often compressed under `surveillance`:

1. **identification** — ensuring continuity of an individual's identity across time and space;
2. **surveillance** — collecting information about identifiable persons and their activities;
3. **profiling** — assembling information into categories used to predict or differentially treat future behavior.

In the identification section, he contrasts small/stable communities, where direct personal experience often suffices to establish who is who, with modern `societies of strangers`. Martin Guerre appears as one of the exceptional premodern cases in which identity became problematic, particularly when persons went away to war.

The affair therefore becomes a boundary example for a normative proposition: **state identification can be analytically separated from broader surveillance and profiling**.

This use differs from Cole. Cole asks how scientific identification came to secure institutional confidence. Thorburn asks which state knowledge practices should be distinguished normatively even when they share data infrastructures.

The same case is thus reallocated from history of science/forensics into a theory of legitimate state information.

Source:
- Malcolm Thorburn, `Identification, Surveillance and Profiling: On the Use and Abuse of Citizen Data`, in G. R. Sullivan and Ian Dennis, eds., *Seeking Security: Pre-Empting the Commission of Criminal Harms* (Hart, 2012); SSRN abstract no. 1991747.

## 4. Gelb and Diofasi Metz, 2018: from historical benchmark to protocol slots

*Identification Revolution* uses Martin Guerre twice in its introductory architecture.

First, the case follows the example of Alecia Faith Pennington, whose lack of documentary registration made proving her legal existence difficult. Martin Guerre is introduced as a different problem: **validating oneself against a known identity**. The book then immediately connects the discussion to the contemporary global `identification and identity verification gap`, rights, entitlements, development programs and state capacity.

Second, after defining three generic factor classes — `something you are`, `something you have`, `something you know` — the authors retrospectively map the Guerre evidence onto the scheme:

- appearance = `are`;
- testimony from Bertrande = `have`;
- private/personal information = `know`.

The point is explicitly that even several factors cannot completely remove fraud or error.

Immediately afterward comes the three-phase model:

`village` → `centralized national identification` → `federated / e-village`.

Thus Martin Guerre is doing at least three jobs at once:

1. historical example of failed validation;
2. bridge from unregistered/non-verifiable persons to development policy;
3. demonstration object for multi-factor authentication logic.

This is the most aggressive **retrospective protocolization** located so far.

Source:
- Alan Gelb and Anna Diofasi Metz, *Identification Revolution: Can Digital ID Be Harnessed for Development?* (Center for Global Development, 2018), sample Introduction: https://www.cgdev.org/sites/default/files/identification-revolution-can-digital-id-sample-chapter-introduction.pdf

## 5. Does Gelb–Diofasi descend from Cole?

Current source check: **not established**.

The accessible CGD sample chapter directly footnotes Natalie Zemon Davis for Martin Guerre. A full-text search of the sample chapter returns no occurrence of `Cole`. The chapter cites a broader historical compilation by Breckenridge and Szreter when discussing identification history, but its specific Martin Guerre note points to Davis.

Therefore do not claim:

`Cole 2001 → Gelb/Diofasi 2018`.

The safer interpretation is:

`Davis case structure`

→ independently usable by Cole as a prehistory of scientific/criminal identification

and

→ independently usable by Gelb/Diofasi as a digital-ID/MFA benchmark.

The striking similarity between Cole's local-acquaintance → formal-identification framing and Gelb/Diofasi's village → centralized-ID framing may reflect a broad identification discourse, shared modernization assumptions, or mediated sources. Direct textual descent remains unresolved.

## 6. Convergent recoding

### Definition

**Convergent recoding** = independent or only partially connected scholarly/technical communities reclassify the same historical case into similar operational categories because the case has a structure that fits their problem, without evidence of a single continuous citation or concept-transfer chain.

Martin Guerre's affordances are unusually strong:

- a claimant asserts continuity with an absent person;
- several kinds of sign are available but none is individually decisive;
- some signs are bodily, others social, remembered or material;
- multiple witnesses disagree;
- legal action requires a binary-enough decision despite evidentiary heterogeneity;
- a later event (the real Martin's return) creates a strong retrospective adjudication;
- the case is famous and already narratively compressed.

Because these components map easily onto verification problems, the case can be repeatedly reassembled as:

`claim` + `factors` + `reference problem` + `decision threshold` + `false acceptance risk`.

That modern form can arise without any actor explicitly inheriting it from a predecessor.

## 7. The important epistemic loss: social relation becomes factor

The 2018 MFA mapping makes the loss especially visible. A spouse's testimony in an early-modern household and court is a densely social relation involving intimacy, gender, legal standing, coercion, memory, property, reputation and affect. In a generic authentication taxonomy it becomes `something you have`.

That is analytically powerful and historically violent in a precise sense: **a relational source of authority is converted into a fungible factor slot**.

This is not simply anachronism. It is a change of data type.

`Bertrande as historically situated epistemic actor`

becomes

`Bertrande as possession/endorsement factor`.

The retyping is what allows the case to circulate in digital-ID discourse.

## 8. Why this matters for history of knowledge

A normal reception history asks where Martin Guerre was cited and represented. A history of knowledge can reconstruct the successive **operations performed on the case**:

- Cole: establish a historical contrast between local evidentiary uncertainty and standardized identifiers;
- Higgs: classify it as failure case and use it to test modernization explanations;
- Thorburn: turn it into a boundary case in a normative taxonomy of state data practices;
- Gelb/Diofasi: recode it into authentication factors and development-system phases.

The unit being transmitted is therefore neither `the story` nor a single proposition. It is a **reconfigurable evidentiary architecture**.

## 9. Negative / bounded finding from policy-domain search

A targeted indexed-web search of World Bank/ID4D and OECD domains did not surface a comparably explicit Martin Guerre reuse in those institutions' public digital-identity materials. This is a bounded search result, not proof of absence. At present, the 2018 CGD treatment should therefore be described as a **high-value explicit development-policy node**, not as evidence that Martin Guerre is a standard World Bank/OECD benchmark.

## 10. Revised strongest statement

**Martin Guerre became a reusable benchmark for modern identification not through one traceable line of descent but through convergent recoding. Cole's 2001 history starts with the absence of a stable physical record and makes the case a negative prehistory of scientific criminal identification; Higgs classifies the same case as an identification failure while challenging the modernization theory often attached to it; Thorburn uses it to delimit identification from surveillance and profiling; Gelb and Diofasi Metz, citing Davis rather than Cole in the accessible chapter, independently convert its bodily resemblance, witness endorsement and private knowledge into the slots of multi-factor authentication. The recurring object is therefore an evidentiary architecture that can be retyped for different knowledge systems. Its portability depends partly on exactly what gets lost: socially and legally heterogeneous relations become standardized verification factors.**

## Next check

The most valuable next move is to follow the **reference-state problem**, not to keep accumulating uses. Compare what counts as the prior state against which a claimant is checked in:

- Martin Guerre / Coras: remembered person, bodily features, household history, shoe lasts;
- Bertillon / fingerprint systems: centrally stored inscription and bodily index;
- civil registration/passport: state-certified biographical record;
- digital ID: enrolled person-identification data + credential;
- biometric authentication: stored template;
- proof-of-humanity: enrolled or freshly generated evidence of unique human presence.

This would convert `prospective enrolment` from a promising formulation into a full history-of-knowledge variable: **who is authorized to manufacture the reference state, when it is manufactured, how durable it is, and which later claims it can adjudicate**.
