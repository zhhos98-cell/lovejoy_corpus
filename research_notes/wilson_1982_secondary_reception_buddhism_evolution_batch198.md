# Batch 198 — Wilson (1982) secondary reception audit: Buddhism/Indic silence versus evolution-method genealogy

Date: 2026-08-26
Status: synced / SECONDARY-RECEPTION CONTROL
Scope: follows Batches196–197 by testing Wilson's secondary-source corpus (items 316–540) for explicit reception of entry19 (`The Buddhistic Technical Terms ...`), entry243 (`Syllabus: The Philosophy of Buddhism`), and entry24 (`Recent Literature in Philosophy and Ethics`), while comparing the result with Wilson's preserved secondary genealogy of Lovejoy's evolutionism and historical method.

## Core result

Wilson's bibliography preserves a marked historiographical asymmetry.

For the early Buddhist/Indic corpus, the recovered architecture remains:

- entry19 = 1898 Buddhist technical-term article;
- entry243 = 1901 four-lecture Buddhism syllabus;
- subject index = `Buddhism, 19, 243`;
- title index = the 1898 article points only to 19; the syllabus points only to 243;
- no secondary entry located in Wilson's annotated secondary corpus explicitly discusses either early Buddhist item by title, technical term, or recovered item-level cross-reference.

For entry24, the same audit pattern holds: its title index gives only item24, and no secondary annotation located in this pass explicitly returns to the 1904 review as an object of historiographical analysis.

By contrast, the evolution/temporalism line is not only densely cross-referenced among Lovejoy's primary works; Wilson's secondary corpus explicitly interprets evolution as a source of Lovejoy's historical method. Kathleen E. Duffin's 1980 JHI essay (item345) argues that emergent evolution underwrote Lovejoy's genetic method of historical analysis and temporalism and concludes that the methodology of the history of ideas is indebted to his reflection on evolution. John Herman Randall Jr. (item406) likewise locates the beginnings of Lovejoy's history-of-ideas interests partly in his evolutionism.

Thus the evolution line has **both primary bibliographic density and explicit secondary methodological reception**, whereas the early Buddhist/Indic line has **primary survival and subject-index co-location but no comparable secondary reception recovered in Wilson**.

This is evidence of differential retrospective visibility, not evidence that the Indic work lacked intellectual importance for Lovejoy.

## 1. Wilson's own completeness claim raises the value of a controlled negative result

In the preface Wilson states that:

- Part Two contains secondary sources dealing directly with Lovejoy's career and writings or bearing directly on them;
- he attempted a complete bibliography of Lovejoy's writings and the secondary literature;
- the secondary sources are complete, so far as he knows, through June 1981, while acknowledging that some unlocated items may remain.

This does not make absence mathematically exhaustive. It does make absence inside the bibliography more meaningful than absence from an casual sample.

The appropriate claim is therefore:

> **Within Wilson's claimed near-comprehensive secondary bibliography through June 1981, no explicit secondary discussion of entries19 or243 has been recovered by title, technical-term, index, or annotation search.**

The claim should not be inflated into `nobody discussed Lovejoy's Buddhism before 1981` without an independent external literature search.

## 2. Entry19: primary item survives, secondary commentary edge does not appear

Wilson's primary entry19 is:

`The Buddhistic Technical Terms upādāna and upādisea`, *Journal of the American Oriental Society* 19, Second Half (1898), 126–36.

Wilson describes it as a highly technical attempt to correct previous interpretations of two terms essential to the philosophical system of Buddhism.

The relevant bibliographic layers recovered are:

### Primary-entry layer

`19 → ∅ [W-SEE]`

No formal `See` line follows the entry.

### Title-index layer

`The Buddhistic Technical Terms ... → 19`

No subsequent number is printed. Under Wilson's stated index convention, no title-index commentary item is thereby identified.

### Subject-index layer

`Buddhism → 19, 243`.

This joins the 1898 paper with the 1901 syllabus at subject level.

### Secondary-search layer

Exact-title, `upādāna`, Buddhism, and early-Indic/Lovejoy searches of the secondary corpus did not recover an annotated secondary source explicitly discussing entry19.

The negative result is therefore multi-layered, not dependent on one failed keyword search.

## 3. Entry243: the 1901 Buddhism syllabus is real but equally thinly received

Entry243 is:

`Syllabus: The Philosophy of Buddhism`, The Washington University Association, 1901.

Wilson records four lecture topics:

1. `The Place of Buddhism among the Philosophies of India`;
2–3. `Two Essential Formulas of Buddhism`;
4. `Buddhist Ethics and its Goal`.

The syllabus establishes that Lovejoy's Buddhist work was not exhausted by the 1898 technical note. At minimum, the bibliography documents a continuation from specialized lexical/exegetical intervention to a structured public/course exposition of Buddhism in 1901.

But Wilson's later architecture still remains thin:

- no recovered `243 → 19` formal See;
- no title-index commentary number after 243;
- no secondary annotation recovered that discusses the syllabus as a formative object in Lovejoy's intellectual development.

The strongest controlled statement is therefore:

> Wilson preserves a small **Buddhist corpus** but does not preserve a corresponding **Buddhist reception genealogy**.

## 4. Entry24 shows a parallel case of primary richness versus bibliographic under-reception

Entry24, `Recent Literature in Philosophy and Ethics` (1904), is represented by Wilson as a short multi-book review concerning pragmatism, ethics, and history of philosophy.

Batch191 and Batch195 showed from the primary text that this article contains much more analytically relevant material:

- French-language reconstruction and translation criticism;
- graded textual-witness control;
- antecedent versus downstream-influence distinctions;
- classification control around Spinoza;
- explicit praise of analytical historical method;
- reconstruction of historical milieu.

Yet Wilson's title index gives:

`Recent Literature in Philosophy and Ethics → 24`

with no commentary number, and the present secondary search recovered no item explicitly centered on the review.

Thus entries19 and24 form two different examples of the same archival problem:

`primary methodological density > retrospective bibliographic visibility`.

The contents are very different, but the historiographical flattening mechanism is comparable.

## 5. Entry48 control: the evolution graph is dense but not a completely reciprocal triangle

Batch197 proposed testing whether 26/48/57 formed a fully reciprocal triangle.

Entry48 is:

`The Argument for Organic Evolution Before 'The Origin of Species'`, *Popular Science Monthly* 75 (1909), 499–514, 537–49.

Wilson's entry moves from annotation directly to a notice of the later revised reprint in *Forerunners of Darwin*, and then to entry49. No formal `See` line is present in the recovered entry.

Therefore:

```text
26 → 48
57 → 48
26 ↔ 57
48 → ∅ [formal See at this entry]
```

The correct topology is a **reciprocal 26–57 pair with shared target48**, not a fully reciprocal triangle.

This correction makes the graph more precise without weakening the broader claim that Wilson organizes a strong evolution genealogy.

## 6. Duffin 345 explicitly supplies the methodological genealogy that the graph suggested

Kathleen E. Duffin, `Arthur O. Lovejoy and the Emergence of Novelty`, *Journal of the History of Ideas* 41 (1980), 267–81, is Wilson item345.

Wilson's annotation reports Duffin's argument in unusually strong terms:

- Lovejoy was an ardent supporter of emergent evolution;
- significant change for Lovejoy is characterized by discontinuity rather than simple continuity;
- emergent evolution supplied the basis for his `genetic method of historical analysis` and his temporalism;
- the concern with novelty/discontinuity operated epistemologically, ontologically, causally, across disciplinary boundaries, and historically;
- Duffin concludes that the methodology of the history of ideas is indebted to Lovejoy's reflection on the import of evolution.

Wilson cross-references Duffin to:

`69, 118, 124, 435`.

This secondary item therefore does something Wilson's treatment of Buddhism does not: it explicitly converts a philosophical/scientific concern into a genealogy of historical method.

### Evidence-grade consequence

Before this audit, the repository could say:

`Wilson's evolution cross-reference network suggests a second early methodological genealogy.`

After Duffin 345, it can say:

> **Wilson's secondary corpus explicitly contains a historiographical thesis that Lovejoy's evolutionism and emergentism contributed to his genetic method of historical analysis.**

That is a secondary historiographical claim, not yet a primary-text demonstration.

## 7. Randall 406 independently foregrounds evolutionism as an origin point

John Herman Randall Jr., `Arthur O. Lovejoy and the History of Ideas`, *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 23 (1963), 475–79, is Wilson item406.

Wilson summarizes Randall as describing the beginnings of Lovejoy's interest in the history of ideas in **his evolutionism and his rationalism**. Randall characterizes Lovejoy as especially concerned with collecting and discriminating among ideas, and less with causal explanation.

Wilson gives:

`406 → 320, 435`.

Randall thus independently supports the historiographical visibility of the evolution line, even though he interprets the method differently from Duffin.

The two secondary claims should remain separate:

- `Randall`: origins partly in evolutionism/rationalism; discrimination of ideas foregrounded;
- `Duffin`: emergent evolution underwrites genetic historical analysis and temporalism.

Their convergence is on **evolutionism as formative**, not on one identical account of Lovejoy's method.

## 8. The secondary corpus therefore preserves at least two explicit origin narratives

Wilson's bibliography now yields two strongly visible historiographical routes into mature history of ideas:

### Route A — Platonic / religious / temporalist / Great Chain

Wilson himself, especially introduction + item435:

`21 + 22 → early history of religious thought / Platonic complex → 51 temporalist bridge → 5 Great Chain`.

### Route B — evolution / emergence / genetic historical method

Wilson's primary cross-reference graph + Randall406 + Duffin345:

`17 / 26 / 48 / 54 / 57 / later evolution writings`

→ `evolutionism / emergence / discontinuity`

→ `genetic historical analysis / history-of-ideas methodology`.

These two routes may overlap substantially through temporalism. Entry51 already demonstrates the bridge: it belongs simultaneously to eternalism, temporalism, and evolution and points back to 21–22 and forward to 5.

Thus `two routes` should not be mistaken for `two isolated methods`.

A better model is a **braided genealogy** whose strands become visible differently in Wilson's bibliography.

## 9. The Buddhist/Indic material occupies a different historiographical status

Against those two explicit narratives, entries19 and243 currently occupy a third status:

```text
1898 technical philology/exegesis
        ↓ chronological continuation only
1901 Buddhism syllabus
        ↕
subject index: Buddhism
```

What is missing in Wilson is the retrospective arrow:

`Buddhist/Indic practice → later Lovejoy method`.

The repository may investigate that arrow, but it has to be established from primary-text continuities, manuscript evidence, reading history, and/or later scholarship outside Wilson. Wilson does not supply it.

That makes the problem sharper:

> The question is not whether Lovejoy had a Buddhist/Indic phase. Wilson's bibliography itself proves that he did. The question is what later intellectual operations, if any, preserve or transform practices developed there, and why the standard retrospective genealogy did not encode those continuities with the same density.

### NEGATIVE-EVIDENCE LIMIT

The absence claim is internal to Wilson's bibliography and the recoverable OCR/search architecture. Wilson himself acknowledges that his secondary bibliography, although intended to be complete through June 1981, may omit a small number of sources. An independent literature survey is required before making a field-wide absence claim.

### DO NOT CLAIM YET

- that Lovejoy's Buddhist philology caused his later historical method;
- that no scholar before 1981 ever discussed Lovejoy's Buddhist work;
- that Wilson deliberately suppressed or marginalized Buddhism;
- that evolutionism alone generated Lovejoy's history-of-ideas method;
- that primary procedural similarity is equivalent to a transmission route.
