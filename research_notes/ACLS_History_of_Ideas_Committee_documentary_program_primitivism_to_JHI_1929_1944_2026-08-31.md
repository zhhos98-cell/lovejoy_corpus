# ACLS History of Ideas Committee: documentary programme, primitivism, and the institutional prehistory of JHI, 1929–1944

Date: 2026-08-31
Status: **DIRECT FINDING-AID CONTROL / INSTITUTIONAL PREHISTORY STRONGLY EXTENDED / FOLDER CONTENTS NOT YET OPENED**

## Core result

The institutional prehistory of the *Journal of the History of Ideas* must now be pushed back at least a decade before the journal's 1940 launch.

The Library of Congress finding aid for the American Council of Learned Societies Records contains:

- `History of Ideas, Committee on, 1929-1939` — **five folders**, in General Office File I, Box I:66;
- a further `History of Ideas, Committee on, 1944` entry in General Office File III.

Official ACLS institutional history separately states that ACLS committees helped found several influential journals, including the *Journal of the History of Ideas*, launched in 1940.

The safest current institutional sequence is therefore:

`1923 JOHNS HOPKINS HISTORY OF IDEAS CLUB`
→ `1929–1939 ACLS HISTORY OF IDEAS COMMITTEE`
→ `1930s DOCUMENTARY / COLLABORATIVE HISTORY-OF-IDEAS PROGRAMME`
→ `1935 PRIMITIVISM VOLUME`
→ `1940 JOURNAL OF THE HISTORY OF IDEAS`
→ `1944 COMMITTEE RECORDS CONTINUE`.

This is not yet a claim that each arrow represents an unchanged committee membership or a single continuous administrative project. It establishes a national institutional layer between the Hopkins club and the journal which lasted through most of the 1930s.

## II. The primitivism project belongs inside this national field-building chronology

Bibliographic and institutional evidence now makes it unsafe to treat the 1935 *Primitivism and Related Ideas in Antiquity* merely as a Hopkins side project which later happened to resemble JHI's programme.

The book appeared as volume I of **A Documentary History of Primitivism and Related Ideas**, with general editors Arthur O. Lovejoy, Gilbert Chinard, George Boas, and Ronald S. Crane. Contemporary and modern bibliographic records preserve the larger series identity.

Hopkins Press describes the volume as intended to be the first of a four-part documentary series covering the history of primitivism and related ideas. Only the first volume appeared in the original sequence.

The Hopkins Philosophy Department finding aid independently records that during the 1920s–30s Lovejoy and Boas used seminar themes such as evolution and relativity as objects of debate/research and brought out the documentary history of primitivism in this same period.

Thus three organisational scales coexist by the early 1930s:

`HOPKINS SEMINAR / CLUB PROBLEM RESEARCH`
+
`MULTI-SCHOLAR DOCUMENTARY PUBLICATION`
+
`ACLS NATIONAL COMMITTEE ON HISTORY OF IDEAS`.

The next archival task is to determine how directly the documentary primitivism project appears in the five ACLS committee folders.

## III. Why 1929 matters for the Lovejoy/JHI genealogy

Previous shorthand risked making Lovejoy's 1938 retirement and the 1940 journal launch look like the decisive institutional transition.

The finding aid changes the chronology:

`LOVEJOY RETIRES 1938`

is late inside a pre-existing ACLS History of Ideas Committee life-cycle, not the beginning of national organization.

This creates a more plausible sequence of field formation:

1. **local experimental research practice** — Hopkins Club/seminar;
2. **national planning/coordination** — ACLS committee;
3. **cooperative documentary product** — primitivism series;
4. **periodical infrastructure** — JHI;
5. later **international society / reference architecture / editorial networks**.

This morphology is analytically stronger than a biography-led narrative of Lovejoy founding a discipline by publishing a methodological manifesto.

## IV. Relation to ACLS's wider committee ecology

The ACLS finding aid places History of Ideas among many committee-based attempts to organize fields, methods and regions. Later files include, among others:

- Far Eastern Studies;
- Indic and Iranian Studies;
- Arabic and Islamic Studies;
- Islamic Culture and Art;
- Near Eastern Studies;
- Southern Asia;
- Negro Studies;
- Latin American committees;
- History of Religions.

Official ACLS history describes its committee system as a long-term mechanism for creating and strengthening fields, including intellectual history, China studies and African American studies.

Therefore the relevant comparative institutional question is not simply `why did JHI globalize?` It is:

> **How did the History of Ideas Committee and the journal it helped produce intersect, or fail to intersect, with ACLS's parallel region/area/religion infrastructures?**

This is especially important after the later evidence for:

- Committee on Chinese Thought / JHI;
- Wiener–Nakamura / East-West Center;
- Zea's Latin-American history-of-ideas network;
- Dictionary entries on Buddhism and Islamic causation.

The absence of an interface must be demonstrated region by region rather than inferred from absence of a JHI title.

## V. McKeon / Chicago branch: probable but still to source-control

Secondary literature repeatedly places Richard McKeon with Lovejoy on the ACLS History of Ideas Committee and identifies both as important to the journal's creation.

McKeon's later institutional trajectory is independently significant: at Chicago he developed the interdisciplinary Committee on the Analysis of Ideas and the Study of Methods.

This creates a plausible branching genealogy:

`ACLS HISTORY OF IDEAS ECOLOGY`
→ `JHI / Lovejoy–Wiener editorial infrastructure`

and

`ACLS HISTORY OF IDEAS ECOLOGY`
→ `McKEON / CHICAGO ANALYSIS-OF-IDEAS + METHODS INFRASTRUCTURE`.

But exact committee membership and causal branching should be locked from ACLS folders or high-grade institutional records before publication-level claims.

## VII. Current article consequence

For the emerging JHI-scale paper, the strongest safe formulation is now:

> **The institutionalization of the history of ideas preceded the founding of JHI by at least a decade. Lovejoy's Hopkins club supplied one local site, while ACLS maintained a national History of Ideas Committee from 1929 through 1939. The collaborative documentary history of primitivism belongs to this interwar ecology of thematic, cross-disciplinary field-building. JHI should therefore be approached not as the sudden institutional expression of a completed Lovejoyian method, but as one later infrastructure produced after years of committee planning, seminar experimentation and cooperative documentary research.**

This gives `primitivism` an institutional role stronger than a thematic coincidence: it was one of the major cooperative products of the field-building period immediately preceding the journal.
