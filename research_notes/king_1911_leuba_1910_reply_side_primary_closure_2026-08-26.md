`King 1910 book → Leuba 1910 Psychological Bulletin review → King 1911 reply/restatement`

## 1. What King says Leuba objected to

King has just defended treating religion from the standpoint of the active life rather than beginning from a catalogue of beliefs or fixed doctrinal contents. He then writes that a friendly reviewer called attention to the book's limitation in the narrowness with which it held to this point of view; footnote 2 identifies Leuba's *Psychological Bulletin* review, vol. vii, p.301.

- existence of Leuba review = **DIRECT PRIMARY via King citation**;

## 2. King's reply is methodological rather than merely defensive

King replies that holding consistently to one point of view can produce clarity and definiteness and asks whether the active standpoint can illuminate the subject. He then defines the larger problem in genetic terms: the relation of active human tendencies to the development of culture and religion.

He also explicitly distinguishes a **hypothesis** from a **verified fact**. A genetic hypothesis may be legitimate and useful so long as it is not allowed to masquerade as established history.

This matters for the current Lovejoy project because the controversy concerns not only substantive theory but the permitted evidentiary status of explanatory constructions.

Compact formulation:

> **A genetic hypothesis may organize inquiry before it is entitled to historical fact-status.**

This is a period American proof-threshold control; do not call it a Lovejoy-derived method.

## 3. King 1911 further decomposes the impersonal-power problem

Later in the same article King returns to the distinction between beliefs in `power` and beliefs in `spirits`.

He argues that neither belief, taken by itself, constitutes religion. Religious status depends on the attitude/use in which such beliefs enter rather than on the mere presence of a particular conceptual object.

This continues the component-wise reclassification already directly visible in his 1910 book:

- impersonal power field = retained;
- its explanatory/religious status = separately judged.

## 4. North-American `manitou` and the genealogy ceiling

King then discusses missionary descriptions of a North-American `Great Spirit`. He warns that missionaries may have interpreted `manitou` through a personal/deity framework although the underlying idea can instead denote an impersonal or quasi-mechanical essence/potency. He notes the wide distribution of related power ideas.

But he immediately separates two propositions:

1. **impersonal/quasi-mechanical power conceptions are widely attested**;
2. **such a conception is historically primitive or prior**.

For the second proposition King says, in substance, that one can only speculate.

This is a strong American publication-level proof partition:

`wide comparative attestation`

≠

`demonstrated historical primitiveness / priority`.

The point is especially useful because Lovejoy 1906 had itself aggregated North-American and transregional evidence into `primitive energetics`. King's 1911 restatement retains the comparative object while lowering the historical-priority claim.

## 5. Relation to Leuba 1912

Leuba 1912 later performs a different proposition-level grading of Lovejoy 1906:

- non-personal power/evidence field: retained;
- strong mechanical/regular-law characterization: contested;
- psychological genesis/priority: pursued through developmental psychology rather than simply inherited from ethnographic evidence.

No direct methodological borrowing should be inferred from King to Leuba or Lovejoy. The historical value lies in the surrounding American controversy ecology:

> **different propositions about the same comparative object repeatedly receive different evidentiary statuses and different proof channels.**
