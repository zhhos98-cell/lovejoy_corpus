The primary text materially changes the comparison. Steinmetz is even less compatible with a caricature of simple single-cause classification than earlier secondary controls suggested. He explicitly attacks `Simplomanie`, insists on distinguishing motives, uses negative conditions and counterexamples, and repeatedly says that hunger alone is insufficient for many surviving cases. Yet he ultimately retains a very strong universal phylogenetic hierarchy: ordinary corpse-eating is reconstructed as a necessary/near-universal consequence of primitive omnivory, hunger and the absence of inhibitions; animistic, ritual, magical and communion-like meanings are later motives or secondary refunctionalizations.

The sharp historical problem is therefore:

> **plural/counterfact-sensitive local explanation + strong universal origin hierarchy**
>
> versus
>
> **Lovejoy 005's later redivision of cannibalistic/ritual forms by operative mechanism without allowing one reconstructed origin to settle the classification of later cases.**

This is a more exact and more interesting contrast than `Steinmetz generalizes; Lovejoy distinguishes`.

## 2. Before the requested packet: Steinmetz explicitly attacks one-motive explanation

The methodological lead-in to §§16–22 is essential for interpreting the requested pages.

Steinmetz argues that the solution of the problem depends on recognizing and clearly distinguishing motives. He warns against simply asking ethnographers for their opinions, which he calls one-sided, and against selecting any plausible motive and then treating it as sufficient by itself. He labels this tendency `Simplomanie` and treats it as an enemy of scientific ethnology.

He then converts the origin question into a set of testable negative conditions. Among the conditions he thinks would make habitual corpse-eating possible are:

- need/desire for meat;
- insufficient aesthetic disgust toward corpses;
- absence of fear that the dead person's spirit will resent the act;
- absence of imagined sympathy with the corpse;
- absence of the feeling that consumption desecrates the person or memory.

The logic is explicitly comparative and counterfactual: if those inhibitions are absent in the reconstructed primitive condition, ordinary corpse-eating becomes possible without requiring a positive religious doctrine.

### Consequence for the old historiographic contrast

Do **not** describe Steinmetz as starting from a single ritual or religious explanation. He is doing the opposite at this stage: decomposing the phenomenon into enabling conditions and testing them separately.

## 3. §§16–22 / pp.36–47: a negative-condition programme, not a simple motive list

The requested continuous packet is now intelligible as a sequence of tests.

### §16 onward — omnivory/meat demand

Steinmetz asks whether primitive humans were herbivorous, carnivorous or omnivorous and argues from comparative anatomy, animal behavior and ethnographic diet toward omnivory and a strong demand for meat. His intended conclusion is that anthropophagy need not initially require a special motive distinct from ordinary nutritional desire.

### §19 — disgust/taste

He then asks whether the modern disgust toward corpses/putrefaction is historically universal. The section accumulates ethnographic examples of foods, carrion, blood and bodily substances unacceptable to European taste, using them as evidence that aesthetic disgust cannot simply be projected backward.

### §§20–22 — fear, sympathy, solidarity and hunger

The following sequence tests other proposed inhibitions: resentment of the dead spirit, pity toward the corpse, solidarity with relatives, and hunger.

The important result is not `hunger explains everything`. On printed p.46 Steinmetz explicitly separates episodic hunger-cannibalism from habitual endocannibalism and argues that hunger by itself is inadequate: endocannibal peoples are not always starving, and the economically poorest groups are not thereby the most characteristic practitioners.

Thus even inside his nutritional-origin theory, hunger is only one causal component combined with the absence of inhibitions.

## 4. Printed p.45: the Mauss citation needs a more exact reading

Mauss later cites Steinmetz p.45 col.2 in connection with magical vengeance by a victim/dead person and rites such as mutilating a corpse or consuming parts. The primary p.44–45 run allows a more careful account.

Steinmetz's surrounding examples include:

- North Queenslanders exposing themselves to substances from a decomposing corpse in order to acquire the dead person's strength;
- witches or specialists using corpse matter as magical material;
- deliberate rough treatment of corpses as protection against the dead person's return or vengeance — binding, breaking limbs, mutilating hands/fingers, staking or otherwise disabling the corpse.

The page is therefore indeed about corpse manipulation, magical danger and transfer/protection. But the recovered OCR does **not** justify simplifying p.45 itself to `selected-part eating = communion`. Mauss is drawing a broader comparative point from a cluster whose primary page includes mutilation, magical material, strength acquisition and anti-ghost prophylaxis.

This makes p.45 useful as a control on how later critics reframe Steinmetz's evidence.

## 5. Printed p.52 §26: the transfer-of-vital-force mechanism is explicitly secondary in Steinmetz

This is the most important primary correction in the whole packet.

Section 26 is headed `Der Animismus als Ursache des Endokannibalismus` and asks whether animism itself could have caused habitual endocannibalism.

Steinmetz answers increasingly negatively. He says that animism by itself could not have caused the custom; it could furnish **new motives**, preserve/support an already existing practice, and adapt it to later conditions.

He then makes the historical layering explicit:

- an originally nutritional use of the corpse could become, or be theoretically supported by, the idea of appropriating the dead person's soul or **vital force**;
- an animistic motive could be inserted later as an explanation of an already existing custom;
- sentimental explanations such as protecting the corpse from worms or honoring it can likewise be later rationalizations rather than original causes;
- killing/eating old or sick persons may acquire the idea that the soul should be absorbed while still vigorous;
- purely ceremonial reduced forms of corpse-eating belong to this later animistic layer.

Therefore the old comparison must be sharpened:

> **Steinmetz already distinguishes the transfer of soul/vital force from the original cause of endocannibalism. He treats it as a later motive or reinterpretation layered onto a pre-existing nutritional custom.**

This is not Mauss's later classificatory result and it is not Lovejoy's later mechanism taxonomy.

## 6. Printed pp.59–60: plural local motives feed into a strong universal origin claim

The conclusion shows why Steinmetz remains historiographically distinct despite the methodological sophistication above.

Immediately before the conclusion he considers communion/alliance theories associated with Lippert/Robertson Smith and the common-meal framework. He allows that such a religious interpretation could give endocannibalism a higher consecration and preserve it after the original motives had disappeared. But he assigns that to a relatively high cultural stage and explicitly separates it from the primitive origin he is reconstructing.

He then makes the hierarchy unmistakable:

- superstition or religion could not plausibly have led humans into cannibalism if periodic hunger had not already done so;
- primitive humans were omnivorous and lacked the later inhibitions against corpse-eating;
- combining his deductive reconstruction with the distribution of observed endocannibal practices yields, in his judgment, a high probability that endocannibalism was a standing custom of primitive humans and lower peoples;
- where his specified conditions held, the consequence is presented as effectively necessary;
- ritual/magical consumption of invalids, old persons or criminals is classified as **secondary**.

The tension is therefore real:

> **anti-simplification at the level of motives and surviving cases**
>
> + **strong unilinear priority at the level of reconstructed origin**.

That is the feature the Lovejoy comparison should target.

## 7. Mauss's intervention is now easier to specify

The existing Batch 41 control can now be read against Steinmetz's own p.52 rather than against a synopsis.

Mauss narrows `endocannibalisme` to `l'anthropophagie rituelle des parents`, emphasizes transfer of the dead person's virtues to survivors, and places the practice in religious/familial solidarity and communion. He also cites Marillier's 1896 Melanesian taboo dossier.

The primary Steinmetz text shows that Mauss is not merely repeating Steinmetz's own causal classification. Steinmetz had subordinated vital-force appropriation and ceremonial forms to a secondary animistic layer after an originally nutritional practice. Mauss instead reclassifies the surviving ritual practice by **social relation and religious mechanism**.

Thus the difference is:

`Steinmetz: reconstructed origin hierarchy governs historical interpretation`  
versus  
`Mauss: relational/ritual mechanism becomes the relevant classificatory level for the surviving form`.

No direct Lovejoy-reading-of-Mauss claim follows from this.

## 8. Lovejoy 005: a further change of classificatory level

Notebook 005 records the exact title `Endokannibalismus — Steinmetz` and later decomposes anthropophagy/cannibalism into several distinct problems:

- famine/nutritional necessity;
- revenge/hatred;
- affection or kin relation;
- acquisition of qualities;
- sympathetic/transferable efficacy;
- propitiation;
- sacramental communion;
- selected-organ transfer of intelligence/courage/sexual power.

The key difference from Steinmetz is **not** that Lovejoy recognizes multiple motives and Steinmetz does not. Steinmetz explicitly does.

The more defensible difference is that Lovejoy repeatedly refuses to let a reconstructed first origin settle the explanatory identity of later cases. Ceremoniality, bodily incorporation, repeated public performance or kin relation are each tested for what they actually diagnose. Cases are redistributed according to current causal bearer/mechanism and historical layer.

The revised sequence is therefore:

> **Steinmetz: multi-factor/counterfact-sensitive comparison → universal nutritional-origin hierarchy → religious/magical motives as secondary layers**
>
> **Mauss: surviving kin anthropophagy reclassified by ritual relation/communion and transfer of virtues**
>
> **Marillier: exact 1896 review still pending**
>
> **Lovejoy 005: later cases decomposed without allowing one origin narrative to exhaust their causal classification.**

## 9. The Marillier review question is now much sharper

Before recovery of Steinmetz's own text, the main question for Marillier's RHR 34 review was:

> `does Marillier classify transfer as communion, magic, funerary solidarity or plural mechanism?`

That remains useful but is no longer sufficient.

The decisive question now is:

> **Does Marillier accept Steinmetz's distinction between primary nutritional origin and secondary animistic/ritual refunctionalization? Does he challenge the universal origin inference while preserving the local motive analysis? Or does he reclassify the surviving forms at the mechanism/relational level, closer to what Mauss later does?**

This makes pp.113–118 even more valuable: they can locate Marillier inside a very specific dispute over the level at which historical explanation should be allowed to govern classification.
