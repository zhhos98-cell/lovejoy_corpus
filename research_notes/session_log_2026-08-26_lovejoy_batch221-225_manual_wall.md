## 4. Batch224 — visible ritual severity is not a civilization scale

The previously underused 005 pp.67–76 provide a distinct negative control to Batch219.

Strong sequence:

- spectacular human sacrifice may differ from ordinary funerary killing because a ruler has different **political/status resources**, not because the religious mechanism is categorically different;
- Lovejoy cautions against ranking cultures by a simple scale;
- animal sacrifice is not automatically evidence of advancement over human sacrifice;
- killing a rain-maker for failure can be **punishment**, not sacrifice;
- ritual killing and cannibalism must be separated from sacrifice;
- one visible rite can carry provisionary, protective/magical, propitiatory, communicative or later reinterpreted motives.

Result:

> **ritual morphology / spectacularity ≠ developmental position.**

This is complementary to Batch219:

- suicide: apparent outcome is reallocated among selectors;
- sacrifice: apparent morphology is denied classificatory authority until function/social setting is established.

## 5. Batch225 — agriculture and domestication do not share a universal master clock

005 p.103 supplies a stronger chronological control.

Lovejoy tests an agrarian-sacrifice genealogy and identifies a possible `vicious circle` where the theory simultaneously needs agrarian rites to precede domestic-animal sacrifice while silently assuming domestication universally precedes agriculture.

He rejects that universal premise and notes that in Africa **agriculture can be anterior to domestication**; domestic animals need not provide agricultural labor.

pp.104–105 then preserve the distinction between:

- a historical relation that must be demonstrated;
- and a locally warranted developmental sequence that may be accepted when evidence supports it.

Result:

> **religious chronology cannot simply borrow an assumed universal subsistence chronology as its master developmental clock.**

This extends Batch209's chronological non-isomorphism across domains:

- subsistence/economic practice;
- domestication;
- ritual morphology;
- sacrificial mechanism;
- religious interpretation.
