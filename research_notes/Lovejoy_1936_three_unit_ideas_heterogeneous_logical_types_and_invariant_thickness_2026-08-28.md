# Lovejoy 1936 — the three `unit-ideas` are heterogeneous logical types

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: **MATURE-PRACTICE CONTROL / GATE-A TYPE CORRECTION / DIRECT LOVEJOY TEXT + RECEPTION CONTROL**

Primary: Arthur O. Lovejoy, *The Great Chain of Being* (1936), especially Lecture II and Lecture X, pp.52–59 and 323–333.

## Core result

The three principles that constitute Lovejoy's canonical Great Chain case do **not** appear to be one homogeneous formal kind of object.

They are better typed as:

1. **plenitude** — a modal/existential completeness principle;
2. **continuity** — a no-gap / adjacency / series-topology principle;
3. **unilinear gradation** — an ordering/ranking principle based on degrees of perfection, privation, potentiality, or powers.

Yet Lovejoy treats all three as the elemental ideas whose combination generated the Great Chain.

Therefore:

> **`unit-idea` in Lovejoy's mature practice is not itself a guarantee of homogeneous logical type. It is a historiographical role: an analytically discriminable component that can be tracked, recombined, and assigned historical effects.**

This materially revises any analyst model in which Gate A merely asks whether historical tokens belong to one equivalence class. Before equivalence can be tested, the project must first type **what sort of object is being held invariant**.

---

## I. Plenitude — modal/existential completeness

Lovejoy's recurrent formulation is that the universe is a `plenum formarum` in which the range of conceivable diversity is exhaustively exemplified: no genuine possibility/potentiality of being should remain unrealized.

Working formalization:

`genuine / compossible possibility`
→ `actual realization`.

The relevant invariants can concern:

- actualization of possibles;
- divine productive fullness;
- absence of arbitrary omissions;
- maximal diversity/fullness.

This is neither a class identity rule nor an ordering relation. It constrains the relation between possibility and actuality.

---

## II. Continuity — no-gap / adjacency relation

Lovejoy takes Aristotle's definition of continuum as an important source and stresses that later thinkers generalized it far beyond Aristotle's securely stated quantitative cases.

The mature Great-Chain form is approximately:

- no unexplained leaps;
- if an intermediate kind between two actual kinds is genuinely possible, it should be realized;
- neighboring forms should differ by the least possible degree;
- a series should contain no blank stretch of unrealized intermediate possibilities.

Working type:

`topological / adjacency / series-completeness relation`.

This is not identical with plenitude. Lovejoy says continuity was later treated as directly deducible from plenitude: if an intermediate form is possible, plenitude requires its realization.

Thus even within the canonical unit set:

`unit B` may be treated as an **implicate/corollary** of `unit A`.

That already complicates a strict elemental-atom picture.

---

## III. Gradation — ordering/ranking relation

Lovejoy traces to Aristotle several partial hierarchies:

- zoological gradation;
- hierarchy of `powers of soul`;
- a vaguer ontological scale tied to actuality/potentiality and privation.

His `unilinear gradation` is built by combining the more intelligible zoological/psychological hierarchies with the ontological idea that finite beings can be graded by the degree of unrealized potentiality / privation / perfection.

Working type:

`ordering / ranking function over beings`.

This differs formally from both:

- plenitude's possibility→actuality rule;
- continuity's adjacency/no-gap constraint.

Lovejoy's own historical reconstruction says this gradational principle was **added to** the assumptions of fullness and qualitative continuity.

---

## IV. The Great Chain is therefore a typed compound, not a molecule of like parts

The mature historical object is generated by combining heterogeneous rules:

`PLENITUDE` — which possibles must exist?

`GRADATION` — how are actual beings ranked?

`CONTINUITY` — what relation should obtain between adjacent positions / possible intermediates?

Together they yield the classic Chain:

`maximal realization`
+
`hierarchical ordering`
+
`no gaps / minimal adjacent difference`.

This makes the chemistry analogy less literal than even the usual `ideas can change` qualification suggests.

The supposed `elements` themselves can be:

- modal rules;
- order relations;
- topological/continuity constraints;
- propositions/principles;
- elsewhere in Lovejoy, presuppositions, metaphysical pathos, dialectical motifs, or common conceptual cores.

The category `unit-idea` is therefore **formally heterogeneous in practice**.

---

## V. Unit-ideas are also not logically independent atoms

Two direct mature controls are important.

### Continuity as an alleged implication of plenitude

Lovejoy says later tradition could directly deduce continuity from plenitude:

`possible intermediate`
+
`plenitude`
→ `intermediate must be realized`.

Thus two canonical unit-ideas can stand in an implication relation.

### Plenitude and continuity eventually conflict

In Lecture X, Lovejoy says that when their implications are pushed far enough, plenitude and continuity become mutually problematic.

His argument:

- plenitude demands maximal diversity of kinds;
- qualitative continuity requires difference to be reducible to degree along something common to the series;
- a genuinely new `quale` creates a breach in continuity;
- maximal diversity therefore tends to generate `leaps` rather than a smooth continuum.

Lovejoy explicitly concludes that plenitude and continuity, although the latter had been supposed implied by the former, are also **at variance with one another**.

This is decisive for the ontology of the `unit`:

> **a unit-idea can be historically tracked even though its logical relation to another canonical unit changes from alleged implication to recognized conflict.**

The history concerns propositions/principles and their historically changing articulation, not immutable formally independent atoms.

---

## VI. Gradation also has a thinner invariant than one fixed hierarchy

`Gradation` is not one immutable list of beings.

Its ranking variable can be expressed through different historical vocabularies:

- biological perfection;
- powers of soul;
- degree of actuality/potentiality;
- privation;
- later psychological or metaphysical criteria.

What Lovejoy retains as invariant is thinner:

> **finite beings can be ordered in a single ascending scale according to degree of some perfection/actuality/value-relevant property.**

This permits historical changes in:

- the entities ranked;
- the criterion of rank;
- the relation of God to the scale;
- whether the scale is static or temporalized.

Thus gradation already exhibits **variable criterion beneath stable ordering form**.

This is another Gate-A risk: at what point does a changed ranking variable produce a new idea rather than a variant of gradation?

Lovejoy does not state a general threshold.

---

## VII. Revised Gate A

Gate A can no longer be formalized merely as:

`historical token x ~ historical token y?`

It now has at least four subquestions:

### A0 — object typing

What kind of thing is the candidate unit?

- proposition;
- modal principle;
- ordering relation;
- topological/continuity relation;
- causal mechanism;
- systemic function;
- presupposition;
- semantic core;
- configurational pattern;
- affective/metaphysical pathos.

### A1 — invariant selection

What feature is being held fixed across occurrences?

### A2 — thickness

How much content is included in the invariant?

### A3 — tolerated transformation

Which changes are treated as historically variable accompaniments rather than identity-destroying changes?

Only after A0–A3 can the historian ask Gate B questions about logical, psychological, causal or genetic relations among the units.

---

## VIII. Consequence for the `logician-like` genealogy

The 1910 formal-logic paper is unusually strong for relation-space and inferential jurisdiction once classes/terms are given.

But the Great Chain's canonical units show why formal logic cannot by itself generate mature historiographical objects:

- the units are not even homogeneous formal types;
- their cross-context identity depends on substantive historical abstraction;
- one unit can be treated as derived from another;
- their logical relations can themselves change historically;
- their invariant thickness can differ.

Thus formal logic is best treated as a **technical articulation/sharpening site for Gate B**, not a sufficient theory of Gate A.

---

## IX. Reception pressure test

Jaakko Hintikka's later critique of plenitude is especially relevant because he argued that Lovejoy's prime unit is itself a compound and has different consequences in different contexts.

Gram/Martin/Wilson-style defenses move toward `family of laws` / structural resemblance.

Carl Knight later argues that this familial move conflicts with the elemental rhetoric of Lovejoy's chemistry analogy and proposes thinner elemental components.

The current project need not choose between these camps globally.

The primary Great Chain case itself supports a prior historical conclusion:

> **Lovejoy's actual canonical units already occupy different formal roles, and his practice permits transformation and internal logical reconfiguration.**

The analyst's task is therefore to type each unit and reconstruct its actual recognition criterion rather than assume one universal ontology of unit-ideas.

---

## X. Current shorthand

> **The Great Chain is compounded from heterogeneous kinds of unit: plenitude is a modal completeness rule, continuity a no-gap relation, gradation an ordering rule. `Unit-idea` is consequently a historiographical function, not a homogeneous formal species. This makes object-typing an antecedent Gate A0 before cross-context identity can even be tested.**
