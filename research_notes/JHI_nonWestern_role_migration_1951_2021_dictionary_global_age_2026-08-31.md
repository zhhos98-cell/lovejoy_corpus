# JHI non-Western role migration, 1951–2021 — from reaction/reception to global translation

Date: 2026-08-31
Status: **DIRECT ISSUE / REFERENCE-CANON / ROLE-MIGRATION CONTROL / ANTI-TRIUMPHALIST**

## Core result

The long history of non-Western material in JHI is better described by **role migration** than by a binary `absent -> global` story.

Direct issue/reference evidence shows at least the following changing roles:

1. non-Western actors as recipients/rejecters/reworkers of imported ideas;
2. non-Western intellectual traditions as internally reconstructed historical objects;
3. Asian traditions as sources/objects in cross-tradition influence histories;
4. inclusion inside a reference canon that remains predominantly Western-centered;
5. explicit early-2000s global-age / world-intellectual-history projects;
6. present issue-level execution organized around translation, asymmetry, intermediaries and global encounters.

Thus:

`NON-WESTERN PRESENCE`
!=
`SAME OBJECT ROLE`
!=
`REFERENCE-CANON SYMMETRY`
!=
`GLOBAL METHODOLOGICAL CONSENSUS`.

This matters to the Lovejoy blog because the paper already argues that Asian material can remain present while changing analytical function. JHI's own longer history supplies a field-level analogue, not a genealogy.

## I. 1951: `Chinese Reactions to Imported Ideas` makes reception/rejection a first-order object

JHI 12.1 (Jan. 1951) contains:

- Joseph Needham, `Human Laws and Laws of Nature in China and the West (I)`, 3–30;
- symposium `Chinese Reactions to Imported Ideas`, beginning 31;
- Arthur F. Wright, `Fu I and the Rejection of Buddhism`, 33–47;
- further symposium contributions including Chinese responses to imported intellectual formations.

Direct JSTOR issue:

https://www.jstor.org/stable/i346174

This is a significant role shift relative to an older schema in which `China` or `India` appears mainly as an antecedent/source for Western ideas.

Here:

`CHINESE ACTOR / TRADITION`
+
`IMPORTED IDEA`
->
`ACCEPTANCE / REJECTION / SYNCRETISM / TRANSFORMATION`.

Buddhism itself appears in Wright's title as an imported tradition being rejected by a named Chinese actor, not as a timeless regional essence.

Current safe proposition:

> **By 1951 JHI could organize a symposium around non-Western actors' reactions to imported ideas, including the reception/rejection of Buddhism inside Chinese intellectual history.**

Do not infer that this role dominated the journal or produced regional symmetry.

## II. 1955: `The Hindu Philosophy of History` supplies an internal-tradition role

JHI 16.4 (Oct. 1955) includes `The Hindu Philosophy of History` among articles on Locke, Herder, Emerson, historiography and other topics.

Direct JSTOR issue:

https://www.jstor.org/stable/i346193

At title/object level this is distinct from `India as source for Europe` or `Indian evidence in a primitive comparison`.

The object is an internally named philosophical/historiographical tradition.

Therefore:

`NON-WESTERN OBJECT`
can function as
`INTERNAL INTELLECTUAL-HISTORICAL OBJECT`.

Exact article argument/author should be direct-read before any more detailed claim.

## III. 1981: Buddhism appears as a cross-tradition intellectual encounter

Heinrich Dumoulin, `Buddhism and Nineteenth-Century German Philosophy`, JHI 42.3 (1981), 457–470.

Direct issue:

https://www.jstor.org/stable/i346268

Metadata:

https://philpapers.org/rec/DUMBAN

This is another distinct role:

`BUDDHISM`
-> `EUROPEAN / GERMAN PHILOSOPHICAL RECEPTION / INTERPRETATION`.

The object is neither Lovejoy's 1898 internal Pāli technical problem nor the 1951 Chinese reception of Buddhism. It is cross-tradition encounter/reception centered on German philosophy.

Thus `Buddhism in JHI` is itself not one stable object category.

## IV. 1973–74 Dictionary: inclusion and Western-centered canon coexist

Philip P. Wiener, ed., *Dictionary of the History of Ideas: Studies of Selected Pivotal Ideas* (Scribner, 1973–74).

Bibliographic controls:

https://onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/webbin/book/lookupid?key=olbp31715

https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BA00713278

Existing repo work has identified meaningful non-Western entries in the original Dictionary.

The crucial later JHI/director-board framing is preserved by the digitization notice quoted in Clio-online:

https://www.clio-online.de/webresource/id/webresource-15647?language=en

It describes the Dictionary as a culminating work in an earlier tradition and says it could still speak confidently about central ideas in the **Western tradition**, while non-Western traditions made only a **marginal appearance**, despite some relevant entries.

This supplies a direct reference-canon distinction:

`NON-WESTERN ENTRIES EXIST`
+
`CANON REMAINS PREDOMINANTLY WESTERN-CENTERED`.

Therefore:

`INCLUSION != SYMMETRY`.

This is stronger and more precise than saying the Dictionary simply excluded non-Western thought.

## V. 2005 New Dictionary: cross-cultural/global expansion becomes an explicit reference-architecture goal

Maryanne Cline Horowitz, ed., *New Dictionary of the History of Ideas* (Scribner, 2005).

Google Books preface/description control:

https://books.google.com/books/about/New_Dictionary_of_the_History_of_Ideas.html?hl=en&id=xoYUAQAAIAAJ&output=html_text

The description explicitly contrasts the original Dictionary as a landmark primarily of European thought with the new six-volume set's new content on:

- Africa;
- Asia;
- Europe;
- Latin America;
- North America;
- the Middle East.

It presents the new work as an expansive cross-cultural/global outlook.

This is an explicit **reference-canon revision**:

`OLD REFERENCE ARCHITECTURE`
-> `NEW REGIONAL / CROSS-CULTURAL ARCHITECTURE`.

Do not infer that all regions or intellectual traditions became equally represented or that the new reference work solved the politics of global knowledge production.

## VI. JHI 2005: `Intellectual History in a Global Age` makes global jurisdiction an internal methodological topic

JHI 66.2 (2005) includes:

- Ulrich Johannes Schneider, `The International Dictionary of Intellectual Historians`, 143–154;
- Donald R. Kelley, `Intellectual History in a Global Age`, 155–167.

Schneider institutional/open record:

https://ul.qucosa.de/landing-page/?tx_dlf%5Bid%5D=https%3A%2F%2Ful.qucosa.de%2Fapi%2Fqucosa%253A13161%2Fmets

The abstract says intellectual history is written everywhere, not only in Western Europe, and proposes treating it as a worldwide concept while explicitly noting that this complicates the category.

Kelley JSTOR:

https://www.jstor.org/stable/3654243

Kelley frames intellectual history as an interdisciplinary practice transformed by perspective/context and discusses internalist/externalist tensions.

Thus immediately before the 2006 JHI institutional relaunch/self-history, global jurisdiction is already an explicit methodological object within the journal.

Important control:

`GLOBAL AGE PROGRAMME`
!=
`PROOF OF GLOBAL EXECUTION AT EARLIER DATES`.

## VII. 2021: JHI issue-level global execution shifts the relation from influence to translation practice

JHI 82.3 (July 2021) contains a cluster beginning with:

Lisa Hellman and Birgit Tremml-Werner, `Translation in Action: Global Intellectual History and Early Modern Diplomacy`, 453–467.

Direct JSTOR issue:

https://www.jstor.org/stable/e27069771

JHI Blog contents:

https://www.jhiblog.org/2021/07/23/jhi-issue-82-3-now-available/

The cluster includes:

- multilingual foreign affairs in eighteenth-century Stockholm;
- translation/diplomacy in Central Asian borderlands;
- translating friendship across time/space;
- early modern Japanese concepts of rulership and political geography in translation.

The introductory abstract emphasizes:

- translators as diverse go-betweens, beyond official diplomats;
- asymmetrical power among actors, languages and communication forms;
- inaccuracies, misunderstanding and strategic representations;
- local translation practices as constitutive of global political encounters.

Direct abstract controls:

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34275942/

https://lnu.diva-portal.org/smash/record.jsf?pid=diva2%3A1593624

This is analytically different from a simple `idea A influences culture B` model:

`MULTIPLE INTERMEDIARY ACTORS`
+
`LANGUAGE / MEDIA / POWER ASYMMETRY`
+
`TRANSLATION PRACTICE`
->
`TRANSFORMED CROSS-CULTURAL POLITICAL MEANING`.

## VIII. Role migration map

The current direct sequence can be rendered:

### 1898 Lovejoy / JAOS

`BUDDHIST TERMS / INTERNAL TEXTUAL-SEMANTIC OBJECT`.

### 1951 JHI China symposium

`BUDDHISM / WESTERN IDEAS AS IMPORTED OBJECTS`
-> `LOCAL REJECTION / ACCEPTANCE / REWORKING`.

### 1955 JHI

`HINDU PHILOSOPHY AS INTERNAL HISTORICAL OBJECT`.

### 1981 JHI

`BUDDHISM -> GERMAN PHILOSOPHICAL RECEPTION / CROSS-TRADITION CONTACT`.

### 1973 Dictionary

`MEANINGFUL NON-WESTERN ENTRIES WITHIN WESTERN-CENTERED REFERENCE ARCHITECTURE`.

### 2005 global-age / New Dictionary

`WORLDWIDE INTELLECTUAL-HISTORICAL JURISDICTION + EXPLICIT REGIONAL REFERENCE-CANON EXPANSION`.

### 2021 JHI

`TRANSLATION / INTERMEDIARY / POWER-ASYMMETRY GLOBAL ENCOUNTER`.

These are not evolutionary stages through which all scholarship passed. They are different carrier/object roles visible at different dates.

## IX. Relation to Lovejoy blog

The blog already distinguishes early Buddhist/Indic material as a close technical/historical object from *Great Chain*, where Asian material often functions comparatively inside an Occidental narrative.

The JHI long-run sequence independently confirms the importance of asking:

`NON-WESTERN MATERIAL PRESENT AS WHAT?`

rather than merely:

`NON-WESTERN MATERIAL PRESENT?`.

This is a strong external calibration of the blog's object-role distinction.

Potential backstage formulation:

> **The larger field likewise did not move from non-Western absence to presence in one step. Asian materials appeared successively and simultaneously as internal traditions, sources, comparisons, imported objects, reception problems, and translated concepts; reference-canon expansion and journal execution changed at different rates.**

Do not insert this history into the current 1,880-word essay unless a specific editorial need arises.
