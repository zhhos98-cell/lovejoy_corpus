## 1. Oldenberg 1882 is Lovejoy's named positive source

Primary object: Hermann Oldenberg, *Buddha: His Life, His Doctrine, His Order*, trans. William Hoey (1882), excursus on `Upadhi` p.427, `Upadana` p.429, `Upadisesa` p.433.

Lovejoy 1898 explicitly cites the English translation p.433 and praises Oldenberg's treatment. No inference about access is needed here: the published source relation is direct.

Oldenberg already supplies most of the semantic direction and principal canonical apparatus later used by Lovejoy:

- he prints the Itivuttaka passage supporting the usual physical-residue interpretation and treats it as genuine contrary evidence;
- he cites the Anāgāmin/Arahat contrast in which `sati va upādisese` cannot be differentiated simply by whether physical aggregates remain;
- he cites the already-deceased monk case, where `saupadisesa` can still be asked of a dead monk and therefore cannot simply mean `alive with body remaining`;
- he compares `upādāna` and `saṁyojana` and treats them as closely synonymous;
- he uses the poison/remnant analogy and other canonical evidence toward an ethical-residue reading.

Thus the ethical direction of `upādisesa/anupādisesa` is not a Lovejoy discovery.

## 2. Oldenberg's strong endpoint: `upādi = upadhi`

Oldenberg ultimately resolves the lexical problem through a confident orthographic/text-history hypothesis. He treats the problematic Pāli `upādi` as a spelling of `upadhi`, probably affected by manuscript assimilation, and uses this to explain the conflict between ethical and physical readings.

The important comparison is therefore not `Oldenberg ignores contradiction / Lovejoy notices it`. Oldenberg explicitly notices the contradiction.

The difference is narrower:

- Oldenberg uses a strong orthographic-genealogical solution to close the contradiction;
- Lovejoy accepts much of Oldenberg's semantic result while refusing to make etymology/textual genealogy carry that final burden.

## 3. The nineteenth-century lexical fork is now page-level controlled

Lovejoy p.134 deliberately sets two derivational lines against one another.

### Childers / Rhys Davids line

Childers's dictionary tradition derives `upādi` from `upādā/upāda`, treats it as a name for the five khandhas, and supports the usual distinction:

- `saupadisesa` — skandhas remain;
- `anupadisesa` — no skandhas remain.

The original Childers facsimile remains citation hygiene, but the entry's content is securely reproduced in a close modern transcription.

T. W. Rhys Davids is now direct-primary controlled in *Buddhism: Being a Sketch...*, revised ed. (1894), **p.113**. The exact wording behind Lovejoy's quotation describes `upādi` as a comprehensive name for the five skandhas, derived in allusion to their cause `upādāna` from `upādā`, `to grasp`.

This side of the fork is therefore no longer a relay-only reconstruction.

### Müller / Dahlmann line

Lovejoy cites:

- Edward Müller, *A Simplified Grammar of the Pali Language* (1884), `p.30`;
- Joseph Dahlmann, *Nirvāṇa* (1896), `p.14`.

The direct return materially corrects the old Batch41 state.

**Müller:** printed p.30 contains consonantal phonology and no relevant `upādi/upadhi` statement. The relevant aspiration-loss correspondence is on **printed p.36**, where the OCR gives the relevant `upādi/upadhi` relation with an Oldenberg reference. Unless another pagination witness explains the discrepancy, Lovejoy's published `p.30` is probably a **six-page citation slip**. Do not silently emend the Lovejoy footnote.

**Dahlmann p.14:** direct primary argues for a close/internal relation between Buddhist `anupādisesanibbāna` and orthodox `anupādhīśeṣanirvāṇa`, while warning that the orthodox substantial-self meaning cannot simply be transferred into Buddhism. Lovejoy compresses this broader doctrinal/historical comparison into the `upadhi ↔ upādi` side of his etymological fork.

The lexical fork is therefore now page-level secure, and its two sides are more internally complex than Lovejoy's compressed footnote presentation suggests.

## 4. Lovejoy's decisive move: etymological underdetermination changes the tribunal

Lovejoy does not choose one genealogy and declare the other false. His published sequence is:

1. Childers/Rhys Davids and Müller/Dahlmann support incompatible derivational stories;
2. **both derivations appear etymologically possible**;
3. therefore the meaning of `upādi` must be settled **without reference to etymology**;
4. semantic judgment shifts to patterned usage and doctrinal contrast.

The methodological structure is:

> **underdetermined word-history → suspend derivational adjudication → reconstruct semantic function from textual distribution.**

This is stronger and more precise than the generic slogan `usage over etymology`: etymology is bracketed because it lacks discriminating power between live semantic hypotheses.

## 5. Lovejoy extends and reweights Oldenberg's textual package

Lovejoy retains Oldenberg's principal positive evidence but changes the evidentiary package.

Secure inherited/reused elements include:

- Anāgāmin versus Arahat;
- the already-deceased monk;
- `upādāna ≈ saṁyojana`;
- the ethical-residue direction;
- recognition of contrary physical-residue passages.

Lovejoy's notebook and print add/repackage further evidence, including a third Sutta-Nipāta passage in which `anupādisesa` functions as an ethical/religious goal and a `hither/further shore` comparison. These were not located in the recovered 1882 English Oldenberg text; treat them as additions to this recovered source package, not proof that Oldenberg never used them elsewhere.

Notebook 004 p.66 / manuscript p.173 contains the especially strong manuscript→print hinge: physical death cannot itself be the Buddhist `summum bonum`; `anupādisesa` in the cited context must concern freedom from attachment rather than mere disappearance of the skandhas. Much of this argument passes into Lovejoy 1898 nearly verbatim.

## 6. Lovejoy's confidence level remains lower than Oldenberg's final orthographic solution

Lovejoy says the oldest and probably most numerous passages favor Oldenberg's ethical reading, but he also preserves contrary texts and ends that the matter **cannot be regarded as finally settled**.

He allows scribal/homophonic confusion among `upādi`, `upadhi`, and Sāṃkhyan `upadhi` as a possible explanation rather than turning that possibility into a fully settled textual history.

The secure difference is therefore:

> **Oldenberg reaches a confident orthographic resolution; Lovejoy retains the semantic preference while leaving the textual genealogy epistemically open.**

This is evidentiary recalibration, not independent semantic discovery.

The common operation is:

> **separate evidence for genealogy from evidence for current semantic/function; do not let one level settle the other without an independent warrant.**

## 8. Relation to 1902

By 1902 the same separation appears at larger scale:

- conceptual affinity does not prove historical borrowing;
- recurring categories/logical motives can be compared without assuming identical historical derivation;
- internal genealogy can explain a configuration that resembles an external tradition.

This is an operational continuity, not proof that Lovejoy consciously generalized the `upādisesa` exercise as a named method.
