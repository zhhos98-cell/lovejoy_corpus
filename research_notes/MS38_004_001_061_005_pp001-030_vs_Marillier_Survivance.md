## 1. Marillier’s secure thesis

### A. Immortality is not originally generated by a demand for justice

Marillier opens by attacking a common theory: that belief in immortality arises because human beings need another world in which divine justice can compensate for earthly injustice. His paper is organized against that assumption.

The 1893 EPHE report states the conclusion in compressed form: the world of the dead resembles the world of the living; rank in the other world follows earthly rank rather than moral merit; the afterlife is **continuation rather than compensation**; and belief in survival is not founded on moral conceptions but is treated as a kind of explanatory theory.

The 1898–1899 EPHE report, from the course Lovejoy attended, says Marillier’s essential task was still to establish that no moral element enters eschatology at the origin and that the other life is continuation rather than compensation, reparation or punishment.

### B. Death is initially treated as accidental/non-natural and therefore mythically explained

Marillier’s 1893 course summary says that death is not initially regarded as a natural and normal phenomenon. The course therefore collected legends explaining how death entered the world.

### C. Social condition persists after death

The same summary states that the land of the dead is structurally similar to the living world and that post-mortem rank corresponds to rank during life, not to moral worth.

### D. Ritual performance and manner of death can determine afterlife without constituting moral retribution

Mauss’s 1896 defense of Marillier is crucial here. Citing Marillier directly, he argues that differentiated afterlives remain compatible with the “theory of continuation” when inequality persists, when rites determine destiny, or when the **manner of death** determines the state of the shade. This is an important control against reading every differentiated destiny as moral judgment.

### E. Imported Christian, Hindu or Buddhist ideas must be separated from earlier evidence

Mauss, again citing Marillier, specifically warns that apparently moralized eschatologies among several peoples are affected by Christian influence, while Indian/Indonesian cases can show Hindu-Buddhist influence; he even notes the Sanskrit term `naraka` as evidence of borrowing. This gives direct contemporary support for the source-critical operation repeatedly visible in Lovejoy’s notebook.

## 3. What Lovejoy appears to take over from Marillier

### 3.1 The initial burden of proof

Lovejoy begins from Marillier’s central negative thesis: do not call an afterlife conception moral merely because it contains differential destinies, rewards-looking benefits or punishment-looking exclusions. First establish that destiny is actually linked to **desert**.

This is more than shared subject matter. It determines the notebook’s recurring question: *what is the causal criterion selecting the dead for one outcome rather than another?*

### 3.2 Continuation as the default model

Earthly rank, occupation, power, social relations and physical needs continue after death. Lovejoy repeatedly treats this as the baseline against which true retribution must be identified.

### 3.3 Critical treatment of contaminated ethnography

The notebook does not treat ethnographic testimony as transparent. Missionary contact, imported Hindu/Buddhist concepts and later reinterpretation can make an observed afterlife system look more moralized than its earlier strata were. This is directly consonant with the Marillier–Mauss position in the 1890s.

### 3.4 Manner of death and ritual as non-moral selectors

This is one of the strongest conceptual continuities. Drowning, suicide, childbirth, ritual performance and bodily treatment can determine post-mortem position without implying moral recompense. Lovejoy repeatedly uses these cases to police the boundary of the category “moral.”

## 4. Where Lovejoy goes beyond Marillier

### A. From binary opposition to a multi-stage taxonomy

Marillier’s published polemic is framed primarily as **continuation versus retribution**. Lovejoy keeps that distinction but inserts intermediate mechanisms:

1. naturalistic continuation;
2. magical/ritual qualification;
3. social/clan/community determination;
4. transitional or “half-moral” differentiation;
5. strictly moral guilt/desert and judgment.

This is probably the single most important intellectual transformation in pp. 1–30.

### B. Lovejoy converts anomalies into diagnostic cases

Suicide is exemplary. Instead of filing it under “punishment,” he asks whether the real mechanism is injury to the clan, violation of custom, exclusion from communal rights, taboo, or imported moralization. The notebook therefore works by **decomposing ambiguous cases** rather than merely accumulating examples for a thesis.

### C. Historical contamination becomes part of explanation

Missionary influence is not just a reason to discard evidence. Lovejoy is interested in how imported moral categories can infiltrate an older non-moral structure and create hybrid systems. This anticipates the later notebook emphasis on syncretism and reinterpretation in sacrifice.

### D. The notebook begins to explain moralization, not merely deny its primitiveness

By pp. 24–27 Lovejoy is asking how differentiated destinations, judgment and trial could arise out of non-moral continuation. That is a constructive historical problem not exhausted by Marillier’s original negative thesis.

## 5. A likely pedagogical sequence

The documentary evidence now supports the following reading of the first 30 pages:

1. **Marillier supplies the research problem**: survival after death is originally non-moral continuation, not compensation for earthly injustice.
2. **Course material supplies comparative cases**: origin-of-death myths, lands of the dead, social rank, manner of death, ritual conditions and American ethnography.
3. **Lovejoy turns the thesis into a classificatory exercise**: what exactly counts as moral, ritual, social, magical or naturalistic determination?
4. **Lovejoy begins a historical model of moralization**: imported ideas, social differentiation and new judgment machinery can transform an older continuation scheme.

This is why notebook 005 should no longer be described simply as “notes influenced by Marillier.” The first block looks much more like **active seminar/research work inside Marillier’s survivance problem, already being analytically reworked by Lovejoy**.

## 6. Relation to the later 1906 paper

The survivance section matters for the 1906 *Monist* article even though its subject is different. The method visible here is already the same one later used for sacrifice and “Primitive Energetics”:

- refuse a broad observer-supplied label;
- identify the actual mechanism determining an outcome;
- separate formally similar cases with different causes;
- detect borrowing, contamination and historical layering;
- reconstruct a more precise causal category.

In pp. 1–30 the disputed label is **moral retribution**. In pp. 46–119 it becomes **sacrifice/communion**. In 1906 it becomes **animism/supernaturalism**. The continuity is methodological rather than merely topical.
