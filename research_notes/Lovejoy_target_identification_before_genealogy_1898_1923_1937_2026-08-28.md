# Lovejoy — target identification before genealogy, 1898 / 1923 / 1937

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: **CANONICAL TARGET-IDENTIFICATION CONTROL / PRE-GENEALOGY GATE**

## Governing correction

The genealogy branch has increasingly differentiated direct edge, ambient tradition, causal weight, recipient prior state, alternatives and propagation. A prior gate must now be made explicit:

> **Before tracing the history or source of an idea, the historian must establish that the target text/system actually instantiates the idea being traced, at the relevant scale and in the relevant sense.**

Analyst shorthand:

`T = target-state identification`.

This is not a term used by Lovejoy. It names a problem that recurs in his practice across very different domains.

If `T` fails, subsequent source-work can become a genealogy of the wrong target.

---

# I. 1898 notebook — diagnostic specificity before historical attribution

Notebook 004's Sāṃkhya/Buddhism problem supplies an early local form of the gate.

A Buddhist passage may contain a doctrine or feature resembling Sāṃkhya. Lovejoy nevertheless asks whether the feature is sufficiently diagnostic of the proposed target system.

The notebook's own intervention after Rhys Davids argues that generic beliefs such as the eternity of matter/self have weak discriminatory power. For the specific historical task, a more characteristic Sāṃkhya marker is the separation of Self and World / thorough-going dualism.

The inferential sequence is:

`observed resemblance`
→ `what target is being identified?`
→ `what feature is diagnostic of that target?`
→ `seek a better textual witness`
→ only then consider genealogy/system chronology.

Thus target identification logically precedes source attribution.

The same notebook also distinguishes:

`recognizable doctrine/feature`
≠ `the whole later system existed as such`.

This is target identification at a scale boundary.

# II. 1923 Rousseau — semantic target discrimination before classification/source history

Arthur O. Lovejoy, `The Supposed Primitivism of Rousseau's "Discourse on Inequality"`, *Modern Philology* 21.2 (1923), 165–186.

Lovejoy identifies a persistent historiographical error: treating Rousseau's *Discourse* as a straightforward glorification of the `state of nature` and hence as primitivism.

The repair begins not with source hunting but with **semantic target discrimination**.

`state of nature` can mean at least:

1. chronological state: earliest human condition;
2. juristic state: persons/groups outside a common government;
3. cultural state: least developed arts/sciences/non-political civilization.

Lovejoy explicitly notes that these senses need not have the same denotation.

He then distinguishes several stages inside Rousseau's pre-political history and shows that Rousseau's ideal valuation attaches neither to every `state of nature` nor simply to the first stage.

Only after this semantic/stage discrimination can source lineages such as Pufendorf/Voltaire, Montaigne/Pope, and Hobbes be assigned to the relevant propositions.

Operationally:

`received label`
→ `split senses`
→ `locate target proposition/stage`
→ `then classify sources/relations`.

This is a direct historiographical analogue of the 1898 diagnostic-specificity problem.

# III. 1937 Milton — mature stress test: upstream tradition can survive target dispute

Arthur O. Lovejoy, `Milton and the Paradox of the Fortunate Fall`, *ELH* 4.3 (1937), 161–179; reprinted 1948.

Current direct-primary text lock remains incomplete because the open-access Project MUSE edition is not machine-readable in the present environment. The argument is secure through Daniel J. Wilson's annotated bibliography and later Milton scholarship.

Lovejoy reconstructs a long Christian tradition of the `fortunate fall` / *felix culpa* conception and applies it to Milton's *Paradise Lost* XII.

Wilson records Lovejoy's conclusion that Milton's immediate source or sources cannot be positively identified, while the idea has a long tradition reaching at least to the fourth century.

But later Milton critics attack a logically prior step.

Virginia R. Mollenkott (1972) argues that Milton rejects the fortunate-fall doctrine rather than affirms it; Dennis Danielson later explicitly describes Lovejoy's Milton attribution as a hypothesis and contests its fit with Milton's theodicy.

The crucial architecture is:

`Christian tradition of idea X exists`
≠ `Milton text instantiates X`.

The first proposition can remain historically well supported even if the second is disputed.

Therefore:

> **A strong upstream genealogy does not repair a weak target-state identification.**

# IV. Target identification is typed by scale

`T` should not be treated as a binary switch.

Possible target levels include:

- lexical target: same word/expression?;
- semantic target: same sense?;
- propositional target: same claim/principle?;
- inferential target: same implication/presupposition?;
- functional target: same role in a system/argument?;
- configurational target: same compound/pattern?;
- movement/system target: same historical complex?;
- actor-position target: affirmed, entertained, reported, dramatized, ironized, or rejected?

The Milton dispute is especially useful because the same textual passage can be agreed upon while its **illocutionary/actor-position status** remains contested.

The historian must therefore specify `T_x`, not merely `T`.

---

# V. Relation to identity jurisprudence

Target identification comes before cross-context identity.

The sequence is:

`identify target object at context B`
→ `individuate candidate object at context A`
→ `test same-at-relation/scale x`
→ only then test historical/genetic connection.

Analyst shorthand:

`T_B`
→ `A =ₓ B ?`
→ `A →genetic B ?`

If target B is misidentified, both later questions are malformed.

This adds a new precision to the Skinner-facing problem. The issue is not merely whether Lovejoy's same-idea criterion survives contextual change. It is also whether the historian has correctly identified the actor's proposition/function in each context before comparison begins.

# VI. Relation to the genealogy vector

The current genealogy vector is:

`T × R × A × E × X × D × P × C`

where:

- `T` target-state identification;
- `R` recipient prior state;
- `A` ambient/tradition-level availability;
- `E` specific edge/contact/carrier;
- `X` alternative-route/exclusivity control;
- `D` delta/causal weight;
- `P` propagation;
- `C` relation-specific confidence.

The ordering matters. `T` is not merely another independent variable. It is logically prior to the causal variables.

A better workflow is:

1. **T — define/identify the target**;
2. **identity/common-core test** across candidate contexts;
3. **A/R — reconstruct ambient and recipient baseline**;
4. **E — test specific route/contact**;
5. **X — compare alternatives**;
6. **D — allocate causal delta/weight**;
7. **P — follow downstream operation**;
8. **C — assign confidence separately to every preceding proposition**.

---

# VII. Why this matters for Lovejoy's `same idea`

The unit-idea controversy often begins too late, with the question:

> How can Lovejoy claim the same idea recurs across contexts?

The case law suggests an earlier question:

> **How does he decide what idea is present in each context in the first place?**

1923 Rousseau shows that Lovejoy knew labels could conflate several historical targets. 1937 Milton shows that his own target identification could nevertheless remain contestable.

This is historically more useful than either a simple defense or rejection of unit-ideas.

Lovejoy's method contains machinery for preventing target conflation; its application can still fail.

---

# VIII. Governing formula revised

Previous shorthand:

`problem-dependent scale → typed relation → target identification → local warrant → controlled promotion`.

The order should now be sharpened:

> **problem-dependent scale → target individuation → typed relation → local warrant/confidence → controlled promotion → reaggregation.**

Genealogy then adds:

> **recipient baseline + ambient availability + edge + alternatives + causal delta + propagation.**

This formulation captures both the analytical strength and the historical fallibility visible in Lovejoy's case law.

---

# IX. Guardrails

Do not claim:

- Lovejoy explicitly formulated a `target-state identification` rule;
- 1898 diagnostic specificity, 1923 semantic discrimination, and 1937 Milton belong to one proven causal lineage;
- later Milton critics refute Lovejoy's reconstruction of the broader *felix culpa* tradition;
- a correct target identification by itself proves cross-context identity or influence;
- semantic precision guarantees correct actor-position attribution.

Preferred statement:

> **Across Lovejoy's practice, historical tracing repeatedly depends on a prior act of identifying the thing to be traced. His best work makes that act explicit; the Milton controversy shows that it remains a substantive historical judgment capable of error.**
