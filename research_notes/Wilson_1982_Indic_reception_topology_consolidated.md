## 1. Relation taxonomy

Wilson's bibliography exposes several non-equivalent relation types. Keep them separate.

- `W-SEE` — formal `See` cross-reference in a Wilson entry.
- `W-ANN` — relation asserted in Wilson's annotation prose but not encoded as `See`.
- `W-INTRO` — retrospective relation stated in Wilson's introduction.
- `W-IDX-SUBJ` — Wilson subject-index relation.
- `W-IDX-AUTH` — author-name index route.
- `W-IDX-TITLE` — title index route.
- `W-IDX-REC` — later item number attached to a title entry under Wilson's explicit rule that subsequent numbers indicate items commenting on that work.
- `S-IN` — incoming relation from a later secondary item in Wilson's bibliography.
- `P-INFER` — a relation independently reconstructed from Lovejoy primary texts by this repository. **Never recode this as a Wilson relation.**

The main historiographical lesson is methodological:

> **chronological adjacency, subject co-location, formal cross-reference, annotation-level comparison, and later reception are different kinds of relation.**

## 2. The explicit Buddhist nodes: 19 and 243

### Entry 19 — 1898 Buddhist technical article

Wilson entry19 is Lovejoy's `The Buddhistic Technical Terms upādāna and upādisesa`.

The recovered entry boundary directly establishes:

`19 → ∅ [outgoing W-SEE]`.

This is an entry-level fact, not a claim that no later item anywhere can refer to 19.

Wilson's subject index supplies the important positive relation:

`Buddhism, 19, 243`.

Thus:

`19 —[W-IDX-SUBJ: Buddhism]— 243`.

### Entry 243 — 1901 `Syllabus: The Philosophy of Buddhism`

Wilson records four lectures:

1. `The Place of Buddhism among the Philosophies of India`;
2–3. `Two Essential Formulas of Buddhism`;
4. `Buddhist Ethics and its Goal`.

The entry boundary likewise directly establishes:

`243 → ∅ [outgoing W-SEE]`.

The 1898 article and 1901 syllabus are therefore **categorically rejoined by the subject index**, but Wilson does not encode a formal entry-level developmental edge between them.

Safe formulation:

> **Wilson preserves a small explicit Buddhist corpus, but at the recovered formal cross-reference layer it is a subject-index cluster rather than a diachronic genealogy.**

Do not infer `19 caused 243` from the index.

## 3. Buddhism and `Otherworldliness` are partitioned in Wilson's index

Wilson's recovered subject headings are:

- `Buddhism = {19, 243}`;
- `Otherworldliness = {21, 22}`.

Entry21, `Religion and the Time-Process`, explicitly includes **Indian civilization** in Wilson's own annotation, yet it is routed through `Otherworldliness` rather than the recovered `Buddhism` heading.

Entry244 supplies a strong control on index selectivity. Wilson's annotation of the Stevenson syllabus explicitly uses `otherworldly ethics`, yet entry244 is not included under the `Otherworldliness` subject heading, which lists only 21–22.

Therefore Wilson's index is not a mechanical keyword concordance. It is an editorial classification system.

Consequences:

> **absence from a Wilson subject heading does not prove absence of a concept from the work.**

and

> **Wilson's bibliography can repartition primary materials that a later researcher may wish to reconnect at another analytical level.**

## 4. Entry176 — 1907 Deussen/Vedānta review: high annotation resolution, weak relational routing

Wilson entry176 concerns Deussen's *Outline of the Vedanta System of Philosophy, according to Shankara*.

The entry boundary establishes:

`176 → ∅ [outgoing W-SEE]`.

An exact `See 176` audit recovered no incoming formal edge. This remains a bounded negative result rather than an absolute proof against every OCR variant.

Wilson nevertheless makes the review retrievable through:

- `Deussen → 176 [W-IDX-AUTH]`;
- `Outline of the Védanta System of Philosophy → 176 [W-IDX-TITLE]`.

Inside the annotation, Wilson preserves higher conceptual resolution:

- Śaṅkara;
- Rāmānuja;
- Hindu philosophy;
- Lovejoy's recommendation to turn from excessive concentration on Śaṅkara toward Rāmānuja.

No separate subject-index headings for Rāmānuja, Śaṅkara, Hindu philosophy, or India were recovered in the audited alphabetic ranges.

This yields a visibility hierarchy:

1. **formal genealogical routing** — strongest relation layer;
2. **subject-index categorical continuity**;
3. **author/title bibliographic retrieval**;
4. **annotation-only semantic resolution**.

Entry176 survives strongly at levels 3–4 while remaining thin at level 1.

The direct primary review now exists separately in `lovejoy_1907_deussen_vedanta_primary_review_batch201.md` and supersedes Wilson for Lovejoy's own wording and argument.

## 5. The positive canonical control: 21–22–51–5

The Indic topology matters only when compared against a denser Wilson-encoded genealogy.

### Entries21–22

`21 Religion and the Time-Process → 5, 22, 51, 435 [W-SEE]`

`22 Dialectic of Bruno and Spinoza → 21, 51, 435 [W-SEE]`.

Thus:

`21 ↔ 22 [W-SEE]`.

Wilson's introduction also identifies 21–22 as early history-of-ideas work:

`21–22 [W-INTRO]`.

Wilson's annotation of 22 connects it to the later *Great Chain* even though entry22's own `See` line does not include 5:

`22 → 5 [W-ANN]`.

### Entry51 — `The Obsolescence of the Eternal` (1909)

Wilson gives:

`51 → 5, 21–22, 118 [W-SEE]`.

This closes a dense triangular bridge:

```text
21 Religion and the Time-Process
 ↕
22 Dialectic of Bruno and Spinoza
 ↘   ↙
 51 Obsolescence of the Eternal
  ↓
 5 Great Chain of Being
```

This is a formal/retrospective genealogy of much greater relation density than the recovered 19/243/176 Indic cluster.

The difference is **bibliographic topology**, not proof of differential historical importance.

## 6. Wilson title-index reception: an explicit positive/negative control

Wilson states in his index instructions that for title entries:

- the first number is the annotation number of the work itself;
- subsequent numbers identify items that `in some fashion` comment on that work.

Define:

`W-IDX-REC` = subsequent commentary number in a Wilson title-index entry.

### Indic nodes

Wilson's title index gives only the primary number for:

- `The Buddhistic Technical Terms ... , 19`;
- `Syllabus: The Philosophy of Buddhism, 243`;
- `Outline of the Védanta System of Philosophy, 176`.

Thus:

`19 ← ∅ [W-IDX-REC]`

`243 ← ∅ [W-IDX-REC]`

`176 ← ∅ [W-IDX-REC]`.

### Positive control

Wilson gives:

`The Dialectic of Bruno and Spinoza, 22, 435`.

Thus:

`435 → 22 [W-IDX-REC]`.

Entry435 is Wilson's own 1980 retrospective essay relating Lovejoy's early work to *The Great Chain of Being*.

Safe conclusion:

> **Within Wilson's own title-index architecture and his secondary-literature cutoff, no later item is indexed as commenting on entries19, 243, or176, while entry22 has an explicit reception edge to435.**

This is not a field-wide claim that nobody discussed the Indic items before 1981.

## 7. The evolution line supplies a second explicit historiographical genealogy

Wilson's evolution network is important because it shows another way early work becomes retrospectively methodological.

### Primary routing

Entry26, `Some Eighteenth Century Evolutionists`, points to 17, 48, 54, 57.

Target resolution adds:

- 54 = `Kant and Evolution` (1910–11), a context-sensitive correction of false precursor construction;
- 57 = `Buffon and the Problem of Species` (1911), with `57 → 26,48`, producing a reciprocal `26 ↔ 57` relation;
- 48 = `The Argument for Organic Evolution Before 'The Origin of Species'`, a shared downstream target of 26 and57 but itself lacking a recovered formal `See` line.

The exact topology is therefore not a fully reciprocal triangle:

```text
26 ↔ 57
 \   /
   48
```

### Secondary methodological reception

Wilson's secondary corpus explicitly preserves two historiographical interpretations.

Kathleen E. Duffin (1980) argues, as summarized by Wilson, that emergent evolution supplied a basis for Lovejoy's **genetic method of historical analysis** and temporalism and that history-of-ideas methodology was indebted to his reflection on evolution.

John Herman Randall Jr. (1963) locates beginnings of Lovejoy's history-of-ideas interests partly in **evolutionism and rationalism**, emphasizing the discrimination and collection of ideas.

These accounts differ in detail but converge in assigning formative methodological importance to evolutionism.

Therefore Wilson's bibliography preserves at least two dense retrospective routes toward mature history of ideas:

1. religion / temporalism / Platonic-Great-Chain route;
2. evolution / emergence / genetic-history route.

The explicitly Buddhist/Indic nodes do not receive a comparable Wilson-encoded methodological genealogy.

Target resolution also confirms that Wilson uses formal cross-references to build other long-range subnetworks.

### Monboddo

### Academic freedom / religious neutrality

Entry25 points to:

These controls show that the absence of equally dense routing around 19/243/176 is not merely a general property of Wilson's bibliography. He actively creates longitudinal relations where he judges them bibliographically useful.

Again, the graph shows **Wilson's and the represented secondary literature's retrieval architecture**, not Lovejoy's intentions.

## 9. Wilson's June 1981 cutoff bounds all negative reception claims

Wilson says he attempted a complete secondary bibliography, so far as he knew, through **June 1981**, while acknowledging possible missed materials.

Therefore the correct negative formulation is:

> **Within Wilson's near-comprehensive secondary bibliography through June 1981, no indexed commentary edge has been recovered for entries19, 243, or176.**

Do not write:

> `nobody before 1981 discussed Lovejoy's Buddhist/Indic work`.

An external survey is required for that field-wide proposition.

The external post-1981 survey has already been run separately; see Batch200.

## 10. Historiographical consequence: bibliographic repartition + reception asymmetry

The primary corpus now makes an 1898–1907 Indic recurrence directly visible:

- 1898 specialist Buddhist article;
- 1901 Buddhism syllabus;
- 1902 Indian/Buddhist material inside `Religion and the Time-Process`, including explicit self-citation to 1898;
- 1904 Indic/Vedānta material within the Bruno–Spinoza article;
- 1907 direct Deussen/Rāmānuja review.

Wilson's architecture does not erase those objects. Instead it distributes them among different routes:

- `Buddhism` subject index;
- `Otherworldliness` / temporalism;
- Western-philosophy history;
- author/title review metadata;
- annotation-only Indic distinctions.

At the same time, Wilson's represented secondary literature supplies explicit methodological genealogies for 21–22 and evolution, but no comparable indexed reception genealogy for 19/243/176.

The strongest historiographical mechanism is therefore two-stage:

> **bibliographic repartition of the primary materials + asymmetrical secondary reception of the explicitly Indic nodes.**

This can make an actual recurrent Indic trajectory harder to retrieve even though the individual objects remain bibliographically present.

## 11. Do not overclaim intent or causation

This dossier supports claims about:

- Wilson's bibliographic relation architecture;
- differences in retrieval granularity;
- indexed secondary-reception asymmetry through June 1981;
- contrast between Indic nodes and denser Great-Chain/evolution networks.

It does **not** prove:

- that Wilson deliberately marginalized Buddhism or India;
- that Lovejoy himself considered the Buddhist work unimportant;
- that Buddhist philology caused his mature history-of-ideas method;
- that no pre-1981 scholar ever mentioned entries19/243/176;
- that a sparse index graph equals intellectual discontinuity.

For actual intellectual continuity use Lovejoy primary texts and manuscript/source-transformation dossiers. For post-1981 reception use Batch200.

## 12. Canonical concise formulation

> **Wilson's 1982 bibliography preserves Lovejoy's early Buddhist and Indic objects but gives them a thinner relation topology than the early works retrospectively tied to the Great Chain or to evolution. The 1898 article and 1901 syllabus are reunited under the subject heading `Buddhism`; the 1907 Vedānta review remains author/title retrievable and semantically rich in annotation; none of the three has a Wilson title-indexed secondary-commentary edge through the bibliography's June 1981 cutoff. By contrast, 21–22–51–5 form a dense formal/retrospective chain, and evolution receives explicit secondary methodological genealogies in Randall and Duffin. The result is not an Indic disappearance from the bibliography but a combination of bibliographic repartition and asymmetrical retrospective reception.**
