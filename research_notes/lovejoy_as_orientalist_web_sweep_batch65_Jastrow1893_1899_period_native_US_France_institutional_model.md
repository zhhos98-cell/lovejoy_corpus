# Batch 65 — Jastrow 1893/1899: a period-native American model of Harvard’s distributed field and Paris’s concentrated `sciences religieuses`

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced
Scope: strengthen the U.S.–France comparison with contemporary American primary statements rather than retrospective institutional synthesis.

## Core result

The project’s current contrast — **American distributed infrastructure versus Paris concentrated workshop** — can now be grounded in a period-native American disciplinary self-description.

Morris Jastrow Jr. wrote two unusually useful programmatic essays, one before Lovejoy entered Harvard and one during Lovejoy’s Paris year / immediate return horizon:

- `Recent Movements in the Historical Study of Religions in America`, *The Biblical World* 1.1 (1893), 24–32;
- `The Historical Study of Religions in Universities and Colleges`, *Journal of the American Oriental Society* 20 (1899), 317–325.

Taken together, they describe almost exactly the institutional asymmetry through which Lovejoy moved.

In 1893 Jastrow presents the American study of religions as an emerging network distributed among existing specialists, courses, clubs, museums and public lecture organizations. He singles out Harvard as the first American university, to his knowledge, to introduce the subject into the curriculum and lists Everett, Toy, Lanman and others as supplying different specialist components. At the same time he explicitly identifies the French model — Collège de France + EPHE Section des Sciences Religieuses + RHR + Musée Guimet — as the more adequate departmental form because the subject requires cooperation among many specialists.

In 1899 he makes the same point even more forcefully: the history of religions is the `child of comparative philology` and foster child of archaeology, but cannot properly be carried on by one person; **the Paris method, with an entire faculty teaching the subject, should be the example to follow**. India belongs with the Indologist, Semitic/Egyptian/Greek/Roman religions with source specialists, and `primitive` religion with the anthropologist. Graduate work should proceed through direct recourse to sources.

This gives us a contemporary disciplinary vocabulary for Lovejoy’s actual trajectory:

> **Harvard specialist/source training → Paris multi-specialist sciences-religieuses environment.**

The claim is not that Lovejoy read Jastrow’s manifestos or consciously enacted his programme. Jastrow functions as a contemporary field witness who makes visible the institutional relation between the two settings.

## 1. Jastrow 1893: the discipline is new because it requires impartial historical investigation

Primary source:

Morris Jastrow Jr., `Recent Movements in the Historical Study of Religions in America`, *The Biblical World* 1.1 (1893), 24–32. JSTOR stable `3135306`.

Jastrow opens by distinguishing religion as an ancient object of speculation from religion as a very recent **object of investigation**. What marks the new field is the acquisition of a historical frame of mind and the `impartial and broad investigation of facts`.

He identifies two major sources of the new discipline:

- research into the history/literatures of the ancient Orient;
- investigation of widely distributed institutions and customs connected with religion.

This is already striking against Lovejoy’s later notebook sequence. The two evidentiary domains Jastrow treats as jointly constitutive of the new science are approximately the two domains through which Lovejoy moves:

- 004: ancient textual/philological material;
- 005: comparative institutions, customs and ethnographic reports.

Do not convert this into a direct intellectual genealogy. Use it as a contemporary map of the field’s evidentiary architecture.

---

## 2. The French model is explicit in 1893

Jastrow’s European survey records:

- Dutch comparative-history chairs from 1876;
- a chair of History of Religions at the Collège de France from 1880;
- creation in 1886 of the EPHE `Section des Sciences Religieuses`, with a faculty of twelve;
- Paris’s special history-of-religions journal and Musée Guimet.

He then says American intellectual development still depends in some measure on European examples and that European activity is helping the historical study of religions receive more serious attention in the United States.

This is important because the France/U.S. comparison was already being made in exactly the decade before Lovejoy’s Harvard-to-Paris movement.

---

## 3. Harvard in Jastrow 1893: an explicitly distributed structure

Jastrow says Harvard was, as far as he knew, the first American institution to introduce the historical study of religions into its curriculum.

But what he describes is not a single department or chair. It is a **distributed bundle of specialist teaching**:

- Charles Carroll Everett: Comparative History of Religions;
- Crawford H. Toy: Hebrew religion compared with other Semitic religions; Islam/Koran;
- Charles Rockwell Lanman: **Sacred Books of Buddhism**;
- F. D. Allen: Greek religion and worship;
- Kittredge: Icelandic sagas / Edda / Germanic mythology;
- Emerton: Christianity and paganism in the first eight Christian centuries;
- Lyon: Babylonian and Assyrian religion in his courses.

This contemporary description gives a much firmer context for 004 than the generic phrase `Harvard Orientalism`. In the institutional year Jastrow describes, Buddhism at Harvard is specifically lodged in **Lanman’s Sanskrit/Oriental specialist domain**, alongside parallel specialist contributions elsewhere in the university.

Lovejoy’s documented later Sanskrit/Buddhist work with Lanman therefore belongs to a structure already legible in 1893 as a dispersed history-of-religions curriculum.

---

## 4. Cambridge History of Religions Club and American lecture network are not later reconstructions

Jastrow directly records two new American institutions.

### Cambridge History of Religions Club

During winter 1891–92 a History of Religions Club was formed at Cambridge, composed largely of Harvard professors and members of learned institutions in Boston. It met monthly for papers and discussion.

This confirms the early date `1891–92` and is a period-native control for the archival title beginning 1892.

It does **not** prove Lovejoy belonged to the Club after arriving at Harvard in 1895. Membership/attendance remains HOLD until direct evidence appears.

### American Committee for Lectures

Jastrow also describes the organization of an annual lecture scheme in History/Comparative Study of Religions, modeled partly on British lecture foundations. Crawford H. Toy became chairman and Jastrow secretary. Cooperating institutions included the University Lecture Association, Brooklyn Institute, Cornell, Peabody Institute and **Lowell Institute**.

This directly precedes the T. W. Rhys Davids lecture series whose published form becomes an exact source in notebook 004.

Again, no attendance claim for Lovejoy is warranted. What is secure is the institutional route by which specialist religious studies circulated publicly into Boston/Cambridge before and during Lovejoy’s Harvard years.

---

## 5. The decisive 1893 formulation: the French school solves the specialist-coordination problem

Jastrow ends the institutional survey by saying that the historical study of religions can be adequately taught only by a department formed, **as in the French school**, through the cooperation of as many specialists as possible.

He insists that general/comparative treatment gains value only in connection with careful, prolonged study of a particular religion and its sources; guidance in that source work belongs to the relevant specialist.

This is almost a period-native definition of the American institutional problem that the project has been reconstructing:

> the United States has specialists and distributed courses, but lacks the concentrated organizational form that joins them into a full discipline.

Paris is Jastrow’s explicit comparative solution.

## 6. Jastrow 1899: philology is the disciplinary parent, but not the whole discipline

Primary source:

Morris Jastrow Jr., `The Historical Study of Religions in Universities and Colleges`, *JAOS* 20 (1899), 317–325, JSTOR stable `592338`.

Jastrow says that proper methods for studying religions are very recent and calls the historical study of religions, in a certain sense, the **child of comparative philology** and foster child of archaeology.

His explanation matters. Comparative philology and newly opened ancient sources transformed the attitude toward religious phenomena and helped produce a new discipline.

This is an unusually useful contemporary bridge for our 004→005 model:

- philological/source criticism is not external to comparative religion;
- it is one of the historical conditions of the discipline;
- but a mature history of religions must then coordinate different source-specialist domains, including anthropology.

Therefore the Harvard-to-Paris shift need not be narrated as a move from one unrelated method to another. Jastrow himself describes the newer discipline as growing from source-specialist philology while exceeding any single philological field.

---

## 7. The strongest sentence for the U.S.–France architecture: Paris as model

Jastrow 1899 states that the study of religions cannot properly be carried on by a single individual because its numerous subdivisions require specialists. He then makes the institutional comparison explicit:

> the method pursued in Paris, where an entire faculty teaches the subject, should serve as the example to follow.

He immediately assigns domains:

- religions of India → Indologist;
- Semites / Egypt / Judea / Chaldea / Greece / Rome → relevant source specialists;
- religious phenomena of `primitive man` → anthropologist.

This is highly relevant to Lovejoy’s trajectory because he moved almost exactly across these specialist jurisdictions:

- Harvard Sanskrit/Buddhist textual work;
- Paris Orientalists (Lévi etc.);
- Marillier’s comparative/anthropological `religions des peuples non civilisés` conference.

The trajectory should not be described as evidence that Lovejoy followed Jastrow’s programme. It is evidence that Lovejoy’s biography crossed the very institutional divisions a contemporary American theorist of the discipline was trying to coordinate.

---

## 8. 1899 graduate-research programme: direct recourse to sources

Jastrow argues that universities should formally recognize the study of a particular religion as graduate/doctoral work conducted with the specialist responsible for its sources.

He says a student trained in Sanskrit, Semitic, Greek, Latin and related philological fields should be given opportunities to study the religions of the peoples whose languages/literatures are being interpreted. The university should establish an example of proper historical method.

His strongest methodological axiom is that **no particular religion can be studied except through direct recourse to the sources**; general/comparative perspectives broaden the horizon but do not substitute for specialist source work.

This sharply reinforces the current interpretation of 004. Lovejoy’s dense source handling — Sanskrit/Pāli categories through Warren, Rhys Davids, Hardy, Senart, Oldenberg and others — is not merely personal antiquarian reading. It is legible within a contemporary institutional ideal of religion research rooted in direct source control.

## 9. The 1893→1899 shift maps the exact years around Lovejoy

Chronologically the two Jastrow essays bracket Lovejoy’s formative period unusually well.

### 1893 — before Lovejoy enters Harvard

- Harvard already described as the first U.S. curricular center for the field;
- Lanman explicitly teaching Sacred Books of Buddhism;
- Cambridge History of Religions Club active;
- American lecture committee being organized;
- French EPHE model already held up as a fuller institutional form.

### 1895–98 — Lovejoy at Harvard

- Sanskrit/Buddhist study under Lanman and related Harvard coursework;
- notebook 004;
- exact uptake of Rhys Davids’s American Lectures publication;
- 1898 JAOS Buddhist article.

### 1898–99 — Lovejoy in Paris

- active auditor in Marillier’s EPHE conference work;
- notebook 005 / survivance / sacrifice research field.

### 1899 — Jastrow’s second intervention

- still complains that even Harvard lacks a dedicated chair;
- explicitly recommends the Paris multi-specialist faculty model;
- assigns `primitive` religion to the anthropologist and Indian religion to the Indologist;
- insists on direct source research.

This chronology turns the U.S.–France frame from a retrospective geographical comparison into a contemporary institutional problem.

## 10. Historiographical control

Donald Wiebe’s 2024 `The Road Not Taken in the Academic Study of Religion` uses Jastrow to reconstruct the incomplete American institutionalization of an academic/scientific study of religion and the significance of European models.

Use Wiebe as a modern institutional-history control, but the primary Jastrow texts now do most of the work needed for the Lovejoy paper because they speak from Lovejoy’s own disciplinary moment.

The project’s added value is Lovejoy-specific and documentary:

> Jastrow describes the available institutional regimes; Lovejoy’s two notebooks let us observe one researcher moving through them and transforming different evidence types inside them.

## 11. Revised transatlantic formulation

Batch 50’s earlier formulation can now be sharpened.

Earlier:

> American distributed infrastructure ↔ Paris concentrated workshop.

Period-grounded version:

> **Contemporary American advocates themselves described the emerging field as dispersed across philologists, Orientalists, philosophers, anthropologists, clubs and lecture networks, and explicitly held the Paris EPHE’s multi-specialist `Sciences Religieuses` section up as the organizational form that could join source expertise to comparison. Lovejoy’s 1895–99 movement from Lanman-era Harvard Buddhist philology to Marillier’s EPHE comparative-religion conference crossed exactly this institutional divide.**

This is stronger because the comparative geography is no longer solely ours.

## 12. Consequence for `operator migration`

The Jastrow evidence does not prove a personal methodological genealogy, but it supplies a disciplinary-scale mechanism for the domain shift already seen in 004→005.

004’s evidentiary object:

`term / canonical passage / philological source / semantic relation`.

005’s evidentiary object:

`traveller/missionary report / custom / rite / social determinant / causal mechanism`.

Jastrow’s institutional schema explicitly places these under different specialists within one emergent science:

`Indologist / source philologist` + `anthropologist` → coordinated history of religions.

Thus `term-in-text → rite-in-report` can now be situated not only in Lovejoy’s individual notebooks but in the late-1890s institutional differentiation of the history of religions itself.

That is likely a major journal-article payoff.

## Evidence cautions

1. No evidence is claimed that Lovejoy read either Jastrow essay.
2. Jastrow is a contemporary field witness, not a direct source for Lovejoy’s notebooks.
3. Do not infer Lovejoy membership in the Cambridge History of Religions Club from its existence.
4. Do not infer Lovejoy attendance at the American Lectures from Rhys Davids’s later book being an exact 004 source.
5. Jastrow’s evolutionary vocabulary and assumptions about `primitive` religion are historically situated and should not be silently normalized.
6. The Paris model Jastrow praises is multi-specialist institutional coordination; it does not mean Harvard lacked specialist research capacity.
7. `Harvard distributed / Paris concentrated` is now well grounded but remains an analytical compression, not Jastrow’s exact phrase.
