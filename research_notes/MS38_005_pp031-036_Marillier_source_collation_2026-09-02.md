## 1. p.31 — rank/status continuity is source-secure as a Marillier problem

Current canonical state: low-confidence inserted leaf, summarized as contrasting lower and higher forms of future life and earthly/social conditions with post-mortem existence.

Marillier's printed p.7 explicitly states that differential afterlife destinies are often governed not by good/bad conduct but by rank, age, sex, and occupation. Kings remain kings and slaves remain slaves; nobles and rich may alone enjoy a new existence while common people perish or occupy an inferior region.

A second source locus, printed p.16, makes the same continuity concrete: cultivator remains cultivator, fisherman remains fisherman, slave remains slave, chief remains chief; Dahomey/Kutomen examples preserve service and social hierarchy in the other world.

Source-level consequence:

`POST-MORTEM DIFFERENCE -> SOCIAL/RANK CONTINUITY, NOT NECESSARILY MORAL RECOMPENSE`.

This strongly supports the present p.31 argument summary, but the notebook's exact wording remains open.

## 2. pp.32–33 — provision, rites, and access must remain separate from moral desert

Current canonical state: p.32 distinguishes a struggle/condition of continued existence from later ideas in which food or gifts are supplied to the dead; p.33 distinguishes distributions/offerings to the dead from cult addressed to deceased persons and then turns toward age/status/rank.

Marillier repeatedly separates the conditions of access or continuance in the afterlife from moral recompense. Printed pp.28–31 give examples in which burial, tattooing, ritual observance, sacrifices, gifts, or funerary ceremonies determine entry, provision, or treatment in the world of the dead. Printed p.31 includes the Yoruba `poule ouvrant la voie`, an Angola rite explicitly described as opening the gates of happiness in the other world, and a Madagascar burial sanction.

Source-level consequence:

`RITUAL / PROVISIONAL CONDITION OF AFTERLIFE != MORAL REWARD OR PUNISHMENT`.

The exact source behind the present p.32 phrase about a `struggle for survival` and the precise p.33 distinction between offerings and cult remain to be fixed from the manuscript image. No diplomatic upgrade is made here.

## 3. p.34 — `[Tonga?] / Polynesia` can be source-resolved to the Bolotoo sequence

Current canonical state: medium-low confidence; `[Tonga?] Islands / Polynesia`; several classes/ranks; question whether common people pass into a different post-mortem status; chiefs/nobles versus ordinary people.

Marillier printed p.9 provides a close and unusually specific match. In the Tonga/Bolotoo material, the dead are classified according to the rank they held in this world rather than moral conduct. The printed source names Mataboles, Mooas, and Tooas; for the Tooas, the common people, the source says either that they have no soul or that it perishes with the body. The same printed sequence then moves to Tahiti, where the Areoi and sufficiently wealthy chiefs can enter a privileged paradise while the people cannot, and to the Marquesas, where aristocratic chiefs and other privileged classes occupy a different post-mortem condition from common people.

Source-level identification:

- geographical field: Tonga / Bolotoo, followed by Tahiti and Marquesas;
- operative variable: social rank/status;
- limiting case: annihilation or absence of surviving soul for common people in the Tongan example;
- moral firewall: rewards of virtue and punishments of crime are described there as terrestrial rather than as the basis of the post-mortem classification.

This is strong enough to withdraw the vague source-level label `[Tonga?]` in future collation work, but the manuscript hand still needs direct verification before the canonical diplomatic text is changed.

## 5. p.36 — current summary probably conflates two source packets

Current canonical state: medium-low confidence; a `great tree` / underworld image, a question of annihilation versus continued existence, followed by `Callaway, Relig. [of] Amazulu`.

Source collation separates at least two relevant printed loci.

### A. Futuna / material form of the abode of the dead

Marillier printed p.8 distinguishes warriors who go to Langi/Pulotu from the other dead, who go to a `fale-mate`; each family or clan has its own, described as `un arbre creux, un rocher, etc.` This is a much better source-level match for the present `tree / region` part of the p.36 summary than a generic `great tree` reconstruction.

### B. Callaway / AmaZulu continuation model

Marillier printed p.4 cites Zulu traditions in which the dead live under the earth and continue the occupations of life; other souls live in the sky, without a just/wicked moral partition. The accompanying footnote identifies Canon Callaway, `The Religious System of the Amazulus`, pp.8, 140, 165; another nearby note cites Callaway, `Zulu Nursery Tales`, p.316.

Therefore the abbreviation currently summarized as `Callaway, Relig. [of] Amazulu` can be source-collated with high confidence to:

`Callaway, The Religious System of the Amazulus, pp. 8, 140, 165`

but the exact abbreviated form written by Lovejoy remains a manuscript-image question.

### C. Annihilation sequence

Marillier printed p.9, immediately around the Tonga/Tahiti/Marquesas packet, also gives a New Zealand Po divided into successive sections in which the soul loses vitality until it becomes a worm and dies. Elsewhere the article repeatedly contrasts continued afterlife with annihilation produced by rank, hazard, or ritual failure.

The present p.36 summary may therefore be flattening adjacent notes from distinct examples: material abode (`fale-mate`, hollow tree/rock), continuation of earthly occupation (AmaZulu), and annihilation/degradation sequences. The direct image must separate them rather than choosing one synthetic paraphrase.
