# Batch 138 — June 1898 EPHE Indianist pedagogy described from inside: philology and religion were different turns of the same textual practice

Date: 2026-08-19  
Status: synced  
Scope: use a contemporary self-description of EPHE Sanskrit/Indian-religion teaching published by Louis Finot and Alfred Foucher in June 1898, immediately before Lovejoy's Paris year, and compare it with Sylvain Lévi's earlier archival statement of his teaching method. The question is not whether Lovejoy attended every Indianist conference, but what pedagogical architecture surrounded a Sanskrit-trained student entering the EPHE religious-sciences environment in 1898–99.

## Core result

A June 1898 article by **Louis Finot and Alfred Foucher**, both pupils of Sylvain Lévi and active teachers inside the same EPHE Indianist network, gives the clearest contemporary description yet recovered of how the two relevant sections were supposed to relate.

They state that Sanskrit was taught in both the **Section des sciences historiques et philologiques** and the **Section des sciences religieuses**. Only the philological conference taught Sanskrit directly `pour lui-même`, but the study of Indian religions could not dispense with knowledge of the sacred language. More importantly, they insist that Indian works could appear indifferently in the programmes of both conferences, because the difference lay **less in the choice of texts than in the orientation of the commentary** — `ici philologique, là religieuse`.

Their own methodological conclusion is explicit: it is more faithful to reality to bring the two orders of study together and **not separate in theory what is so close in practice**.

This is unusually important for the Lovejoy project. Notebook 004 and the 1898 JAOS article preserve a philological/Buddhist textual apparatus; notebook 005 and Lovejoy's documented Marillier participation move inside comparative/history-of-religions problems. The 1898 EPHE insiders themselves describe philological and religious work not as sealed domains but as neighboring modes of commentary on overlapping textual material.

This does not prove institutional causation or that Lovejoy read Finot/Foucher's article. It establishes a period teaching architecture in which the 004→005 shift is historically legible without inventing a clean disciplinary conversion.

## 2. Philological and religious teaching shared a textual field

The article opens by stating that Sanskrit is taught in both the historical-philological and religious-sciences sections. The philological conference alone is directly devoted to Sanskrit as a language in itself, but the religious study of India cannot dispense with the language in which so much religious literature is written.

They then make the institutional overlap explicit. Very few Indian works, they say, are incapable of appearing in the programmes of either conference. The actual distinction is:

`the orientation of commentary`

rather than

`a separate corpus of texts`.

Their wording distinguishes:

- `ici philologique`;
- `là religieuse`.

Primary source: p.488, Persée.

This gives us a period model of **domain translation by commentary**. The same or closely related textual object can support a philological reading in one institutional setting and a history-of-religion reading in another.

That is a closer historical analogue to the Lovejoy archive than a generic statement that Orientalism was interdisciplinary.

---

## 3. The strongest sentence: do not separate in theory what is close in practice

Finot and Foucher explicitly say that it seemed more faithful to the reality of the facts to unite the two kinds of study in the same account and:

`de ne pas séparer en théorie ce qui est si voisin dans la pratique`.

Primary source: p.488, Persée.

For this project the sentence is valuable because it blocks a misleading historiographical narrative:

`Harvard / 004 = philology`

then

`Paris / 005 = anthropology or comparative religion`

as if one discipline simply replaced another.

The contemporary teaching system itself was designed so that philological and religious modes could operate on adjacent or identical source material.

The safe paper-level implication is therefore:

> **Paris offered Lovejoy not simply a new body of comparative-religion material but an institutional setting in which philological and religious commentary were explicitly treated as neighboring practices.**

This is contextual reconstruction, not a claim that Finot/Foucher formulated Lovejoy's method for him.

---

## 4. The teaching system was explicitly stratified by preparation

Finot and Foucher then look back to the first EPHE Sanskrit programme of 1869 and state that its organization had remained intact for nearly thirty years. It comprised:

- practical conferences for **beginners**;
- explanations of texts for **veterans**.

Primary source: p.488, Persée.

This is a significant control on the Lovejoy–Lévi problem. The institution did not conceive every student as entering at the same level through one standardized course. It maintained different modes for beginners and already prepared students.

Lovejoy arrived with independently documented Harvard Sanskrit/Buddhist-text training and with notebook 004 / the 1898 JAOS Buddhist article already in his immediate intellectual background. That does not prove he was formally classified as a Sanskrit `vétéran`; it means that an advanced textual mode existed for exactly the kind of student who arrived with prior preparation.

Stickney's later experience supplies a concrete control: he could work intensively on the Bhagavad-Gītā and later continue text reading with Lévi outside the two regular conferences.

## 5. The p.490 threshold: the Religions de l'Inde conference ideally required Sanskrit competence

Roland Lardinois quotes Finot and Foucher's p.490 formulation exactly:

`On écrirait volontiers sur la porte de la conférence des religions de l'Inde : "Que nul n'entre ici s'il n'est sanscritiste".`

Scholarly source preserving exact original pagination:

https://books.openedition.org/editionscnrs/60557

This is presently controlled through an exact scholarly quotation of p.490 rather than a directly extracted primary page in our runtime. The original article is digitally available, so publication-grade facsimile collation remains desirable.

The proposition itself is important. Finot and Foucher were not merely saying Sanskrit was useful. They cast Sanskrit competence as the **ideal entry condition** for the Indian-religions conference.

Lardinois immediately adds a sociological caution: this was more prescription than universal reality; not every participant possessed Sanskrit. That distinction should be retained.

For Lovejoy, the point is not that Sanskrit was literally required administratively. It is that his Harvard training gave him precisely the kind of linguistic capital the Indian-religions teachers themselves presented as methodologically desirable.

## 6. Lévi's 1889 teaching statement supplies the architecture behind the 1898 article

Lardinois also reproduces a letter written by Sylvain Lévi to the director of higher education on **13 August 1889**, when Lévi was organizing his teaching across the Sorbonne and EPHE.

In the quoted archival letter Lévi says he leaves to the École des Hautes Études:

`l'enseignement supérieur de l'indianisme, réservé aux auditeurs déjà préparés`.

There he treats a special question in detail:

`avec le concours actif des élèves`.

For the Section des sciences religieuses, Lévi says he takes a **religious text** as his base and, through an explanation that is:

`précise, serrée, rigoureuse`,

tries to make the essential and general features of the religion emerge.

Scholarly transcription/quotation:

https://books.openedition.org/editionscnrs/60557

This is an archival letter known here through Lardinois's transcription, not through a directly inspected manuscript image. The repo should cite it accordingly until the underlying archival object is acquired.

Methodologically, however, it is strikingly clear:

`exact textual explanation`

→ `religious/general characterization`.

This is not philology abandoned for comparative religion. It is philological exactitude being used as the evidentiary base for religious interpretation.

## 7. Why this matters specifically for Lovejoy rather than only for French Sanskrit studies

Three separate Lovejoy facts already established in the repo make the institutional model relevant:

1. Harvard 1896–97 gave Lovejoy formal Sanskrit and Buddhist-text study under Charles R. Lanman.
2. Notebook 004 and the 1898 JAOS article show active Buddhist/Sāṃkhya philological work before/during the Paris transition.
3. EPHE's 1898–99 report directly names Lovejoy as an auditor who took an active part in Marillier's V-Section work.

What remains **unproved** is Lovejoy's formal attendance in Lévi's IV-Section Sanskrit conference or Foucher's Indian-religions conference.

Therefore the proper inference is capability plus environment:

> **Lovejoy entered the Paris religious-sciences environment with precisely the kind of textual preparation that the EPHE Indianists themselves regarded as the ideal basis for advanced religious study.**

Do not rewrite this as `Lovejoy enrolled as a veteran Sanskrit student` without a registration/attendance record.

## 8. A new interpretation of the private-reading episode

Batches 135–137 established:

- Pāli/Buddhist translation belonged to Lévi–Finot's recent pre-mission pedagogical repertoire;
- the 1897–98 mission interrupted Lévi's teaching;
- the recovered 1898–99 formal programme did not advertise Pāli;
- Lévi demonstrably conducted supplementary text work with advanced students outside regular conferences;
- his private residence at 9 rue Guy-de-la-Brosse is independently fixed in Lovejoy's exact year;
- Wilson says Lovejoy responded to the unavailable Pāli course by occasionally reading sacred texts with Lévi at home.

The 1898 Finot/Foucher article adds another layer: **the system was already designed around differences in preparation and around close text explanation rather than a rigid single-course curriculum.**

Thus Wilson's reported episode is increasingly intelligible as a normal adaptation of an advanced textual pedagogy to a student whose desired regular course was unavailable.

That remains an interpretation until the Lovejoy letters are read. The exact sacred texts and languages are still unknown.

## 9. Historiographical consequence for the 004→005 argument

The strongest current model is no longer:

`004 philology → Paris anthropology → 005 comparative religion`.

Nor should it be:

`Lovejoy invented one stable method and merely applied it to a new topic`.

The archive and period institutional record instead support:

`prepublication Buddhist philological apparatus`

+

`Paris institution where philological and religious commentary overlap on shared textual objects`

+

`Marillier comparative-religion seminar with calibrated burdens of proof`

→ `005 transfers level-separating proof practices into a different evidence genre`.

The June 1898 Finot/Foucher article is especially valuable because it supplies period language for the **institutional permeability** of these operations exactly at the moment of transition.

A concise paper formulation is:

> **The Paris transition did not carry Lovejoy from texts to religion as from one discipline into another. EPHE Indianists themselves described philological and religious commentary as two orientations toward closely overlapping materials, and they reserved advanced work for already prepared auditors. Lovejoy arrived with Sanskrit and Buddhist philology already in hand and entered a religious-sciences environment in which that preparation remained methodologically legible.**
