## 0. Anti-duplication check

Repository search for `The Treatment of "Opposition" in Formal Logic`, `A Note from Professor A. O. Lovejoy`, `slip in writing`, `erratum`, and self-correction language found bibliographic metadata and the existing Kant Batch217–220 materials, but no dedicated analysis of this 1910 correction as a revision-operation control.

This batch therefore asks a narrow question:

> Does Lovejoy's other explicit 1910 published correction provide a second instance of the intellectual/category revision seen in the 1909→1910 Kant sequence?

## 1. Source pair

### Original paper

Arthur O. Lovejoy, `The Treatment of "Opposition" in Formal Logic`, *The Journal of Philosophy, Psychology and Scientific Methods* 7 (1910), 101–105.

The paper attacks the conventional textbook treatment of logical opposition as insufficiently clear/systematic and proposes a more explicit rule-based/diagrammatic treatment.

### Published correction note

Arthur O. Lovejoy, `A Note from Professor A. O. Lovejoy`, *The Journal of Philosophy, Psychology and Scientific Methods* 7, no. 5 (3 March 1910), 133.

Bibliographic controls:

- JSTOR issue record: https://www.jstor.org/stable/i308927
- Google Play free scan of volume 7: https://play.google.com/store/books/details/The_Journal_of_Philosophy?id=8xhRAAAAYAAJ
- Searchable full-volume OCR mirror used to recover the note text: https://www.scribd.com/document/94192795/journalofphiloso07lancuoft-bw

The note is dated Columbia, Missouri, 19 February 1910. Lovejoy explicitly characterizes the problem as an `erratum`, a `slip in writing`, and something that escaped him in `too hasty proof-reading`; he takes responsibility for the oversight. He asks that a phrase at the beginning of Rule IV on p.104 be deleted and restates the rule beginning with the condition that two propositions together exhaust all five possible cases.

## 3. Verdict

> **AUTHORIAL-ERRATUM / LOCAL-FORMAL-RULE / NEGATIVE CONTROL.**

This is a genuine explicit self-correction, but it is not a second predicate-fission event. Lovejoy himself presents it as a local compositional/proofreading error, not as a judgment revised after evidential or historical reconsideration.

That distinction improves Batch220. Explicit corrections should not be pooled together merely because the author says he was wrong. At least two correction regimes are now directly visible in 1910:

1. **surface/propositional repair** — local wording is repaired while the analytic object and classification remain fixed;
2. **historical predicate revision** — closer investigation forces a broad actor-predicate to be decomposed and its scope redistributed.

The Kant case therefore remains, within the currently controlled ≤1910 corpus, the strongest direct published evidence for an **intellectual self-reclassification** rather than a mere erratum.

## 4. Consequence for the longitudinal model

Batch220's `resolution increase in historical classification` should retain a discriminant threshold:

> **acknowledged error is insufficient; the relevant event must alter the evidentially licensed unit, relation, scope, or predicate of historical judgment.**

This negative control prevents a category mistake in our own analysis. Mechanical errata, local notation repair, and intellectual reclassification are different revision operations.

## 5. Evidence limit

The currently recovered searchable-volume text establishes Lovejoy's own characterization of the note and the nature of the requested deletion. The exact deleted opening phrase of Rule IV has not yet been isolated from a page facsimile in this batch.

That missing phrase is **not required** for the present classification because Lovejoy explicitly identifies the event as a writing/proofreading slip and prescribes a local deletion. If later publication-grade reconstruction of the formal rule matters, p.104 + p.133 can be pulled from the free volume/JSTOR page image. For the current Lovejoy-method thesis, a human download would add little, so no new MD item is opened.
