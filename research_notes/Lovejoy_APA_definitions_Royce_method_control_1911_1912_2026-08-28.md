# Lovejoy / APA Committee on Definitions / Royce — method-control dossier, 1911–1912

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: **DIRECT PRIMARY COMMITTEE REPORT / DIRECT CONTEMPORARY ROYCE RESPONSE IDENTIFIED / METHOD-POLITICS CONTROL**

## Core result

The 1911 `Report of the Committee on Definitions of the American Philosophical Association`, co-signed by Arthur O. Lovejoy, is a stronger bridge between his formal-logical work and his later history-of-ideas method than generic claims about an `analytic temperament`.

The committee explicitly attempts to organize cooperative philosophical inquiry by:

1. fixing operational definitions for the discussion;
2. partitioning the object into logically distinguishable classes;
3. separating connected but discriminable questions;
4. enumerating abstractly possible positions `after the manner of formal logic`;
5. restricting the live debate to a selected subset of the logically possible positions;
6. compiling heterogeneous theories under concise formulas while warning that the formulas are not always mutually exclusive and may need combination.

This is not historical writing, but it is direct actor-level evidence for a methodological repertoire of **definition → relation partition → exhaustive possibility space → question restriction → controlled comparison/recombination**.

Josiah Royce's 1912 `On Definitions and Debates` then attacks this very attempt to structure philosophical discussion. The dispute therefore makes the politics and epistemology of analytical isolation visible before Lovejoy's mature history-of-ideas programme.

## I. Direct primary: Committee on Definitions report (1911)

Primary:

F. J. E. Woodbridge, Frank Thilly, Dickinson S. Miller, Arthur O. Lovejoy, W. P. Montague, E. G. Spaulding, `Report of the Committee on Definitions of the American Philosophical Association`, *The Journal of Philosophy, Psychology and Scientific Methods* 8.26 (21 Dec. 1911), 701–708.

Public-domain full text:
- `https://archive.org/stream/jstor-2013305/2013305_djvu.txt`
- Internet Archive item: `https://archive.org/details/jstor-2013305`

### A. The committee explicitly organizes cooperative inquiry

The report says it does more than define words. It attempts to **formulate the issues precisely** and indicate what appears to be the most promising approach. It calls the exercise an unusually extensive attempt to organize cooperative philosophical inquiry and asks participants, provisionally, to conform to its general plan of procedure.

This should be read as a method for making philosophical disagreement tractable, not merely terminological housekeeping.

### B. Classification is explicitly logical

The report first distinguishes objects along two axes:

- real / unreal;
- perceived / unperceived.

It then states that there are **four logically distinguishable classes** generated by those axes.

This is a direct relation/grid operation rather than a narrative classification.

### C. Questions are connected but discriminable

The report defines two questions for discussion and calls them **connected but discriminable**:

1. numerical identity/difference among the classes of real and perceived objects;
2. the positive nature of the difference between an object's perceived and unperceived status, i.e. the nature of consciousness in a perceptual situation and its logical relation to the first question.

The method therefore explicitly prevents one connected philosophical question from automatically settling another.

### D. Five possible positions are enumerated `after the manner of formal logic`

The strongest passage occurs in `IV. Analysis of the First Question`.

The report states that, with respect to the first problem, **five views seem at least abstractly possible** and that these can be presented **`after the manner of formal logic`**.

The five cases specify distinct inclusion/identity patterns between perceived and real objects. The report then says that the main controversy concerns these five logically possible positions, but deliberately restricts the meeting's discussion to the last three because recent controversy has centered on those cases.

This gives a complete analytical sequence:

`define terms`
→ `construct logical possibility space`
→ `enumerate positions`
→ `identify live controversy`
→ `restrict discussion without confusing restriction with logical exhaustiveness`.

This is extremely close in structure to Lovejoy's 1910 opposition paper, where possible relations among two terms are enumerated and the inferential content of each proposition is restricted to the cases it licenses.

### E. Recombination is allowed after discrimination

In `V. Analysis of the Second Question`, the committee compiles thirteen recent formulations of consciousness. It explicitly warns that:

- the formulations are not always reciprocally exclusive;
- some may need to be **combined** for a complete characterization;
- the formulas are analytical redescription and in some cases were not ratified by the philosophers to whom they are ascribed.

Thus analytical separation does not entail permanent atomization. The committee distinguishes first, then permits combination where the relations warrant it.

This is a strong philosophical analogue to Lovejoy's 1909 `new compound in intellectual chemistry` procedure.

## II. Authorship and attribution firewall

The report is a committee product signed by:

- F. J. E. Woodbridge;
- Frank Thilly;
- Dickinson S. Miller;
- Arthur O. Lovejoy;
- W. P. Montague;
- E. G. Spaulding.

Therefore:

**Secure:** Lovejoy formally participated in and endorsed the report as a committee member.

**Not secure from signature alone:** that every classification, phrase, definition, or formal-logical device was composed personally by Lovejoy.

Do not quote an unsigned sentence from the report as `Lovejoy says` unless separate authorship evidence is recovered.

The value here is methodological participation, not sentence-level sole authorship.

## III. Royce 1912: the method becomes an explicit object of dispute

Josiah Royce, `On Definitions and Debates`, *Journal of Philosophy, Psychology and Scientific Methods* 9 (1912), 85–100, responds directly to the Committee on Definitions experiment and the 1911 Harvard APA meeting.

Controls:
- JSTOR issue record: `https://www.jstor.org/stable/i334653`
- Royce article/manuscript is preserved in Harvard's Josiah Royce papers, Box 48.
- CSU Bakersfield Josiah Royce Edition lists the article and provides a digitized copy.

Daniel J. Wilson's annotated bibliography summarizes the response as questioning whether the effort, **led by Lovejoy**, to structure APA debate was worth the cost. Royce argues that the framework can exclude significant philosophical positions from participation even while he supports cooperation in principle.

This is especially significant because Royce was one of Lovejoy's principal Harvard teachers.

The dispute is therefore not simply:

`old idealist teacher vs new realist student`.

It is also:

`how far may philosophical inquiry pre-structure its objects, definitions and admissible positions before the structure itself becomes philosophically prejudicial?`

That is a methodological question with direct relevance to later criticisms of Lovejoyian `unit-ideas`.

## IV. Historiographical consequence

The mature Lovejoy problem can now be reframed more historically.

A standard critique treats the `unit-idea` as though Lovejoy simply assumed that reality comes pre-divided into stable conceptual atoms.

The 1907–1912 evidence instead shows a philosopher repeatedly practicing and publicly institutionalizing **analytical isolation**:

- 1907: apply formal-logical scrutiny to Kant's forms of judgment;
- 1908: decompose `pragmatism` into logically independent contentions;
- 1909: combine compatible motives only after analysis;
- 1910: enumerate proposition-relations in formal logic;
- 1911: separate formal implication from existence/historical proof;
- 1911 APA report: define, partition, enumerate possible positions, restrict a problem-space, then permit controlled combinations;
- 1912: this procedure itself becomes the object of Royce's criticism.

The strongest new hypothesis is:

> **A Lovejoyian `unit` may have begun less as a metaphysical claim that ideas are naturally atomic than as a methodological demand that an investigator construct discriminable units before comparison, inference, disagreement and recombination can be controlled.**

This does not vindicate the mature method. It creates the historically sharper criticism:

> **what licenses an analyst-constructed unit, initially justified as a device for tractable inquiry, to become a trans-contextually identifiable historical object?**

This is exactly where methodological isolation can harden into historiographical reification.

## V. Relation to 004 and the early archive

The 1898 notebook 004 already distinguishes multiple relation types — temporal, logical, semantic, causal, genealogical — and preserves temporal antecedence and logical subdivision without collapsing them.

The 1911 report provides a later explicit philosophical analogue:

- multiple axes generate different classes;
- connected questions remain discriminable;
- logical possibility does not equal empirical/historical actuality;
- formal exhaustiveness and practical restriction are separate operations;
- analytical formulations may later be recombined.

Current evidence does **not** prove that formal logic generated 004.

The defensible longitudinal proposition is narrower:

> **relation partition appears in Lovejoy's early historical notebooks before it becomes overtly formal-logical and institutionally programmatic in his published philosophy.**

That makes the origin of the operation an open historical problem rather than a settled derivation.

## Working formulation

> **By 1911 Lovejoy was not merely using analysis privately. He participated in an attempt to redesign philosophical discussion around definitions, discriminable questions and formally enumerable positions; by 1912 his former teacher Royce was already asking what such pre-structuring excludes. The history of Lovejoy's method therefore contains its own contemporary critique before the later contextualist critique existed.**
