## Why this matters

The Verhaegh full-text ingest recovered a direct institutional contact that is more substantial than a ceremonial introduction.

At the Eastern Division meeting of the American Philosophical Association in Baltimore in late December 1935, Rudolf Carnap delivered `Testability and Meaning`, his first conference address on U.S. soil. Verhaegh states that Arthur O. Lovejoy introduced Carnap. W. V. Quine's later recollection establishes that Lovejoy also made a substantive critical intervention to which Carnap replied.

This event therefore joins three histories that later historiography often separates:

- Lovejoy's critical-realist / formal-logical philosophical career;
- the arrival of logical positivism / scientific empiricism in the United States;
- the later retrospective story of the American `analytic turn`.

The event must be read as a boundary encounter, not as evidence that Lovejoy became a logical positivist or belonged to a later analytic school.

## 1. Verhaegh reconstruction

Sander Verhaegh, *Logical Positivism: An American History* (2026), pp. 214–215, reconstructs Carnap's December 1935 arrival in the United States.

On 28 December, Carnap, Quine, Goodman, Leonard, and David Prall traveled to Baltimore for the APA Eastern Division meeting. Carnap's `Testability and Meaning` was programmed on the final day. Verhaegh states that **Arthur Lovejoy introduced Carnap**, after which Carnap read a shortened version of the paper he had prepared partly as a reply to C. I. Lewis and partly to correct the American interpretation of logical positivism.

Verhaegh treats the Baltimore address as the start of Carnap's U.S. lecture tour and as part of his attempt to find common ground between the Vienna Circle and American pragmatism / empiricism.

### Current date hygiene

Secondary literature is inconsistent about the exact calendar date of the presentation. Verhaegh's 2026 book and his earlier article place the trip/presentation sequence on or beginning **28 December 1935**; some later secondary discussions say **31 December 1935** or loosely call the event a 1936 Baltimore congress.

Until the original APA programme/proceedings is recovered, use:

> **Baltimore APA Eastern Division meeting, late December 1935**

and preserve `28 Dec. 1935` as Verhaegh's specific reconstruction rather than silently normalizing all sources.

## 2. Quine's actor-level recollection — substantive exchange, not ceremonial contact

Quine later recalled the Baltimore episode in `Homage to Carnap`, collected in *The Ways of Paradox and Other Essays*, revised and enlarged edition (Harvard University Press, 1976), p. 42.

His recollection is explicit that he and other younger allies accompanied Carnap into what he retrospectively called the `metaphysicians' camp`. He remembers Carnap replying to **a diatribe by Arthur Lovejoy** by separating alternative interpretations of what Lovejoy might mean and deriving different consequences from each.

The exact Quine passage is widely reprinted in later historiography. The important evidentiary content is:

- Lovejoy did more than introduce Carnap;
- Lovejoy made a public critical intervention;
- Carnap replied analytically by disambiguating Lovejoy's claim into alternative readings;
- Quine remembered the scene decades later as emblematic of Carnap's philosophical method.

Coding:

- `direct interlocutor` — secure at the level of public conference exchange;
- `boundary critic` — secure;
- `infrastructural contact` — secure;
- `doctrinal influence` — unproved;
- `school membership` — not warranted.

## 3. Historiographical afterlife — the event becomes an origin myth of American analytic philosophy

The Baltimore exchange was later turned into a small origin scene for `analytic philosophy in America`.

Hans-Johann Glock, *What Is Analytic Philosophy?* (Cambridge University Press, 2008), uses Quine's recollection as a front-matter epigraph. Other secondary works repeat an anecdote, attributed to Quine or later oral transmission, that the beginning of analytic philosophy in the United States could be dated to the Baltimore encounter in which Carnap answered Lovejoy by first asking what Lovejoy meant.

This afterlife is highly useful for the Lovejoy repo because it produces a clear **historiographical sorting event**:

- the same scene can cast Carnap/Quine as the new analytic protagonists;
- Lovejoy becomes the `metaphysical` opponent against whom the new style defines itself;
- yet the repo's direct-primary sequence already shows that Lovejoy himself had practiced formal logic, logical analysis, proposition-sensitive distinction, and jurisdiction partition for decades.

Therefore the event is not evidence for a simple replacement narrative:

`old metaphysics`
→
`new analysis`.

A better historical model is:

> **competing analytical cultures and competing views of what analysis, meaning, evidence, metaphysics, and philosophical method should be permitted to do.**

## 4. Connection to the 1930 operationism / meaning dispute

Verhaegh's own book makes the Baltimore encounter less surprising by placing Lovejoy five years earlier inside debates on relativity, operationism, and meaning.

In 1930 Lovejoy argued against a Bridgman-style reduction of meaning to verifying operation. Verhaegh treats this as part of the American scientific-philosophy problem-space that logical positivism entered around 1930.

The chronological sequence is therefore:

`1930 Lovejoy on relativity / empirical criterion / meaning`
→
`1931 McGilvary reply`
→
`1930s American meaning-verification controversy`
→
`1935 Carnap arrives with Testability and Meaning`
→
`Lovejoy introduces and challenges Carnap at Baltimore`
→
`Carnap replies by disambiguating Lovejoy's formulation`.

This is a much stronger boundary history than a generic claim that Lovejoy happened to be present when logical positivism arrived.

## 5. Connection to the active `jurisdiction partition` hypothesis

The Baltimore scene should be compared, cautiously, with the Lovejoy sequence already established in the repository:

- 1908: logically independent theses under one movement-name must be assessed separately;
- 1909: historical complexes require logical analysis plus historical comparison;
- 1911: formal implication, meaning/intelligibility, evidence/verification, and historical truth have distinct jurisdictions;
- 1914: a purported deductive system can fail standards of logical adequacy;
- 1930: an experimental criterion or operation need not be identical with conceptual meaning;
- 1935: Carnap answers Lovejoy by splitting possible meanings and assigning consequences conditionally.

The striking feature is that both sides value discrimination. The dispute may concern **where the discriminations terminate and what they license**, not analysis versus non-analysis.

This suggests a new question:

> **Was the Lovejoy–Carnap boundary partly a dispute between two forms of jurisdiction partition — Lovejoy's plural evidentiary/semantic/logical jurisdictions versus Carnap's syntax/meaning/verification architecture?**

This is a research question, not yet a conclusion.
