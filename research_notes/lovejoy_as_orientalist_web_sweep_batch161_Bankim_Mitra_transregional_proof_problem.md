## Core result

Both proposed nodes survive verification and sharpen the article's first-order problem.

- **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay (1871)** explicitly separates the existence of similarity from the further inference of historical direction: resemblance between Sāṃkhya and Buddhism does not by itself decide whether Buddhism borrowed from Kapila or Kapila's philosophy depended on Buddhism.
- **Rajendralal Mitra (1883)** explicitly separates pre-existing philosophical materials from the formation of a complete system, and separately rejects genealogical conjecture resting on phonetic resemblance without adequate proof.

The two together establish that the evidentiary questions later visible in Garbe/Jacobi/Oldenberg/Lovejoy were not confined to a European Indological conversation. South Asian scholars were already formulating closely related distinctions in print.

The safe claim is therefore **transregional field participation**, not a direct Bankim/Mitra → Lovejoy line.

## 1. Bankim Chandra, `Buddhism and the Sankhya Philosophy` (1871)

### Bibliography and attribution

The essay was first published anonymously in the _Calcutta Review_, vol. 53, no. 106 (1871), pp. 191–203. A modern transcription identifies it as Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay's essay and derives from the English works collected in _Bankim Rachanavali_ (1960). A scan of _Calcutta Review_, vol. 53, issues 105–106, is independently preserved on Wikimedia Commons / Internet Archive.

Because the readily searchable transcription is not itself proofread against the original scan, publication-grade quotation should eventually be checked against the 1871 page images. The argument and bibliographic location, however, are sufficiently secure for research use.

### Bankim enters through Max Müller's objection

The article opens by stating that Barthélemy St. Hilaire and others had treated Buddhist metaphysics as borrowed from earlier Brahmanical philosophy, especially Sāṃkhya. It then identifies Max Müller as a major authority who doubted that the needed relation had been established, while acknowledging that a demonstrated relation would be invaluable for the chronology of Indian philosophical literature.

Thus Bankim is participating in exactly the same proof problem later formalized by Garbe: what historical conclusion can be drawn from resemblance between philosophical doctrines?

### `Similarity is not identity`

Near the conclusion Bankim answers Müller's objection that Sāṃkhya emancipation and Buddhist Nirvāṇa diverge. His explicit rule is:

> `Similarity is not identity, and is often compatible with very wide divergence.`

This matters because Bankim treats divergence as compatible with derivation. Historical dependence does not require identity of doctrine.

### Similarity and direction are separate questions

The article then ends by saying that, having argued for similarity, it has **not yet** answered the next question: whether that similarity warrants the inference that Buddhism borrowed its philosophy from Kapila, or instead that Kapila based his philosophy on Buddhism.

That gives a clean two-step proof architecture:

`establish similarity` → `separately establish direction of dependence`.

This is stronger than using `Similarity is not identity` as an isolated aphorism. The article itself structurally separates those two tasks.

### Another useful internal control

A footnote on the relation between the Sāṃkhya Aphorisms and the Kārikās asks why textual similarity should automatically be used in one direction of dependence rather than the reverse. This reinforces the same evidentiary discipline: resemblance does not carry its own arrow of transmission.

## 2. Rajendralal Mitra, _The Yoga Aphorisms of Patañjali_ (1883)

### Bibliographic control

Rajendralal Mitra's _The Yoga Aphorisms of Patañjali: with the Commentary of Bhoja Rájá, and an English Translation_ was issued through the Asiatic Society of Bengal / Bibliotheca Indica; the complete 1883 publication is bibliographically secure (Calcutta; Bibliotheca Indica new series nos. 462, 478, 482, 491–492; prefatory material + Sanskrit/English text).

The searchable prefatory text is available online and is sourced to the public-domain edition; Google Books independently preserves an 1881/1883 state of the work.

### Doctrines/materials are not yet a system

Mitra refuses an instantaneous-origin model for Sāṃkhya. He argues that many ontological and biological questions must have been debated before Kapila and that earlier doctrines/dogmas supplied the materials from which the system was made.

He then states the distinction with unusual clarity:

> `The bricks existed, and Kapila was the architect who used them in constructing a novel edifice.`

The preceding sentence makes the analytical point explicit: pre-existing phases of philosophical thought were current, and Kapila's role was to put them together `to create a complete system`.

This supplies a first-order South Asian formulation of:

`earlier doctrine/material ≠ already existing complete system`.

That is strikingly close in logical form to 004's later objection that a passage may not be good evidence that the Sāṃkhya `system existed as a whole`.

No direct influence should be inferred. The significance is that the distinction was already part of the wider nineteenth-century discussion of how Indian philosophical systems formed.

### Phonetic resemblance is not genealogy

Later in the preface Mitra attacks Albrecht Weber's attempt to connect the Vedic Kāpya Patañcala with Kapila and Patañjali. Mitra reconstructs Weber's varying formulations and says the argument appears to rest entirely on phonetic resemblance: Kāpya shares letters with Kapila and Patañcala sounds like Patañjali.

He mocks this reasoning through Shakespeare's Fluellen/Macedon–Monmouth analogy and concludes that, although he cannot prove there was no family/racial connection, `we have at present no proof worth the name in support of the conjecture.`

The important structure is again two-stage:

`phonetic similarity` → possible conjecture, but **not** demonstrated historical relation.

And Mitra distinguishes absence of disproof from positive proof: he has nothing showing there was no connection, but this does not make the conjecture evidentially established.

## 3. Bankim + Mitra change the geography of the proof problem

These texts make it unsafe to narrate the emergence of this evidentiary discipline as a method generated within European comparative philology and then simply applied to Indian philosophy.

A more accurate first-order reconstruction is:

- Max Müller raises a burden-of-proof problem around Sāṃkhya/Buddhist relation;
- Bankim in Calcutta (1871) argues that resemblance can coexist with doctrinal divergence and insists that **direction** remains a separate inference;
- Mitra in Calcutta (1883) distinguishes **pre-existing philosophical materials** from a **complete system** and rejects genealogy from mere phonetic resemblance;
- Garbe (1892) makes the demand for diagnostic rather than generic similarities explicit and ranks details / involuntary formal peculiarities as stronger evidence;
- Garbe (1894) applies dense formal correspondence to identify an `alien element` and is directly copied by Lovejoy;
- Lovejoy's 004 then separates evidence for a genuinely Sāṃkhyan doctrine/element from evidence that the later Sāṃkhya `system existed as a whole`.

This is a transregional history of an evidentiary problem, not evidence that these authors constituted one self-conscious school.

## 4. What we may and may not claim

### Secure

- Bankim's 1871 essay responds directly to Max Müller on the Sāṃkhya/Buddhism relation.
- Bankim explicitly says similarity is not identity and postpones direction of borrowing as a distinct question.
- Mitra explicitly describes pre-existing philosophical materials as `bricks` assembled by Kapila into a complete system.
- Mitra explicitly rejects Weber's phonetic genealogical conjecture as lacking `proof worth the name`.
- These are South Asian interventions in the same broad nineteenth-century evidentiary field later occupied by Garbe/Jacobi/Oldenberg/Lovejoy.

### Not secure

- No evidence currently shows Lovejoy read Bankim or Mitra.
- No evidence currently shows Garbe's proof protocol derived from Bankim or Mitra.
- `Similarity`, `direction`, `doctrine`, and `system` should not be presented as a shared codified vocabulary or common method unless direct citation/transmission is found.
- Bankim's quotation wording should be checked against the 1871 _Calcutta Review_ scan before publication-grade verbatim citation.

## 5. Consequence for the article thesis

The broader claim can now be sharpened from `comparative philology exported a method into intellectual history` to:

> Indian philosophy became a transregional site in which scholars tested how far resemblance could support historical inference: whether it established affinity, borrowing, the direction of borrowing, the survival of a doctrine, or the prior existence of a whole system.

This formulation preserves the importance of comparative philology while avoiding a one-way Europe → India method-transfer narrative.

It also gives a more precise meaning to the 004 `system existed as a whole` sentence: Lovejoy is entering a problem that had already been articulated in multiple locations, rather than inventing the distinction de novo.
