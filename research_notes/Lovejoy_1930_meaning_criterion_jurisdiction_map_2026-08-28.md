## Question

Does Lovejoy's 1930 attack on relativity merely contain the slogan `criterion ≠ meaning`, or does it instantiate a more structured analytical operation that can be compared with the repository's 1908–1914 `jurisdiction partition` sequence?

Current answer:

> **It is a structured jurisdiction argument. Lovejoy separates semantic content from the operation used to test it, separates evidence/sign from the fact inferred, allows multiple evidential routes to a proposition without multiplying its meaning, and then argues that a choice among admissible criteria cannot be allowed to smuggle the desired theoretical conclusion into the premise.**

This is stronger than the previous shorthand. It must nevertheless be paired with McGilvary's 1931 correction: the operator-level distinction may be clear while Lovejoy's attribution of the collapsed meaning/operation view to Einstein was disputed immediately and plausibly.

## 3. Operator A — verifying experience does not exhaust signification

Verhaegh reproduces Lovejoy's p. 620 objection that it is `preposterous` to suppose that `no term can ever signify anything more than what is actually given in the verifying experience`.

Lovejoy's astronomical example then separates two relations:

`apparent brightness`

= at one historical stage the sole available **experimental criterion** for probable stellar distance

but

`apparent brightness`

≠ what astronomers **mean by** stellar distance.

Lovejoy's own alternative vocabulary, again quoted by Verhaegh from p. 620, says an experimental finding may be `the sign or circumstantial evidence of something else` rather than that thing's `meaning`.

### Operator form

`operation / finding / criterion`

→ can warrant inference to `X`

without

`operation / finding / criterion`

= semantic content of `X`.

This is not merely anti-operationism. It is a relation-type firewall:

`evidential relation`

≠

`semantic identity`.

## 4. Operator B — plurality of verification routes does not multiply proposition identity

Lovejoy's p. 628 bedridden-patient example, as reported and page-located by Verhaegh, attacks a stronger consequence of identifying meaning with actual verifying procedure.

A patient observes one person being rained on outside and later encounters another person entering wet. She can infer that rain fell on both through different evidential routes. If different verification procedures automatically constituted different meanings, then what appears to be the same proposition about rain would fragment according to the route by which it was confirmed.

Lovejoy treats that result as absurd.

### Operator form

`verification route A`

≠

`verification route B`

while both may warrant

`same proposition P`.

Therefore:

`difference in warranting route`

≠ automatically

`difference in proposition meaning / identity`.

This is particularly important for the active unit-identity branch. It is a mature philosophical instance in which Lovejoy explicitly permits **identity across heterogeneous evidential access**. Do not yet convert this into a genealogy of `unit-ideas`; it is a structural control for how he handles identity conditions.

## 5. Operator C — method of inference must be separated from nature of the inferred fact

Wilson's annotated bibliography summarizes the 1930 argument in language that is highly congruent with the direct p. 620 material: Lovejoy objects to confusing the **method of inferring a relation or quality** with the **nature of the fact to be inferred**.

This can be coded more generally as:

`epistemic access relation`

≠

`ontological / semantic constitution relation`.

That distinction is thicker than `criterion ≠ meaning`. It separates:

1. how we detect or infer X;
2. what warrants belief in X;
3. what X means;
4. what X is.

Lovejoy's argument depends on refusing automatic transfer among these four jurisdictions.

## 6. Operator D — alternative criteria and anti-question-begging control

Jammer's reconstruction of Part II preserves a further Lovejoy move about Einstein's distant-simultaneity criterion.

Lovejoy proposes an alternative criterion: events at A and B could be treated as simultaneous when their light signals arrive together at a point midway between A and B **at the moment of arrival**, rather than making the relevant judgment depend on an observer whose midpoint status changes with motion. On Lovejoy's account, this alternative would not yield relativity of simultaneity.

The argumentative point is not only that another definition is imaginable. Lovejoy alleges that preferring Einstein's criterion because it alone generates the relativity result risks making the desired conclusion determine the criterion from which that conclusion is then derived.

### Operator form

possible criterion C1 → theoretical result R

possible criterion C2 → not-R

If the sole reason to privilege C1 is that C1 yields R, then:

`criterion-selection warrant`

must be independently justified

rather than

`desired result R` → privilege C1 → derive R.

This is a **premise-selection / consequence-transfer firewall**. Wilson's synopsis calls Lovejoy's Part II objection both arbitrary and circular.

## 7. The four-part jurisdiction architecture

The 1930 argument can therefore be reconstructed as four separations:

### A. semantic jurisdiction

`meaning of X` ≠ `operation by which X is tested`.

### B. evidentiary jurisdiction

`evidence/sign for X` ≠ `X itself`.

### C. identity jurisdiction

`different verification routes` ≠ `different proposition meanings`.

### D. inferential jurisdiction

`criterion chosen for testing` must not receive its warrant merely from the theoretical consequence it is then used to establish.

Compactly:

`operation`
→ `evidence / sign`
→ `warranted inference`
→ `proposition / fact`

with Lovejoy repeatedly preventing the arrows from collapsing into identity signs.

This is a mature, explicit example of what the repo has been calling **jurisdiction partition**.

## 8. Immediate contemporary counter-control — McGilvary 1931

Evander B. McGilvary, `Dialectical Arguments Against Relative Simultaneity`, *Journal of Philosophy* 28.16 (30 July 1931), 421–435, DOI 10.2307/2016187.

The crucial counterpoint, quoted and reconstructed by Verhaegh from McGilvary p. 427, is that Lovejoy attacks a stronger theory of meaning than Einstein actually requires.

McGilvary's circle example makes the distinction sharply:

- a circle is defined geometrically;
- we then use that definition to determine how to find out whether a particular figure is a circle;
- the empirical operation is therefore downstream from, rather than identical with, the concept's definition.

Verhaegh's historical judgment is that McGilvary quickly rejected Lovejoy's reading of Einstein: Einstein did not claim that concepts must be **defined by the operations used to test them**, but tied concepts to operations more indirectly.

### Why this matters for the Lovejoy repo

This counter does **not** erase Lovejoy's operator map. It changes its historical target-status:

secure:

> Lovejoy explicitly distinguished meaning from empirical criterion, sign/evidence from thing meant, and verification route from proposition identity.

less secure / contested:

> Einstein himself actually collapsed these relations in the way Lovejoy alleged.

This is exactly the sort of proposition-sensitive warrant distinction the repo should preserve.

## 9. Field control — the distinction was not Lovejoy's private invention

Verhaegh places the controversy in a broad American field shaped by relativity, Bridgman's operationism, pragmatism, realism, and emerging scientific philosophy. Bixler, Nagel, Van de Walle, Benjamin, Margenau and others were arguing over what operational criteria implied for meaning and scientific concepts.

The same 2024 reconstruction notes that *Philosophy of Science* later assembled an intellectually mixed editorial/advisory network including Bridgman, Carnap, Feigl, Lovejoy, Margenau, Montague, Northrop, Reichenbach, Schlick, and Whitehead.

So the safe historical claim is:

> **Lovejoy's 1930 jurisdiction architecture is actor-level and specific, but the problem-space was field-level.**

Do not treat `criterion ≠ meaning` as a Lovejoy invention or as evidence of disciplinary isolation.

## 10. Relation to the 1908–1914 sequence

Existing repo sequence:

- 1909: historical complex → logical analysis + historical comparison → compatibility/recombination;
- 1911: formal implication, intelligibility/meaning, evidence/verification, historical truth occupy bounded jurisdictions;

1930 adds a mature science/meaning instance:

- an empirical criterion can warrant a proposition without constituting its meaning;
- heterogeneous evidential paths can preserve proposition identity;
- the fact inferred is not reducible to the method of inference;
- criterion selection must be warranted independently of the conclusion it is used to derive.

Current structural result:

> **The continuity is thicker than a repeated taste for distinctions. Lovejoy repeatedly blocks warrant from migrating across relation-types without an explicit bridge.**

This is still an analyst-level structural continuity, not proof of unchanged doctrine or direct methodological self-transmission from 1911 to 1930.

## 11. Connection to Baltimore 1935

Carnap's same-day diary now records Lovejoy's Baltimore objection in a related but distinct form: if the new position makes everything conventional, then logical positivists can no longer simply exclude metaphysics as meaningless.

This yields a five-year sequence:

`1930: empirical criterion does not exhaust meaning`

→

`1935: conventional framework choice does not automatically license semantic exclusion of metaphysics`.

The common shape may be:

> **Do not infer a semantic conclusion merely from the rule/procedure/framework by which admissibility or testing has been organized.**

That is a promising comparison, not yet an identity claim. The 1935 objection must be reconstructed against Carnap's actual manuscript before the homology is promoted further.
