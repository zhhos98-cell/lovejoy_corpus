# Batch 91 — Citation shadow downstream of Lovejoy: Malinowski 1915 routes impersonal power through King and Leuba while Lovejoy's name disappears

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced
Scope: extend Batches 81–89 from direct reception and source-function partition to a downstream case in which the Lovejoy citation disappears while receiving syntheses that had already decomposed Lovejoy remain explicitly recommended. Primary object: Bronisław Malinowski, *Wierzenia pierwotne i formy ustroju społecznego. Pogląd na genezę religii ze szczególnem uwzględnieniem totemizmu* (Kraków: Akademia Umiejętności, 1915), written/prefaced London, April 1914. Control: James H. Leuba's direct 1905 review of Irving King's dissertation in *L'Année psychologique*.

## Core result

Malinowski 1915 offers a useful **downstream relay / citation-shadow** case, but not a direct Lovejoy reception case.

The searchable full Polish text contains:

- no occurrence of `Lovejoy`;
- no occurrence of `Primitive Energetics`;
- no occurrence of `Monist XVI`.

Yet in the chapter on the genesis of religion Malinowski develops exactly the impersonal-power problem-space in which Lovejoy had intervened. He discusses mana, arungquilta, churinga, manitu and orenda; calls manitu in essence a **mechanical factor** conferring mystical power on objects of religious veneration; and at the same time rejects the attribution to primitive people of sharply defined abstract concepts or even a subtle, clear distinction between personal agents and impersonal forces. In the footnote attached to this dossier he sends the reader directly to **Irving King, ch. VI, `The Mysterious Power`, pp.134–164, and James H. Leuba, ch. IV, pp.70–84**.

Those Leuba pages are independently identified as ch. IV, `The Origin of the Idea of Impersonal Powers`, in *A Psychological Study of Religion* (1912). They are precisely the pages where Leuba names Lovejoy, reproduces the `regular and intelligible laws` / `Primitive Energetics` proposition, rejects its mechanical strength, and nevertheless retains Lovejoy's paper as a good evidence summary.

Therefore the secure result is not:

> `Lovejoy -> Malinowski`.

It is:

> **Malinowski explicitly enters a relay environment in which King and Leuba have become sufficient named gateways to the impersonal-power dossier, while Lovejoy's name has vanished from the receiving text.**

This is a historically meaningful **citation shadow**. It shows how an author can become structurally dispensable as a citation after a problem and its evidence have been absorbed into later syntheses. It does **not** prove that Malinowski's `mechanical factor` wording derives from Lovejoy, since Malinowski simultaneously cites Marett, Hubert and Mauss, Preuss, Durkheim, King, Leuba and the primary ethnographic sources.

## 2. Malinowski's King: theoretical criticism plus positive retention

Malinowski first treats King as a major recent contribution. He reconstructs King's religion as a form of human valuation, not simply a belief in gods, spirits or a particular class of supernatural representations. He praises King's broad linkage of religious acts to vital functions and his analysis of how consciousness of value and customary norms emerge.

But when King tries to specify the religious `valuational attitude` through the social group, Malinowski becomes critical. The criticism is explicit:

1. King does not sufficiently analyze psychologically the proposed relation between religion and the group's social atmosphere.
2. He moves too quickly from a few ethnographic examples to general conclusions.
3. He neglects the possibility that part of the problem can be solved on specifically psychological grounds.
4. He mixes psychological and sociological considerations.
5. Most importantly, he fails to formulate clearly and satisfactorily what distinguishes religion from other `valuational attitudes`.

Malinowski nevertheless immediately says King's work has very great value because it emphasizes the close relation between religious/magical acts and spontaneous/natural life activities, plus the dependence of beliefs and practices on geographical, social and psychological conditions.

This resembles the source-function partition established for Leuba 1912: an authority can be locally inadequate as theory or discriminator while remaining valuable for a different analytical operation. The similarity is **structural**, not evidence of direct borrowing from Leuba at this point.

### Code

`King / definition & differentiation of religion`
→ `THEORETICAL TARGET / PARTIAL REJECTION`.

`King / religion-life-social dependency`
→ `POSITIVE ANALYTICAL RESOURCE`.

## 3. Malinowski rejects clear primitive abstraction

Immediately after the King discussion Malinowski attacks what he calls the rationalistic tendency to attribute excessively clear and abstract concepts to primitive people. His examples include:

- sharply anthropomorphic and defined spiritual beings;
- the supposedly subtle distinction between personal agents and impersonal forces;
- precisely formulated laws of homeopathic and contact magic.

His alternative is psychological and anti-reificatory. Primitive thought inhabits a world of vague and hazy concepts; abstract forces and general phenomena are more often **felt than conceptually grasped**. Beneath more definite spirits, fetishes and magical objects lies a vague, undifferentiated sense of mysterious/mystical force.

This creates an important downstream transformation of the Lovejoy-era problem-space:

`impersonal efficacy survives`

while

`clear native abstract concept is denied`.

This is especially relevant because Lovejoy's 1906 intervention had abstracted native linguistic and customary material into a general causal category. In Malinowski, the category-like comparative pattern remains analytically usable, but the native psychological state is explicitly described as vague and pre-conceptual.

Again, this is a comparison of argumentative structures, not a direct Lovejoy reception claim.

## 4. The striking conjunction: vague force + mechanical factor

Malinowski's examples then become very concrete.

- Melanesian `mana` is an invisible force associated with effects exceeding ordinary expectations about the regular course of nature.
- Central Australian `arungquilta` is nonpersonal, undifferentiated, acts at a distance and is absolutely effective when properly handled.
- analogous mysterious power appears in `churinga`.
- Algonkin `manitu` is a vague belief in a cosmic mysterious power permeating nature.
- Malinowski then says that **manitu can be regarded essentially as a mechanical factor that gives mystical power to objects of religious veneration**.
- Iroquois `orenda` is another manifestation of the same mysterious power.

The conjunction matters:

> **mechanical causal characterization no longer requires a sharply articulated native abstraction.**

Malinowski can simultaneously say:

`primitive conceptuality = vague / felt / non-abstract`

and

`impersonal force = mechanically efficacious at the analyst's descriptive level`.

This looks like a downstream separation of two components that Lovejoy 1906 had more tightly joined: causal efficacy/regularity and an abstract general category.

It should be coded as **PARALLEL TRANSFORMATION / INDIRECT RELAY ENVIRONMENT**, not `Lovejoy received`.

## 5. The footnote architecture: direct ethnographic sources + theoretical relays

Malinowski's note to this section is unusually valuable because it exposes the source architecture.

### Primary ethnographic dossier

He identifies as principal ethnological sources:

- Codrington, *The Melanesians* — mana;
- William Jones, `The Algonkin Manitou`;
- J. N. B. Hewitt/Howitt, `Orenda and a suggestion toward the origin of religion`;
- Spencer and Gillen, *The Native Tribes of Central Australia*.

### Theoretical interpreters

He then names:

- R. R. Marett;
- Hubert and Mauss;
- Th. Preuss;
- Durkheim, pp.268–292;
- **King, ch. VI, `The Mysterious Power`, pp.134–164**;
- **Leuba, ch. IV, pp.70–84**.

This hierarchy is significant. Lovejoy's 1906 article is not named either among the source dossier or among the theoretical analyses, although the Leuba chapter specifically recommended is the chapter in which Lovejoy had been directly quoted, audited and retained as an evidence summary.

The safest historical interpretation is:

> **Lovejoy has become one step removable from the citation surface.**

A reader using Leuba ch. IV encounters Lovejoy; Malinowski can cite Leuba rather than repeating Leuba's own source citation. That is a possible mechanism for **citation compression / name loss**.

It remains possible that Malinowski reached the same mechanical language through Jones, King, Marett, Durkheim, Hubert/Mauss, Preuss, or his own synthesis. The footnote does not identify a sentence-level source for `mechanical factor`.

## 6. Bibliographic asymmetry confirms name loss, but with an anomaly

Full-text search of Malinowski 1915 yields no `Lovejoy`, no `Primitive Energetics`, and no `Monist XVI`.

The bibliography explicitly lists:

- Irving King, *The Development of Religion* (New York, 1910);
- James Leuba, `Introduction to a Psychological Study of Religion`, *The Monist* XI, 195–255;
- James Leuba, `Religion, its Impulses and its Ends`.

Curiously, the bibliography as transcribed does **not** separately list Leuba's 1912 *A Psychological Study of Religion*. Yet Malinowski elsewhere explicitly cites that exact title and p.339, and the impersonal-power note sends readers to `Leuba op. cit., ch. IV, pp.70–84`. Independent bibliographic control shows that Leuba's 1912 book begins chapter IV at p.70 and that chapter IV is `The Origin of the Idea of Impersonal Powers`.

Thus the most economical reading is that Malinowski is indeed using the 1912 book, despite its omission from the bibliography list as transcribed. Because of the anomaly, code this as:

`DIRECT IN-TEXT TITLE/PAGE + STRUCTURAL CHAPTER/PAGE MATCH; BIBLIOGRAPHY OMISSION`.

Do not silently repair the bibliography.

## 7. Citation shadow as a reception mechanic

Batches 81–89 established:

- **argument disaggregation** — category, causal thesis, dossier and proof procedure travel separately;
- **warrant substitution** — the evidentiary channel changes when the proposition changes;
- **source-function partition** — the same authority can be rejected in one proposition-space and retained in another.

Malinowski adds a fourth operation:

> **citation compression / citation shadow** — a downstream synthesis can retain access to a source-conditioned problem-space through intermediary syntheses while dropping the earlier author's name.

The sequence is demonstrable only at different confidence levels:

`Lovejoy 1906`
→ `Leuba 1912 directly quotes/audits Lovejoy` **DIRECT PRIMARY**
→ `Malinowski 1915 explicitly directs readers to Leuba ch. IV pp.70–84` **DIRECT PRIMARY**

But:

`Lovejoy 1906 -> Malinowski's mechanical-factor sentence`
= **NOT DEMONSTRATED**.

Therefore `citation shadow` is an analytical description of the network position, not a claim of hidden influence.

A useful formulation is:

> **A source may disappear as a name before it disappears as an available route through the dossier.**

And more sharply:

> **citation-level absence does not imply reception-ecology absence.**

## 8. A pre-1906 control: Leuba was already reading King

The Lovejoy story should not swallow the prehistory of the King–Leuba relation.

Persée directly preserves James H. Leuba's 1905 review of Irving King's *The Differentiation of the Religious Consciousness*, pp.554–559 of *L'Année psychologique* 12. The first page already shows that Leuba treated King's Chicago doctoral thesis as an important professional-psychological intervention into the origin of religion, explicitly noting King's pragmatism/functionalism and summarizing the thesis that religious consciousness differentiates from an initially undifferentiated social life through indirect/mediating reactions that acquire group/social recognition.

Thus:

`Leuba <-> King intellectual contact`
**predates Lovejoy 1906**.

This is an important negative control against a Lovejoy-centric genealogy. Later King–Leuba convergence, disagreement or source partition cannot be assumed to have been produced by Lovejoy. Lovejoy entered an already active psychology-of-religion exchange.

We currently have direct OCR for the beginning of Leuba's 1905 review but have not collated all pp.554–559 sentence by sentence. Use it as a relation/prehistory control, not yet as a full proposition matrix.

## 9. Reception model after Batch91

The reception model now has at least four levels:

1. **Direct citation reception**
   - Leuba 1912 names Lovejoy and audits propositions.
2. **Revision-layer recruitment**
   - Jung 1917 inserts Lovejoy while constructing a new proof layer.
3. **Authorial feedback**
   - Lovejoy 1914 returns to review Leuba; exact correction still requires direct text.
4. **Citation-shadow relay**
   - Malinowski 1915 cites King and Leuba in the exact impersonal-power dossier while Lovejoy's name is absent.

This avoids treating every downstream conceptual resemblance as influence. The model records the difference between:

- named source;
- named intermediary;
- accessible downstream route;
- proposition-level similarity;
- demonstrated transmission.

## Working formulation

> **By 1915 the Lovejoy problem-space could circulate without the Lovejoy citation. Malinowski's Polish synthesis rejects sharply abstract native concepts while describing manitu as mechanically efficacious, and its documentary apparatus directs readers to King ch. VI and Leuba ch. IV. This is evidence for citation compression, not proof of hidden influence.**
