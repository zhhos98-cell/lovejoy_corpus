## Direct results

The preface explicitly defines the book's central object through `Occidental philosophy` and persistent presuppositions in `Western thought`. The main historical genealogy is therefore self-declared as Western/Occidental.

But the returned full text decisively corrects the earlier bounded-OCR impression that Buddhism/India might be nearly absent.

In Lecture II, Lovejoy defines `otherworldliness` comparatively. The discussion explicitly invokes:

- some Upanishads;
- the Vedānta system;
- a `Vedāntist and Buddhist strain` in Schopenhauer;
- `primitive Buddhism`, characterized as a pragmatic form of otherworldliness;
- India as a society in which an otherworldly philosophy can shape a socially prevalent scale of values.

At printed p.35 Lovejoy then calls Plato the main historical source of the **indigenous** strain of otherworldliness in Occidental philosophy and religion, explicitly distinguishing it from `imported Oriental varieties`.

Later, at printed p.97, primitive Buddhism reappears as one of several possible forms of pessimistic/otherworldly response.

The index independently confirms:

- `Buddhism, 30, 97`;
- `Vedânta philosophy, 30, 42`;
- `Otherworldliness, defined, 24–31` and Plato at p.35.

## Revised mature-work interpretation

The safe epilogue is **not** that the early Indic objects disappeared from *Great Chain*.

The stronger and more precise result is a **functional repartition**:

- the book's narrative center and historical genealogy are explicitly Occidental/Western;
- Indic/Buddhist material remains present inside the argument;
- but it functions mainly as a comparative/typological control for otherworldliness, pessimism and contrasts with the indigenous Occidental line;
- the early Lovejoy problem of Sanskrit/Buddhist textual identity, semantic function, causal sequence and genealogy is no longer the central evidentiary mode in which those materials appear.

Thus the transition is better described as:

`early Indic object as local philological/genealogical proof problem`
→ `mature Indic/Buddhist object as comparative philosophical type / boundary comparator inside an Occidental genealogy`.

This is more historically interesting than simple exclusion. The non-Western material survives, but its **function and evidentiary resolution change**.

## JHI-facing payoff

A compact mature-work close can now say:

> *The Great Chain of Being* explicitly centers an Occidental genealogy, but it does not simply erase the Indic materials of Lovejoy's youth. Buddhism and Vedānta remain visible as comparative forms of otherworldliness. What changes is their function: the early notebooks treat Indian materials as problems of textual, semantic and causal warrant, whereas the mature synthesis more often redeploys them as comparative types around a Western historical narrative.
