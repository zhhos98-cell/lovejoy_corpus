# New Realism 1910–1913 — analysis as a shared and contested field operation

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: **CONTEMPORARY FIELD CONTROL / DIRECT PUBLIC PRIMARY METADATA + LOVEJOY DISPUTE CONTROL**

## Core result

The discovery that Lovejoy practiced formal/logical analysis must not be converted into a contrast in which his pragmatist or realist opponents lacked analysis.

The American New Realists made **analysis itself** an explicit methodological banner. The six authors of the 1912 cooperative volume *The New Realism* were Edwin B. Holt, Walter T. Marvin, William Pepperell Montague, Ralph Barton Perry, Walter B. Pitkin, and Edward Gleason Spaulding. The volume includes Spaulding's chapter **`A Defense of Analysis`**. Contemporary/historical accounts describe the group as endorsing cooperative, piecemeal philosophical procedure and close analysis against idealist holism.

Therefore Lovejoy's 1911–1913 disputes with Marvin, Montague, Holt, Pitkin and McGilvary should be reconstructed as **intra-analytical disagreements**, not as `analysis versus non-analysis`.

## I. Shared field

The New Realist programme attempts to separate problems and relations, defend analysis against the idealist maxim that truth is the whole, and make philosophy strongly dependent on logic.

Lovejoy in the same period:

- 1909: subjects an historically real philosophical tendency to `logical analysis and historical comparison`;
- 1910: publishes directly on the opposition of propositions in formal logic and proposes a Venn-diagram method;
- 1911: disputes Walter T. Marvin's formal-logical definition of existence;
- 1913: decomposes New Realist treatments of error instead of taking `New Realism` as one proposition.

The field-level commonality is therefore real:

> **piecemeal analysis, proposition/relation discrimination, cooperation, and anti-holistic pressure were available across opposed philosophical camps.**

Do not attribute the generic operation `analysis` uniquely to Lovejoy.

## II. Where Lovejoy differs

The strongest Lovejoy-specific feature currently visible is not analysis as such but **jurisdiction partition**.

In `Existence and Formal Logic` (1911), Lovejoy refuses to let formal implication determine the meaning of existence. A historical proposition can be meaningful yet unverifiable and evidentially indefensible. Thus:

`logical relation`
≠ `meaning`
≠ `evidential warrant`
≠ `historical truth`.

In the 1909 pragmatism dispute, he likewise refuses either pure historical naming or pure logical reconstruction: a movement must have historical reference and then be subjected to logical analysis and historical comparison.

In the 1913 realism disputes, a shared realist label cannot make heterogeneous explanations of error equivalent.

Current hypothesis:

> **Lovejoy's portable operation may be less `analysis` than analysis followed by strict control over which relation type is allowed to warrant which proposition.**

This is directly compatible with the relation-specific architecture recovered in notebooks 004/005.

## III. Spaulding is especially important

Edward Gleason Spaulding is not merely a name in the New Realist collective. His 1912 chapter is explicitly `A Defense of Analysis`, and Lovejoy later collaborated with Spaulding in structuring the 1916 American Philosophical Association discussion on mental and physical entities.

This gives a particularly useful relation:

`New Realist defense of analysis`
↔ `Lovejoy critique of New Realist conclusions`
↔ `Lovejoy + Spaulding cooperative problem-definition at APA`.

Thus methodological cooperation and substantive disagreement coexist. The philosophical field cannot safely be partitioned into stable schools with internally uniform methods.

## IV. Wider implication for the unit-idea genealogy

If analytical decomposition was already a shared methodological currency among pragmatists, realists, idealists/logicians, and their critics, then the genealogy of the `unit-idea` cannot be written as the invention of analytical splitting by Lovejoy.

The better attribution problem is:

1. which analytical operations were field-level;
2. which were especially salient in Lovejoy's own practice;
3. how he combined analysis with semantic, psychological, historical/genetic and evidentiary relation types;
4. when that combined practice became a historical method for tracing recurrent components.

The potential Lovejoyian distinctiveness lies in **configuration and portability**, not possession of analysis itself.

## Evidence ceiling

### Secure
- *The New Realism* (1912) is a cooperative volume by Holt, Marvin, Montague, Perry, Pitkin and Spaulding.
- it contains Spaulding's `A Defense of Analysis`.
- Lovejoy directly disputed Marvin (1911), Montague (1909), and New Realist treatments of error (1913).
- Lovejoy and Spaulding jointly structured an APA discussion in 1916.

### Open
- exact proposition-level comparison between Spaulding's defense of analysis and Lovejoy's formal-logical papers;
- direct reception by Lovejoy of Spaulding's chapter before his 1913 New Realism critique;
- whether the shared analytical repertoire came through common Harvard/professional networks, independent convergence, or several routes.

### Do not claim
- New Realism caused Lovejoy's analytical method;
- Lovejoy alone introduced piecemeal analysis to American philosophy;
- all New Realists shared one method or one ontology;
- methodological cooperation implies doctrinal agreement.
