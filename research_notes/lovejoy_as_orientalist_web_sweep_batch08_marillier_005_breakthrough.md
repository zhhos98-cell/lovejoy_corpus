# Lovejoy as Orientalist — web sweep batch 08: Marillier / EPHE breakthrough for notebook 005

Date: 2026-08-18

## Major archival identification

Notebook `MS38_004_001_061_005` can now be placed much more securely in **Paris, academic year 1898–1899**, and very likely identified as a working/course-research notebook connected with **Léon Marillier’s EPHE conferences on “Religions des peuples non civilisés.”**

This is supported by four independent kinds of evidence that converge unusually tightly.

### 1. The notebook itself is physically Parisian

The front stationer’s leaf reads:

`LIBRAIRIE / PAPETERIE / FOURNITURES CLASSIQUES / CABANON / ... / 162, Rue Saint-Jacques / PARIS / En face de l’École de Droit.`

The notebook therefore was bought/used in Paris. The first substantive pages concern future life, death, the moral/non-moral basis of eschatology, and ethnographic literature.

### 2. Internal dates fit the 1898–99 academic year

The corrected notebook contains:

- PDF p. 11: **“Dec. 14.”**
- PDF p. 27: **“May 29”**
- PDF p. 29: inserted slip headed **“Marillier — Survivance — 12 June”**
- PDF p. 120: a faint back-matter reference beginning **“V. Marillier”**.

By themselves these dates lack a written year. Combined with the independent EPHE record below, the most economical dating is December 1898–June 1899.

### 3. EPHE’s official 1898–99 report names Lovejoy as an active participant

The official report for Marillier’s two conferences in the École pratique des hautes études, Section des sciences religieuses, states:

**Conference 1 — beliefs concerning survival of the soul.** Marillier’s central thesis was that in the eschatology of “non-civilized” peoples no moral element enters at the beginning: the other life is conceived as a **continuation**, not compensation/reparation/punishment, of terrestrial life. New evidence came especially from the two Americas.

**Conference 2 — human sacrifice and ritual anthropophagy.** Special study of Bantu peoples of southern Africa; analysis of reasons for human victims in expiatory, funerary, anthropophagic, and magical sacrifice; comparisons with the Guinea coast, North American Indigenous peoples, and non-Aryan India; parallel study of animal sacrifice, especially agrarian and fecundative rites.

The same report lists:

> `Auditeurs ayant pris une part active aux travaux : MM. Lovejoy, W. S. Andrews et Lorette.`

This is direct institutional evidence that Lovejoy did not merely live near the EPHE or read Marillier independently: he **took an active part in the work of Marillier’s conferences in 1898–99**.

Primary record: Léon Marillier, “I. Religions des peuples non civilisés,” *École pratique des hautes études, Section des sciences religieuses. Rapport sommaire sur les conférences de l'exercice 1898-1899*, p. 33 (Persée).

### 4. The notebook’s argument and source architecture closely match the course

The correspondence is specific, not generic.

Notebook opening:

- future-life beliefs begin as continuation of earthly status, not moral reward/punishment;
- prince remains prince, magical power continues after death;
- ritual performance can work in a “quasi-mechanical” way without moral judgment;
- mode of death (suicide, drowning, childbirth) can determine destination;
- survival rather than inherent immortality; second death/annihilation;
- death among “non-civilized” peoples is not originally a natural necessity but requires a special cause;
- origin-of-death myths; Codrington, Melanesian and other ethnographic material.

This is nearly the same analytical problem stated in the official report of Marillier’s first conference.

The later notebook then expands into exactly the field of Marillier’s second conference:

- funerary, expiatory/propitiatory, magical, anthropophagic and fecundative sacrifice;
- Bantu and southern-African evidence;
- Guinea/West African evidence;
- North American material;
- non-Aryan/Indian comparisons;
- agricultural/first-fruit sacrifice;
- blood as magical/fecundative efficacy;
- cannibalism and selected body parts;
- repeated attempts to distinguish sacramental communion from other causal mechanisms.

The notebook’s complete 120-page trajectory therefore corresponds to both halves of Marillier’s 1898–99 teaching program.

## A second, even stronger contextual match: Marillier’s earlier published report

An earlier EPHE report by Marillier on survival of the soul (1893–94) formulates several themes almost line-for-line with the beginning of 005:

- death is not treated as natural/normal but requires explanation;
- myths explain how death entered the world;
- the land of the dead reproduces earthly life;
- rank after death follows terrestrial rank, not moral merit;
- the afterlife is continuation rather than correction of this life;
- Melanesian traditions are studied through Codrington and comparative evidence.

Notebook 005 repeatedly abbreviates a source as `Surviv.` and on p. 29 explicitly writes **“Marillier — Survivance — 12 June.”** The likely reference is Marillier’s *La survivance de l’âme et l’idée de justice chez les peuples non civilisés*.

## Consequence: the dating and function of 005 must be revised

Previous working chronology placed 005 loosely at `c.1901–1906` because of its strong conceptual relationship to the 1906 *Monist* paper “The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy.” That dating is now superseded.

Current evidentiary judgment:

> **005 is strongly datable to Paris 1898–99 and is very likely a working notebook generated in close connection with Lovejoy’s active participation in Léon Marillier’s EPHE conferences.** It may contain additional independent reading and later insertions, so it should not be reduced to a verbatim lecture notebook; but the course context, chronology, themes, named teacher, and physical notebook evidence converge strongly.

## Consequence for the 1906 paper

This changes the interpretation of the 005 → 1906 relationship.

The notebook no longer looks like a near-contemporary draft or research notebook from shortly before publication. Instead, the 1906 theory of “primitive philosophy” appears potentially to **reactivate and theorize a comparative-religion archive assembled during Lovejoy’s Paris formation seven years earlier**.

That long interval is historically more interesting. It suggests a sequence:

**Marillier/EPHE empirical classification (1898–99) → Lovejoy notebook 005 → later theoretical abstraction into causal efficacy / “primitive energetics” (1906).**

This remains to be proved through a full source/example concordance with the 1906 primary text. But the notebook’s institutional origin is now much firmer than its publication relationship.

## Wider significance

The Orientalist/comparative-religion formation now has two directly documented Paris strands:

1. **Sylvain Lévi**: Lovejoy hoped for Pāli-Buddhist study and, when the course was unavailable, privately read sacred texts with Lévi.
2. **Léon Marillier / EPHE**: Lovejoy is officially listed as an active participant in courses on survival of the soul, human sacrifice and ritual anthropophagy; notebook 005 closely tracks the same program.

This makes Paris 1898–99 a central research site, not merely a biographical bridge between Harvard and Lovejoy’s American teaching career.

## Additional EPHE program evidence

The published **programme for 1898–99** is especially revealing. In the same Section des sciences religieuses it advertised:

- Marillier: survival of the soul; ritual anthropophagy and human sacrifice;
- Léon de Rosny: Far Eastern/American religions, including practical interpretation of Chinese philosophical terms;
- A. Foucher: elements of Nyāya and Vedānta; Laws of Manu;
- Maurice Vernes: Psalms and ancient Israelite religion;
- further courses on Judaism, Islam, Egypt, Greece and Rome.

The official report for the Indian-religions conference shows the actual 1898–99 work concentrated on **Nyāya logic**: definition, three sorts of causes, and theory of knowledge. There is no Pāli-Buddhism conference in the published program. This independently fits Wilson’s statement that the specific Pāli course Lovejoy wanted from Sylvain Lévi was not available that year.
