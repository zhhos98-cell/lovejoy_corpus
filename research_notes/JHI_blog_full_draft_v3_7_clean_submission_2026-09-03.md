The bibliography appended to Arthur O. Lovejoy’s [*Essays in the History of Ideas*](https://www.press.jhu.edu/books/title/5183/essays-history-ideas) gives him an unlikely beginning.[^1] Its first entry is an [1898 article on two Buddhist technical terms](https://dlbs.liberal.ntu.edu.tw/FULLTEXT/JR-ENG/love.htm)—not a precursor to *The Great Chain of Being*, not a statement of the unit-idea program, but a piece of specialist Buddhist scholarship. The impression is false: Lovejoy had already published an essay on Lord Monboddo in 1895.[^1] Yet the false beginning is useful. Before we have learned much about the young Lovejoy, a later bibliography has already arranged his career for us.

Nor was this Buddhist episode simply forgotten. Daniel J. Wilson documented Lovejoy’s comparative-religion training, Sanskrit and Buddhist studies, and later work in Paris; Simon Schaffer could still [note in 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/007327531004800309) that Lovejoy studied Pāli and Sanskrit there (484). Wilson’s archival transcriptions preserve something more useful than the biographical fact. Writing from Auxerre on 20 October 1898, Lovejoy proposed “to elucidate the theoretical presuppositions and doctrines of Buddhism from a philosophical point of view”: philologists, he complained, had expounded Buddhist literature while caring little for philosophy, whereas philosophers had reconstructed Buddhist conceptions “more or less out of their own heads” while neglecting “the technicalities of the texts.” He hoped to study with Sylvain Lévi for precisely this purpose; by December, when no Pāli Buddhism course was being offered, he expected instead to read Buddhist texts with Lévi privately.[^2] This was not a mature methodological manifesto. It was a young scholar naming a practical jurisdictional problem—textual control on one side, philosophical reconstruction on the other—while trying to work across it.

Among Lovejoy’s papers at Johns Hopkins is a Harvard Cooperative Society notebook labeled in his hand “Sankhya + Buddhism.”[^3] To read it as an authored treatise would immediately produce a misleading Lovejoy. The notebook is a working apparatus: copied passages, translated or paraphrased authorities, bibliographical cues, classifications, questions, objections, and local syntheses can occupy the same run of pages; handwriting tells us that Lovejoy carried a proposition onto the page, not necessarily that he originated or assented to it. Nor does a page break automatically mark a source break, while a printed source that clarifies an illegible name cannot silently supply manuscript words we cannot see. The useful question is therefore smaller and more source-critical: once the upstream source packet has been identified and its propositions returned to their owners, where does Lovejoy interrupt, reorder, qualify, connect, or stop? The notebook becomes interesting precisely at those seams.

One such seam begins with [Henry Clarke Warren](https://buddhadust.net/resources/pdf/warren/warren-on-p-s.pdf). Warren had warned that the Buddhist formula of dependent origination should not be treated as a simple “chain of causation”: its links expressed different kinds of dependence, and each had to be examined separately (xxvii). Yet when he reached *taṇhā* (desire) and *upādāna* (attachment), he treated their relation as one of identity. Lovejoy copied the judgment and interrupted it: “But not so if we are to follow the present text” (“Sankhya + Buddhism,” MS p. 75). He then compared Buddhist classifications—four kinds of attachment, six forms of desire—to ask whether the textual evidence could sustain Warren’s equation. The source criticism matters here because Warren already owns the larger problem and much of the apparatus; Lovejoy’s surviving operation is narrower, a local refusal to let one proposed relation inherit warrant from another.

With Émile Senart, the same procedure becomes easier to see because source and response separate visibly. In [“À propos de la théorie bouddhique des douze nidânas”](https://play.google.com/store/books/details/M%C3%A9langes_Charles_de_Harlez?id=g849OKp_GrsC), Senart argued that the twelve *nidānas* contained heterogeneous categories assembled from different sources; repetition and mixture could therefore indicate secondary construction (284–88). Several notebook formulations that at first look like Lovejoy’s own diagnosis belong instead to Senart’s problem field. Lovejoy can nevertheless concede heterogeneous sources, a nonhomogeneous temporal series, even borrowing, while resisting the inference that the resulting arrangement has thereby been shown accidental or unintelligible; his cleanest local response begins by naming Senart’s account and culminates in a claim that a “discoverable logical system” remains. Hermann Oldenberg had already challenged important parts of Senart’s semantic case in the third edition of [*Buddha*](https://books.google.com/books?id=ta7rJ6uWLfwC) (272–73, 443–55), so the point is neither priority nor solitary insight. It is what becomes visible after source ownership is restored: provenance can be conceded without being allowed to settle present organization.

Other pages require the same caution before they can be used as evidence about Lovejoy. A long run around dependent origination is built from Rhys Davids, Warren, Childers, Oldenberg, and local interventions rather than from one continuous Lovejoy exposition; a Four Truths/*nidāna* parallel once tempting to treat as his observation closes instead to *Vinaya Texts*; numerical *khandha* material and a continuity model belong largely to Childers. Yet source recovery can sharpen rather than dissolve the authorial seam. On manuscript p. 123, source-supplied constituent facts are recombined in a formulation for which no exact upstream wording has surfaced: “Viññāṇa is temporally (?) an antecedent of nāmarūpa, and logically a subdivision of it.” The page itself also nests *viññāṇa* within the *nāma* side of a classification. Forced onto one axis, the statements look awkward; read as a working sheet, they keep temporal sequence and logical inclusion distinct. The same term can occupy one position in a classification and another in a sequence, and the historical claim can remain strictly local: not that Lovejoy invented relational analysis, but that here he refused to make one ordering answer for another.

The path into print gives another kind of source control. On 10 April 1897 an American Oriental Society circular announced Lovejoy’s paper as a “Critical summary of the argument of the Milinda-pañha”; the final record of the Baltimore meeting, where the absent Lovejoy’s paper was read by title, listed instead “On the meaning of the Buddhist technical terms ...”; in 1898 the article appeared as “The Buddhistic Technical Terms Upadana and Upadisesa.”[^4] The sequence does not prove that one unchanged manuscript was renamed twice, but it independently documents a contraction in the public object before the final two-term article. In print Lovejoy still allows that Senart’s terms, “perhaps even the ideas,” may be largely borrowed while their “arrangement and application” remain original and characteristic (127, 132); for *upādisesa*, he says meaning must be settled “rather by an examination of its use than by etymological arguments,” retains contrary passages, and ends with the matter not “finally settled” (134, 136). Notebook, meeting record, and printed article are therefore different witnesses, not stages to be collapsed into one lost draft: together they show a broad working dossier being selectively reduced to claims Lovejoy was prepared to carry into print.

Harvard supplies provenance without supplying a single method. Lovejoy’s individual graduate record shows that in 1896–97 he took `Sanskrit 5` and `Semitic 13`, receiving an A in both; the contemporaneous course announcements identify course 5 as Charles Rockwell Lanman’s advanced Pāli course, “The Sacred Books of Buddhism,” reading the *Dhammapada*, *Sutta Nipāta*, and *Majjhima Nikāya*, and Semitic 13 as Crawford H. Toy’s “History of the Hebrew Religion, with comparison of other Semitic religions.” Charles Carroll Everett’s comparative study of religion, including Buddhism and Hindu philosophies, occupied the same graduate environment.[^5] Enrollment establishes practice, not transmission of a unified Harvard method. What the record does make unusually concrete is the institutional adjacency that Lovejoy later described as a jurisdictional problem: close textual study, comparative religion, and philosophy were all available, but they were not the same scholarly office.

A second notebook changes both the evidence and the source-critical problem. Notebook 005 has a strong Paris 1898–99 core but was reused later; scan pages can contain host-page prose, inserted sheets, folded slips, cancellations, numbered schemes, and material layers that cannot be treated as one chronological voice.[^6] Much of its apparent theory also belongs upstream. Marillier, Robertson Smith, Frazer, Tylor, Trumbull, Mannhardt and ethnographic carriers already supply many classifications of sacrifice, blood, fetish, magic, afterlife, agrarian rites, and transferable efficacy. Some June sheets are explicitly Marillier-mediated witnesses. The critical procedure must therefore run source-first: identify the printed or seminar packet, exhaust what it already says, then ask what Lovejoy does to it. What survives most securely is relational work—ranking one source as more restrained than another, reclassifying a case as alimentary rather than ritual, separating fused rationales, asking which social bearer is competent to perform a mechanism, rejecting substitution genealogies when chronology fails, and leaving rival explanations open when the evidence will not close. The page architecture matters because inserted slips and branching schemes make such acts of comparison and revision visible without giving us license to invent a composition chronology from ink, page order, or proximity alone.

This makes notebook 005 less useful as a quarry for “Lovejoy’s theory of primitive religion” and more useful as a guide to his handling of heterogeneous evidence. A differentiated fate after death cannot simply be called moral reward because it looks like one; blood in a rite does not settle whether the operative relation is propitiatory, incorporative, magical, alimentary, or something else; a similar visible practice does not by itself establish common genealogy. Source criticism consequently changes the intellectual-historical claim. The notebooks do not show Lovejoy inventing a repertoire of fine distinctions. They show him working inside already differentiated scholarly literatures and reveal, at particular seams, what he selected, reassigned, bracketed, or refused to conclude.

The later publications are most useful as exits from this notebook tour, not as its hidden destination. In [“Religion and the Time-Process”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3153919) (1902), Lovejoy could call the affinity between Aristotle’s theology and Vedānta “obvious” while rejecting eastern influence as “entirely gratuitous” when an internal Greek route was available (446–47): resemblance and genealogy remained different claims. In 1906 the stakes of classification also became overtly political. “Democracy in the Twentieth Century” split Enlightenment “generic Man” longitudinally by social-evolutionary stage and cross-wise by race or nationality, then carried those distinctions into colonial and franchise questions, including Black enfranchisement and disfranchisement. Finer distinctions were no guarantee of political innocence; the question of what evidence licensed a classification was inseparable from what that classification could be made to do.

The rest of 1906 prevents a tidy maturation story. In “Kant’s Antithesis of Dogmatism and Criticism,” Lovejoy tested a large inherited category against local propositions and rejected it; reviewing Paul Elmer More, he returned to the *Sutta Nipāta* to resist an over-positive account of Nirvāṇa; yet in [“The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy”](https://doi.org/10.5840/monist190616324) he moved in the opposite scalar direction, abstracting impersonal, communicable, quasi-mechanical efficacy across heterogeneous fields and, after seeking lexical warrant, returning that analyst’s relation at a larger address as a “fundamental concept” of “primitive philosophy” and “savage thought,” even while acknowledging incomplete evidence and an initially over-categorical statement. The notebooks therefore need not be made into a prehistory of either caution or generalization. They show the smaller operations from which either scalar outcome could be built.

By the time Lovejoy published [*The Great Chain of Being*](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL7692618M/The_Great_Chain_of_Being), Asian materials had not disappeared, but they occupied a different place. The preface defines the principal historical object through “Occidental philosophy” and “Western thought”; Buddhism and Vedānta remain visible, while “primitive Buddhism” recurs as a comparative form. In the 1890s notebooks, Buddhist and Indian materials had themselves been local textual, semantic, causal, and genealogical problems; in *Great Chain*, they more often serve as comparative types or boundaries inside a predominantly Occidental genealogy. The change is one of historical function and address, not simple presence or absence.

Returning to the notebooks therefore does not reveal a forgotten Buddhist origin of the history of ideas. It reveals something more archival. A notebook page is not yet a proposition, a handwritten proposition is not yet Lovejoy’s, a source match is not yet assent, a material insertion is not yet a dated revision, and a printed article is not simply the cleaned endpoint of everything that preceded it. Once those distinctions are kept in view, the early Buddhist episode becomes less mysterious and more consequential: we can watch a young scholar inherit other people’s questions and categories, test the relations among them, narrow some claims for publication, transport some distinctions into other fields, and sometimes reassemble locally separated relations into much larger historical objects. The odd first item in the *Essays* bibliography then looks different. What later bibliographies arranged as an early publication can now be read, through the surviving working papers, as a set of source-critical acts whose scale and ownership changed from page to page.
