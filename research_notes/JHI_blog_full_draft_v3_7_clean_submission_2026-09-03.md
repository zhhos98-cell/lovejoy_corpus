In 1898 Arthur O. Lovejoy published [“The Buddhistic Technical Terms Upadana and Upadisesa”](https://dlbs.liberal.ntu.edu.tw/FULLTEXT/JR-ENG/love.htm), an article on the meaning and place of two Buddhist terms within the philosophical system represented in the Pāli texts.[^1] The article asks a narrow question, but Lovejoy’s notes from the same period show the larger difficulty behind it: how could the doctrines described by philologists be reconstructed philosophically without allowing philosophical reconstruction to outrun the texts?

Writing from Auxerre on 20 October 1898, Lovejoy put that difficulty plainly. He proposed “to elucidate the theoretical presuppositions and doctrines of Buddhism from a philosophical point of view.” Philologists, he complained, had expounded Buddhist literature while caring little for philosophy; philosophers had reconstructed Buddhist conceptions “more or less out of their own heads” while neglecting “the technicalities of the texts.” He chose Sylvain Lévi for the Buddhist side of the problem; by December, when no Pāli Buddhism course was being offered, he expected instead to read Buddhist texts with Lévi privately.[^2]

His Harvard work had already combined Pāli study with comparative religion. In 1896–97 Lovejoy took `Sanskrit 5` and `Semitic 13`, receiving an A in both. The course announcements identify the first as Charles Rockwell Lanman’s advanced Pāli course, “The Sacred Books of Buddhism,” reading the *Dhammapada*, *Sutta Nipāta*, and *Majjhima Nikāya*, and the second as Crawford H. Toy’s “History of the Hebrew Religion, with comparison of other Semitic religions.”[^3] That curriculum was not his only point of contact with comparative religion. In March 1897 *Prabuddha Bhārata* printed Lovejoy among the signatories of an address from members of the Cambridge Conferences to Vivekananda, identifying him as secretary of the Harvard Graduate Philosophical Society for 1896–97; the address described the Conferences as devoted to comparative study in “Ethics, Philosophy, and Religion.”[^3a]

Among Lovejoy’s papers at Johns Hopkins survives a Harvard Cooperative Society notebook labeled in his hand “Sankhya + Buddhism.”[^4] Its pages mix extracts from Buddhist scholarship with lexical notes, diagrams, objections, and short interventions. Early in the notebook Lovejoy says that the “general philosophical position” of Buddhism has to be understood through the technical working-out and “very complicated inter-relation” of the twelve *nidānas*, by “a careful analysis (i.e. a comparison of texts).” The problem was therefore not exhausted by translating individual terms. Their relations also had to be specified. One page makes the point spatially: six lines of consciousness converge on *nāmarūpa*, which then fans into six sense-organs and six parallel channels through contact, sensation, and desire.[^4] But the notebook is not a continuous authored exposition. Much of its surrounding apparatus belongs to the scholars Lovejoy was reading; the strongest evidence for his own work appears where he interrupts a source, rearranges source-supplied material, or marks how far a claim may go. One early page marks that boundary in its own syntax: after a Rhys Davids source block, Lovejoy begins “To R.D.’s remark it shld be added” and shifts the test from generic claims about the eternity of Matter and Self to what he calls Sāṃkhya’s “thorough-going dualism,” the separateness of Self and World.[^4] Recovering a source can therefore reduce what should be attributed to Lovejoy while making a local intervention more precise.

[Henry Clarke Warren](https://buddhadust.net/resources/pdf/warren/warren-on-p-s.pdf) supplied one difficulty. Warren had already warned that dependent origination should not be treated as a simple “chain of causation.” In his own analysis, different links could stand as antecedent condition, cause and effect, category and instance, identity, or interdependence; time, he argued, played only a secondary role. The relation of each member to the next had to be examined separately (xxvii–xxx). Warren’s own treatment of *taṇhā* (desire) and *upādāna* (attachment) was not a flat identity claim: he described attachment as the “tenacity of desire” while also reporting a stage distinction between desire before possession and attachment once the object was seized (§34). Lovejoy compressed that close semantic relation into a stronger “identity” problem and interrupted it: “But not so if we are to follow the present text” (“Sankhya + Buddhism,” MS p. 75). He then set four kinds of attachment beside six forms of desire and asked what different work the two terms were doing. The point is not that Lovejoy discovered heterogeneous relation-types; he sharpened one of Warren’s own assignments into a proposition and turned Warren’s philological rule back on it.

Émile Senart posed another difficulty. In [“À propos de la théorie bouddhique des douze nidânas”](https://play.google.com/store/books/details/M%C3%A9langes_Charles_de_Harlez?id=g849OKp_GrsC), Senart argued that the twelve *nidānas* contained heterogeneous categories assembled from different sources. On his reading *upādāna* could abbreviate the five *upādānakkhandhas*: the aggregates would then appear once collectively and again in distributed form among neighboring links, making repetition evidence for a secondary construction (284–88). Lovejoy accepted much of the compositeness problem but rejected the conclusion that the order was merely accidental. One part of the dispute was already shared across the field: Oldenberg’s 1897 third edition had rejected Senart’s identification of *upādāna* with the five *upādānakkhandhas* from canonical usage.[^7] Lovejoy’s stronger local seam lies elsewhere. At the point where Senart’s *ordre plus ou moins accidentel* is under discussion, he grants the historical problem but writes that the repetitions are intentional and that a “discoverable logical system” governs the arrangement. Historical compositeness did not by itself decide present logical order. The compound *upādānakkhandha* itself did not fix one causal direction: the commentary Lovejoy cites allows the skandhas either to be produced by *upādāna* or to be its causes, and he says both readings are to be accepted (128). Two pages later in the notebook, after returning to Senart’s historical-genealogical material, an initialled question marks another intervention: “But is there necessarily any ontological function involved? A.O.L.”[^4] In print he makes the restriction explicit: Buddhism is “based, not upon a metaphysic, but upon a Psychology of sensation,” and the first *nidāna* does not name an ontological Absolute (131–32).

The sharpest statement appears on manuscript p. 123: “Viññāṇa is temporally (?) an antecedent of nāmarūpa, and logically a subdivision of it.” The same term occupies one place in a temporal sequence and another in a logical classification. A second distinction begins from a source Lovejoy names. R. Spence Hardy had already described renewed existence as produced through *upādāna* while karma governed its manner; on the notebook page citing Hardy p. 394, Lovejoy recasts that description as a question about the “fact” versus the “manner” of continued existence.[^7] In the 1898 article he stabilizes the distinction: karma determines the particular condition of rebirth, while *upādāna* determines rebirth “in itself.” Lovejoy then gives the division a historical rationale. Karma, as an inherited moral-causal category, could explain the better or worse condition of a future life while taking continued rebirth for granted. A Buddhist system in which rebirth itself can cease posed another question: what explains renewed existence as such? The two terms can therefore participate in the same rebirth process without doing the same explanatory work.

The article’s second technical term forced the same problem into lexical form. Oldenberg had already argued that *upādisesa* referred chiefly to an ethical residue rather than simply to the continued presence of the physical aggregates, and had assembled much of the canonical evidence Lovejoy later used. But the nineteenth-century lexical literature offered incompatible derivations: a Childers–Rhys Davids line connected *upādi* with grasping and the five *khandhas*, while Müller and Dahlmann supported a relation with *upadhi*.[^8] Lovejoy did not first choose one word-history and let it settle the meaning. He treated both derivations as etymologically possible and shifted the burden to usage. In the notebook, one of his clearest arguments is that physical death cannot itself be the Buddhist *summum bonum*; in print, the Anāgāmin/Arahat contrast, the case of an already-deceased monk, and other textual usages are made to bear on what sort of residue is at issue. Here the difference from Oldenberg is narrow but revealing. Oldenberg closed the contradiction with a confident orthographic and textual-history solution, effectively identifying *upādi* with *upadhi*; Lovejoy retained much of the semantic result while refusing to let that genealogy settle the case. The semantic preference becomes stronger while etymology and the total textual history remain less certain.

A year before the article appeared, the AOS’s public description of Lovejoy’s communication changed. A final circular of 10 April 1897 announced “Critical summary of the argument of the Milinda-pañha.” At the Baltimore meeting on 22–24 April Lovejoy was absent, and the later Proceedings place No. 30 among papers read by title; they do not independently preserve the exact title used at the meeting. In the June printed Proceedings, No. 30 appears instead as a paper on the meaning of *upādānam* and *upādāna-kkhandhā*. The published article of 1898 was on *upādāna* and *upādisesa*.[^5] In the finished article the *Milindapañha* supplies evidence; *upādānakkhandha* remains central to the dispute with Senart in the first section; *upādisesa* occupies the second. The sequence records a change in what was made to carry the paper’s public identity, even though the mechanism of that change is not recoverable from the surviving carriers. The *Milindapañha* moves from title-level object to textual witness; *upādānakkhandha* moves from a June title-level co-object into an internal controversy; *upādisesa* is promoted into the published title. This is more than a record of changing wording, but it is not evidence for one unchanged manuscript passing through three titles.

The notebook also records Lovejoy narrowing what his paper needed to do. After a broader discussion of Nirvāṇa he writes, “This is all that is required for the purposes of my paper.” A substantial Sāṃkhya/Buddhism chronology branch does not appear in the article. A long *khandha–nidāna* worksheet is reduced to a remark that the analysis cannot be reviewed there. This pruning is uneven. Broad genealogy and large classificatory schemes recede; the Hardy fact/manner allocation, the Senart problem of compositeness and order, and the usage-based *upādisesa* argument survive in narrower forms. One notebook page even divides “Original Elements” from “Derived Elements,” but the article does not publish that historical anatomy as a settled genealogy. Instead it allows terminology, and perhaps ideas, to be borrowed while arguing that their “arrangement and application” can still be distinctive. Even there Lovejoy stops short of a final settlement: after arguing for an ethical reading of *upādisesa*, he leaves contrary passages standing and concludes that the matter cannot be regarded as “finally settled” (136).

A related caution about moving from classification to historical sequence appears in notebook 005, a physically and chronologically composite body of comparative-religion notes with a strong Paris 1898–99 core.[^6] The institutional link is direct: Marillier’s official EPHE report names Lovejoy among the `auditeurs ayant pris une part active aux travaux` of his conferences on `Religions des peuples non civilisés`, while separately listing the *élèves titulaires*.[^6] Lovejoy reclassifies one reported practice as alimentary rather than ritual and rejects a proposed ritual genealogy when external dates do not fit it. Elsewhere a sacrifice can be “purely mechanical,” like a charm acting directly on natural phenomena even when “the god is not concerned in it at all”; on another page Lovejoy says mixed sacrificial forms can be dissociated only by a “delicate analysis.” He also writes that animal sacrifice is “not an evidence of advancement,” while a later sequence tests human sacrifice being modified into domestic-animal sacrifice.[^6] Here too, classification does not by itself establish historical sequence.

The surviving bibliographic record also lists a 1901 `Syllabus: The philosophy of Buddhism, four lectures` for the Washington University Association; the syllabus itself remains unrecovered.[^9] A related limit on historical inference appears again in [“Religion and the Time-Process”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3153919) (1902). Here the continuity is partly authorial rather than retrospective: while summarizing the Buddhist analysis on p. 442, Lovejoy directed readers to “the writer’s paper” on *upādāna* and *upādisesa*; the footnote itself misprinted the earlier *JAOS* volume as XVIII rather than XIX.[^9] He then called the affinity between Aristotle’s theology and Vedānta “obvious” while treating eastern influence as “entirely gratuitous” when an internal Greek route was available (446–47). Resemblance was not enough to establish transmission. This later case does not make the Buddhist notebook a blueprint for a mature method; it shows that the burden separating comparison from historical derivation could reappear at a larger scale. The same habit of separating relations remained available in 1904. In “The Dialectic of Bruno and Spinoza,” Lovejoy split immanence and transcendence into whether finite things’ being—their “that”—was included in the Absolute and whether their determinate nature—their “what”—was included, using Vedānta as a worked case in which the first relation could be affirmed while the second was denied.[^9] A second 1904 article makes the proof burden even more explicit: in “Some Eighteenth Century Evolutionists,” progressive temporal succession does not by itself entail species mutability, and evidence can “suggest a good deal” without being designed to “bear any of the weight of proof.”[^9] A more direct Buddhist continuity appears in 1906: reviewing Paul Elmer More’s *Shelburne Essays*, Lovejoy returned to *Sutta Nipāta* 1073–1076, the same canonical text important to his 1898 *upādisesa* argument, to resist a positive reconstruction of Nirvāṇa.[^9]

The late-1890s Buddhist problem was therefore larger than the translation of difficult terms. Buddhist doctrine could not be reconstructed by treating every relation as the same kind of relation. *Viññāṇa* could precede *nāmarūpa* temporally while falling within it logically. *Upādāna* and karma could answer different causal questions. A composite formula could still have an intelligible order. Etymology could remain unsettled while usage supported a semantic judgment. Similar rites could have different histories. Temporal order, logical inclusion, causal function, lexical genealogy, textual usage, resemblance, and historical derivation had to be argued separately. The notebook matters not because it contains an embryonic version of Lovejoy’s later method, but because it shows these burdens being redistributed in the course of concrete disputes, and then unevenly selected for print.
