# Lovejoy’s Buddhism Problem

In 1898 Arthur O. Lovejoy published [“The Buddhistic Technical Terms Upadana and Upadisesa”](https://dlbs.liberal.ntu.edu.tw/FULLTEXT/JR-ENG/love.htm), an article on the meaning and place of two Buddhist terms within the philosophical system represented in the Pāli texts.[^1] The article asks a narrow question, but Lovejoy’s notes from the same period show the larger difficulty behind it: how could the doctrines described by philologists be reconstructed philosophically without allowing philosophical reconstruction to outrun the texts?

Writing from Auxerre on 20 October 1898, Lovejoy put that difficulty plainly. He proposed “to elucidate the theoretical presuppositions and doctrines of Buddhism from a philosophical point of view.” Philologists, he complained, had expounded Buddhist literature while caring little for philosophy; philosophers had reconstructed Buddhist conceptions “more or less out of their own heads” while neglecting “the technicalities of the texts.” He chose Sylvain Lévi for the Buddhist side of the problem; by December, when no Pāli Buddhism course was being offered, he expected instead to read Buddhist texts with Lévi privately.[^2]

The two sides of the problem were already present in his Harvard work. In 1896–97 Lovejoy took `Sanskrit 5` and `Semitic 13`, receiving an A in both. The course announcements identify the first as Charles Rockwell Lanman’s advanced Pāli course, “The Sacred Books of Buddhism,” reading the *Dhammapada*, *Sutta Nipāta*, and *Majjhima Nikāya*, and the second as Crawford H. Toy’s “History of the Hebrew Religion, with comparison of other Semitic religions.” Charles Carroll Everett’s comparative study of religion, including Buddhism and Hindu philosophies, occupied the same graduate environment.[^3]

Among Lovejoy’s papers at Johns Hopkins is a Harvard Cooperative Society notebook labeled in his hand “Sankhya + Buddhism.”[^4] Its pages mix Warren, Rhys Davids, Childers, Oldenberg, Senart, lexical notes, diagrams, objections, and short interventions. Early in the notebook Lovejoy says that the “general philosophical position” of Buddhism has to be understood through the technical working-out and “very complicated inter-relation” of the twelve *nidānas*, by “a careful analysis (i.e. a comparison of texts).” The problem was therefore not exhausted by translating individual terms. Their relations also had to be specified.

[Henry Clarke Warren](https://buddhadust.net/resources/pdf/warren/warren-on-p-s.pdf) supplied one difficulty. Warren had warned that dependent origination should not be treated as a simple “chain of causation”: different links expressed different kinds of dependence and had to be examined separately (xxvii). Yet when he reached *taṇhā* (desire) and *upādāna* (attachment), he treated their relation as one of identity. Lovejoy copied the judgment and interrupted it: “But not so if we are to follow the present text” (“Sankhya + Buddhism,” MS p. 75). He then set four kinds of attachment beside six forms of desire and asked what different work the two terms were doing.

Émile Senart posed another difficulty. In [“À propos de la théorie bouddhique des douze nidânas”](https://play.google.com/store/books/details/M%C3%A9langes_Charles_de_Harlez?id=g849OKp_GrsC), Senart argued that the twelve *nidānas* contained heterogeneous categories assembled from different sources; repetition and mixture could therefore indicate secondary construction (284–88). Lovejoy accepted much of the compositeness problem but rejected the conclusion that the order was merely accidental. At the point where Senart’s *ordre plus ou moins accidentel* is under discussion, Lovejoy writes that the repetitions are intentional and that a “discoverable logical system” governs the arrangement. For him, evidence of borrowing did not by itself decide whether the resulting arrangement made philosophical sense.

The sharpest statement appears on manuscript p. 123: “Viññāṇa is temporally (?) an antecedent of nāmarūpa, and logically a subdivision of it.” The same term occupies one place in a temporal sequence and another in a logical classification. Lovejoy makes a similar distinction when he asks whether *upādāna* is the cause of the fact of continued existence while *karma* is the cause of its manner. In the 1898 article he states the distinction directly: karma determines the particular condition of rebirth, while *upādāna* determines rebirth “in itself.”

The subject of Lovejoy’s AOS paper changed during 1897. A final circular of 10 April announced “Critical summary of the argument of the Milinda-pañha.” At the Baltimore meeting two weeks later, where Lovejoy was absent and the communication was read by title, the Proceedings record a paper on the meaning of *upādāna* and *upādāna-kkhandhā*. The published article of 1898 was instead on *upādāna* and *upādisesa*.[^5] In the finished article the *Milindapañha* supplies evidence; *upādānakkhandha* remains central to the dispute with Senart in the first section; *upādisesa* occupies the second.

Notebook 004 also records Lovejoy limiting the argument he intended to publish. After a broader discussion of Nirvāṇa he writes, “This is all that is required for the purposes of my paper.” A substantial Sāṃkhya/Buddhism chronology branch does not appear in the article. A long *khandha–nidāna* worksheet is reduced to a remark that the analysis cannot be reviewed there. The notebook’s “Original Elements / Derived Elements” allocation is not carried into print. The article concentrates instead on the relations needed to settle the two technical-term problems. Even there Lovejoy stops short of a final settlement: after arguing for an ethical reading of *upādisesa*, he leaves contrary passages standing and concludes that the matter cannot be regarded as “finally settled” (136).

Notebook 005 turns from these Buddhist technical questions to comparative religion. It has a strong Paris 1898–99 core but was reused later; its scan pages contain host-page prose, inserted sheets, folded slips, cancellations, and numbered schemes.[^6] Across notes on sacrifice, blood, fetish, magic, afterlife, agrarian rites, and transferable efficacy, Lovejoy repeatedly asks what kind of explanation a case will support. He recasts one case as alimentary rather than ritual; distinguishes collective expiation from magic by asking whether the action belongs to the community or to a specialist priest; keeps Grout’s reported case while rejecting the deferred-payment explanation attached to it; and allows circumcision to resemble sacrificial substitution while refusing the proposed substitute-human-sacrifice genealogy.

Chronology repeatedly changes the answer. On one set of pages, an attractive ritual genealogy is tested against external dates and fails. Elsewhere two rival explanations remain open. Lovejoy treats moralized afterlife beliefs as capable of entering older journey traditions later; sacrifice can contain elements with different histories; ancestor cult, local cult, nature cult, and high-god material need not have the same date merely because they occur together. A classification of forms, in other words, does not by itself establish their historical sequence.

The later reuse of notebook 005 shows two different applications of developmental language. Lovejoy can write of a “higher stage of culture” and of “primitive peoples,” placing populations or cultures on an evolutionary scale. But in a 1905 teaching outline he also writes “Greek primitive” and “N.T. intermediate.” There *primitive* marks an earlier stratum within a tradition. The same word can therefore refer either to a population’s place in a broader developmental ranking or to an earlier layer within Greek or Christian history.

The distinction between likeness and history appears again in [“Religion and the Time-Process”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3153919) (1902). Lovejoy called the affinity between Aristotle’s theology and Vedānta “obvious” while treating eastern influence as “entirely gratuitous” when an internal Greek route was available (446–47). Resemblance was not enough to establish transmission.

By 1906 these questions were being asked on much larger materials. “Democracy in the Twentieth Century” divided Enlightenment “generic Man” longitudinally by social-evolutionary stage and cross-wise by race or nationality, then carried those distinctions into colonial and franchise judgments, including Black enfranchisement and disfranchisement. In [“The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy”](https://doi.org/10.5840/monist190616324), Lovejoy argued that impersonal, communicable, quasi-mechanical efficacy recurred across heterogeneous religious materials and could be treated as a “fundamental concept” of “primitive philosophy” and “savage thought.” The developmental distinctions that had appeared in comparative religion now also entered explicit judgments about peoples, government, and historical stage.

Later still, [*The Great Chain of Being*](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL7692618M/The_Great_Chain_of_Being) framed its principal history through “Occidental philosophy” and “Western thought.” Buddhism and Vedānta remained present, but mainly as comparisons within that history. The 1890s Buddhist notes had required Lovejoy to decide, case by case, what a term meant, what kind of relation joined it to another term, whether an apparent sequence was temporal or logical, whether resemblance established descent, and whether a present combination represented one historical layer or several.

That is Lovejoy’s Buddhism problem in the 1890s. Buddhist doctrine could not be reconstructed by treating every relation as the same kind of relation. *Viññāṇa* could precede *nāmarūpa* temporally while falling within it logically. *Upādāna* and karma could answer different causal questions. A composite formula could still have an intelligible order. Similar rites could have different histories. Elements found together in a religion could belong to different dates. And *primitive* could name either a ranked population or an earlier stratum within a tradition. Temporal order, logical inclusion, causal function, resemblance, and historical derivation had to be argued separately.
