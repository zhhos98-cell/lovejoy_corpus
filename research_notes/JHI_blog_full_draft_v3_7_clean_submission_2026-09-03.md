# Lovejoy’s Buddhism Problem

In 1898 Arthur O. Lovejoy published [“The Buddhistic Technical Terms Upadana and Upadisesa”](https://dlbs.liberal.ntu.edu.tw/FULLTEXT/JR-ENG/love.htm), an article on the meaning and place of two Buddhist terms within the philosophical system represented in the Pāli texts.[^1] The article asks a narrow question, but Lovejoy’s notes from the same period show the larger difficulty behind it: how could the doctrines described by philologists be reconstructed philosophically without allowing philosophical reconstruction to outrun the texts?

Writing from Auxerre on 20 October 1898, Lovejoy put that difficulty plainly. He proposed “to elucidate the theoretical presuppositions and doctrines of Buddhism from a philosophical point of view.” Philologists, he complained, had expounded Buddhist literature while caring little for philosophy; philosophers had reconstructed Buddhist conceptions “more or less out of their own heads” while neglecting “the technicalities of the texts.” He chose Sylvain Lévi for the Buddhist side of the problem; by December, when no Pāli Buddhism course was being offered, he expected instead to read Buddhist texts with Lévi privately.[^2]

His Harvard work had already combined Pāli study with comparative religion. In 1896–97 Lovejoy took `Sanskrit 5` and `Semitic 13`, receiving an A in both. The course announcements identify the first as Charles Rockwell Lanman’s advanced Pāli course, “The Sacred Books of Buddhism,” reading the *Dhammapada*, *Sutta Nipāta*, and *Majjhima Nikāya*, and the second as Crawford H. Toy’s “History of the Hebrew Religion, with comparison of other Semitic religions.”[^3]

Among Lovejoy’s papers at Johns Hopkins survives a Harvard Cooperative Society notebook labeled in his hand “Sankhya + Buddhism.”[^4] Its pages mix extracts from Buddhist scholarship with lexical notes, diagrams, objections, and short interventions. Early in the notebook Lovejoy says that the “general philosophical position” of Buddhism has to be understood through the technical working-out and “very complicated inter-relation” of the twelve *nidānas*, by “a careful analysis (i.e. a comparison of texts).” The problem was therefore not exhausted by translating individual terms. Their relations also had to be specified.

[Henry Clarke Warren](https://buddhadust.net/resources/pdf/warren/warren-on-p-s.pdf) supplied one difficulty. Warren had warned that dependent origination should not be treated as a simple “chain of causation”: different links expressed different kinds of dependence and had to be examined separately (xxvii). Yet when he reached *taṇhā* (desire) and *upādāna* (attachment), he treated their relation as one of identity. Lovejoy copied the judgment and interrupted it: “But not so if we are to follow the present text” (“Sankhya + Buddhism,” MS p. 75). He then set four kinds of attachment beside six forms of desire and asked what different work the two terms were doing.

Émile Senart posed another difficulty. In [“À propos de la théorie bouddhique des douze nidânas”](https://play.google.com/store/books/details/M%C3%A9langes_Charles_de_Harlez?id=g849OKp_GrsC), Senart argued that the twelve *nidānas* contained heterogeneous categories assembled from different sources; repetition and mixture could therefore indicate secondary construction (284–88). Lovejoy accepted much of the compositeness problem but rejected the conclusion that the order was merely accidental. At the point where Senart’s *ordre plus ou moins accidentel* is under discussion, Lovejoy writes that the repetitions are intentional and that a “discoverable logical system” governs the arrangement. For him, evidence of borrowing did not by itself decide whether the resulting arrangement made philosophical sense.

The sharpest statement appears on manuscript p. 123: “Viññāṇa is temporally (?) an antecedent of nāmarūpa, and logically a subdivision of it.” The same term occupies one place in a temporal sequence and another in a logical classification. Lovejoy makes a similar distinction when he asks whether *upādāna* is the cause of the fact of continued existence while *karma* is the cause of its manner. In the 1898 article he states the distinction directly: karma determines the particular condition of rebirth, while *upādāna* determines rebirth “in itself.”

A year before the article appeared, the AOS’s public description of Lovejoy’s communication changed. A final circular of 10 April 1897 announced “Critical summary of the argument of the Milinda-pañha.” At the Baltimore meeting on 22–24 April Lovejoy was absent, and the later Proceedings place No. 30 among papers read by title; they do not independently preserve the exact title used at the meeting. In the June printed Proceedings, No. 30 appears instead as a paper on the meaning of *upādānam* and *upādāna-kkhandhā*. The published article of 1898 was on *upādāna* and *upādisesa*.[^5] In the finished article the *Milindapañha* supplies evidence; *upādānakkhandha* remains central to the dispute with Senart in the first section; *upādisesa* occupies the second.

The notebook also records Lovejoy narrowing what his paper needed to do. After a broader discussion of Nirvāṇa he writes, “This is all that is required for the purposes of my paper.” A substantial Sāṃkhya/Buddhism chronology branch does not appear in the article. A long *khandha–nidāna* worksheet is reduced to a remark that the analysis cannot be reviewed there. The article concentrates instead on the relations needed to address the two technical-term problems. Even there Lovejoy stops short of a final settlement: after arguing for an ethical reading of *upādisesa*, he leaves contrary passages standing and concludes that the matter cannot be regarded as “finally settled” (136).

A related caution about moving from classification to historical sequence appears in notebook 005, a physically and chronologically composite body of comparative-religion notes with a strong Paris 1898–99 core.[^6] Lovejoy reclassifies one reported practice as alimentary rather than ritual, rejects a proposed ritual genealogy when external dates do not fit it, and elsewhere leaves rival mechanisms open when the evidence does not decide between them. Here too, classification does not by itself establish historical sequence.

A related limit on historical inference appears again in [“Religion and the Time-Process”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3153919) (1902). Lovejoy called the affinity between Aristotle’s theology and Vedānta “obvious” while treating eastern influence as “entirely gratuitous” when an internal Greek route was available (446–47). Resemblance was not enough to establish transmission.

The late-1890s Buddhist problem was therefore larger than the translation of difficult terms. Buddhist doctrine could not be reconstructed by treating every relation as the same kind of relation. *Viññāṇa* could precede *nāmarūpa* temporally while falling within it logically. *Upādāna* and karma could answer different causal questions. A composite formula could still have an intelligible order. Similar rites could have different histories. Temporal order, logical inclusion, causal function, resemblance, and historical derivation had to be argued separately.
