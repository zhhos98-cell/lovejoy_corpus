# What Was Arthur Lovejoy Doing with Buddhism?

The bibliography appended to Arthur O. Lovejoy’s [*Essays in the History of Ideas*](https://www.press.jhu.edu/books/title/5183/essays-history-ideas) gives him an unlikely beginning. Its first entry is an [1898 article on two Buddhist technical terms](https://dlbs.liberal.ntu.edu.tw/FULLTEXT/JR-ENG/love.htm), a piece of specialist Buddhist scholarship published decades before *The Great Chain of Being*.[^1] Lovejoy had in fact already published an essay on Lord Monboddo in 1895. Yet the bibliographical sequence poses a useful question: what was the future historian of the great chain of being trying to settle in a dispute over Buddhist terminology?

Nor was this Buddhist episode simply forgotten. Daniel J. Wilson documented Lovejoy’s comparative-religion training, his Sanskrit and Buddhist studies, and his later work in Paris. Simon Schaffer could still [note in 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/007327531004800309) that Lovejoy studied Pāli and Sanskrit there (484). The biographical fact survived; what survived less clearly was the work inside it. Among Lovejoy’s papers at Johns Hopkins is a Harvard Cooperative Society notebook labeled in his hand “Sankhya + Buddhism.” Its pages bring us into the disputes behind the published article.[^4]

Lovejoy himself named the larger difficulty from Auxerre on 20 October 1898. He proposed “to elucidate the theoretical presuppositions and doctrines of Buddhism from a philosophical point of view.” Philologists, he complained, had expounded Buddhist literature while caring little for philosophy; philosophers reconstructed Buddhist conceptions “more or less out of their own heads” while neglecting “the technicalities of the texts.” He therefore chose Sylvain Lévi as a teacher on the Buddhist texts, and by December expected to read with him privately when no Pāli Buddhism course was offered.[^2]

That project also had an institutional prehistory. In 1896–97 Lovejoy took Charles Rockwell Lanman’s advanced Pāli course, “The Sacred Books of Buddhism,” and Crawford H. Toy’s “History of the Hebrew Religion, with comparison of other Semitic religions,” receiving an A in both; Charles Carroll Everett’s comparative study of religions occupied the same graduate environment.[^3] Harvard did not hand Lovejoy one unified method, but it placed textual study, philosophy, and comparison in the same field.

Two disputes establish the terrain of notebook 004. Henry Clarke Warren had warned that dependent origination expressed several kinds of dependence rather than one simple causal chain; Lovejoy used that warning to resist treating *taṇhā* and *upādāna* as doing the same work. Émile Senart, meanwhile, argued that heterogeneous and repeated components pointed to a secondary construction; Lovejoy accepted possible borrowing but denied that provenance by itself settled whether the resulting Buddhist arrangement was intelligible.[^7]

One page makes the larger issue unusually clear. On manuscript p. 123, Lovejoy places *viññāṇa* within the *nāma* side of a classification and writes beside it that “Viññāṇa is temporally (?) an antecedent of nāmarūpa, and logically a subdivision of it.” Forced onto one axis, the statements look contradictory. They are not. *Viññāṇa* can precede *nāmarūpa* in a sequence and at the same time fall within it as a logical subdivision. The question mark beside “temporally” matters: temporal order is still being tested while logical inclusion is stated.[^4]

The 1898 article preserves the same separation in print. Lovejoy allows that the formula’s terms, “perhaps even the ideas,” may be borrowed, yet calls their “arrangement and application” original and characteristic (127, 132). Provenance and function are different relations: borrowed components do not by themselves decide what a later arrangement means or does.[^7] The same restriction appears when he refuses to let competing etymologies settle *upādisesa* and concludes that the question “cannot be regarded as finally settled” (136).[^8] Even the surviving AOS title record shows the public object shifting across 1897–98, though it does not recover a simple revision sequence.[^5]

Notebook 005 repeats the point on different material. It has a strong Paris 1898–99 core but was reused later, and Léon Marillier’s official EPHE report lists Lovejoy among the active auditors of his conferences on *Religions des peuples non civilisés*.[^6] In one sequence of notes, a proposed development in sacrifice is tested against an independent chronology of agriculture and domestication. What looks plausible as a developmental order produces a possible “vicious circle” once chronology is introduced, forcing the premise itself to be tested separately. Much of the case material came from the literature Lovejoy was excerpting; the local move was the chronology test. Here again, one order could not establish another: classificatory or developmental sequence was not yet historical sequence.[^6]

Lovejoy later put similar distinctions more compactly. In [“Religion and the Time-Process”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3153919) (1902), the affinity between Aristotle’s theology and Vedānta was “obvious,” but the theory of eastern influence was “entirely gratuitous” because an internal Greek route could account for the position (446–47). Comparison was not transmission.[^9]

By 1906, the choice of relation had explicit political stakes. In “Democracy in the Twentieth Century,” Lovejoy said that social-evolutionary stages and racial or national difference had split Enlightenment “generic Man” “in two directions—longitudinally and cross-wise” (94). The two axes were not equivalent: institutions fitted to one stage might certainly be unfitted to an earlier one, whereas racial or national difference did not make institutional difference necessary, and Lovejoy warned that race had become an overworked category (94–95). Yet these distinctions entered his discussion of colonial policy and political franchise. On p. 102 he regretted the previous generation’s “hasty” enfranchisement of Black Americans and sympathized with attempts to disfranchise illiterate Black voters, while withholding a final judgment on those cases (101–2). Distinguishing relations did not make their political use neutral.[^10]

By the time Lovejoy published [*The Great Chain of Being*](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL7692618M/The_Great_Chain_of_Being), Asian materials remained but had changed historical function. The preface defines the book’s principal object through “Occidental philosophy” and persistent presuppositions of “Western thought.” Buddhism and Vedānta still appear in its account of otherworldliness (30), and “primitive Buddhism” remains a comparative form (97). On p. 35 Plato becomes the principal source of an “indigenous” Occidental strain distinguished from “imported Oriental varieties.” In the notebooks of the 1890s, Buddhist and Indian materials had themselves been objects of close textual and historical inquiry; in *Great Chain*, they more often mark comparisons and boundaries within a predominantly Occidental genealogy.[^11]

Returning from the mature book to the notebooks therefore does not reveal a forgotten Buddhist origin of the history of ideas. Wilson had already preserved the early training, the 1898 article, and Paris; Schaffer later retained the Pāli and Sanskrit fact. What the notebooks add is the work inside that biography. They show Lovejoy repeatedly asking what kind of relation a piece of evidence could establish before allowing one relation to stand in for another. That recurrence does not prove a continuous method running from Buddhist studies to the mature history of ideas.

The odd first item in the *Essays* bibliography thus points back not to a hidden origin but to a more specific set of distinctions. On manuscript p. 123, temporal antecedence and logical inclusion could coexist without contradiction; in print, borrowing could coexist with an original arrangement; in 1902, resemblance could be obvious while transmission remained unproved. For the young Lovejoy, Buddhism was a field in which such relations still had to be worked out.
