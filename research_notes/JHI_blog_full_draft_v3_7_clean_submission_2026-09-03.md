# What Was Arthur Lovejoy Doing with Buddhism?

The bibliography appended to Arthur O. Lovejoy’s [*Essays in the History of Ideas*](https://www.press.jhu.edu/books/title/5183/essays-history-ideas) gives him an unlikely beginning: its first entry is an [1898 article on two Buddhist technical terms](https://dlbs.liberal.ntu.edu.tw/FULLTEXT/JR-ENG/love.htm), a piece of specialist Buddhist scholarship published decades before *The Great Chain of Being*.[^1] Lovejoy had in fact already published an essay on Lord Monboddo in 1895. Yet the bibliographical sequence poses a useful question: what was the future historian of the great chain of being trying to settle in a dispute over Buddhist terminology?

Nor was this Buddhist episode simply forgotten. Daniel J. Wilson documented Lovejoy’s comparative-religion training, his Sanskrit and Buddhist studies, and his later work in Paris; Simon Schaffer could still [note in 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/007327531004800309) that Lovejoy studied Pāli and Sanskrit there (484). The biographical fact survived; what survived less clearly was the work inside it. Among Lovejoy’s papers at Johns Hopkins is a Harvard Cooperative Society notebook labeled in his hand “Sankhya + Buddhism.” Its pages bring us into the disputes behind the published article: whether two Buddhist terms meant the same thing, how the links in a formula fitted together, and what borrowing could explain about the resulting doctrine.[^4]

Lovejoy himself named the larger problem from Auxerre on 20 October 1898. He proposed “to elucidate the theoretical presuppositions and doctrines of Buddhism from a philosophical point of view.” Philologists, he complained, had expounded Buddhist literature while caring little for philosophy; philosophers had reconstructed Buddhist conceptions “more or less out of their own heads” while neglecting “the technicalities of the texts.” He chose Sylvain Lévi for the Buddhist side of the problem; by December, when no Pāli Buddhism course was being offered, he expected instead to read Buddhist texts with Lévi privately.[^2]

One of the clearest notebook disputes begins with [Henry Clarke Warren](https://buddhadust.net/resources/pdf/warren/warren-on-p-s.pdf). Warren had warned that dependent origination should not be treated as a simple “chain of causation”: its links expressed different kinds of dependence, and each had to be examined separately (xxvii–xxx). His own treatment of *taṇhā* (desire) and *upādāna* (attachment) was more nuanced than a flat identity claim—he described attachment as the “tenacity of desire” and also reported a stage distinction before and after possession. Lovejoy nevertheless compressed that close relation into an identity problem and interrupted it: “But not so if we are to follow the present text” (“Sankhya + Buddhism,” MS p. 75). He then set four kinds of attachment beside six forms of desire and asked what different work the two terms were doing. Following Warren’s own instruction to examine each link separately thus became a reason to resist Warren’s account of this one.[^7]

A second dispute moved from the relation between links to the history of the formula itself. In [“À propos de la théorie bouddhique des douze nidânas”](https://play.google.com/store/books/details/M%C3%A9langes_Charles_de_Harlez?id=g849OKp_GrsC), Émile Senart argued that the twelve *nidānas* contained heterogeneous categories assembled from different sources; repetition and mixture could therefore be evidence of a secondary construction (284–88). Lovejoy conceded much of the premise. The terms need not all be originally Buddhist, the series is not a homogeneous temporal sequence, and materials may have been borrowed. But he refused the further inference that the resulting arrangement had thereby been shown accidental or unintelligible. Hermann Oldenberg had already challenged important parts of Senart’s semantic case in the third edition of *Buddha* (272–73, 443–55), so Lovejoy’s significance is not priority. The question left open by the proposed borrowings was what Buddhist authors had made of them: how had they arranged the parts, and what did that arrangement explain?[^7]

That distinction survives in Lovejoy’s 1898 article. There he names Senart, tests the textual witnesses Senart had cited, and says that the terms of the formula, “perhaps even the ideas,” may be largely borrowed while their “arrangement and application” remain original and characteristic (127, 132). But reading article and notebook together also shows how much was left out. The notebook ranges across Sāṃkhya-Buddhist chronology, psychological classifications, textual criticism, etymology, causation, and competing genealogies. The article is much narrower. Publication did not simply polish the notebook; it reduced the size of some of its claims. The surviving AOS record sharpens that point without giving us a simple manuscript genealogy: *Milindapañha*, *upādānakkhandha*, and *upādisesa* change documentary roles across the 1897–98 title carriers, while the mechanism of revision remains unrecovered.[^5]

Across the manuscript, the same materials are repeatedly reopened under different questions. Lovejoy asks whether two terms are identical in use, what causal work a term performs, what comes before what in time, what belongs inside what as a classification, where a doctrine or term came from, and what a borrowed element does once placed in a larger arrangement. An answer to one question does not automatically answer the others. On manuscript p. 123, for example, he places *viññāṇa* within the *nāma* side of a classification while writing beside it that “Viññāṇa is temporally (?) an antecedent of nāmarūpa, and logically a subdivision of it.” Forced onto one axis, the statements can look contradictory. On the page they answer different questions: the same term can occupy one position in a classification and another in a sequence. The question mark beside “temporally” matters. Logical inclusion is stated; temporal order is still being tested. The page is less a declaration of method than a record of unfinished sorting.[^4]

A different technical term shows what happened when this kind of unfinished sorting moved into print. In the notebook Lovejoy moves among competing etymologies of *upādisesa*, textual passages, and stronger possibilities about the term’s relation to death and Nirvāṇa. Oldenberg had already assembled much of the evidence for an ethical-residue reading, while nineteenth-century lexical literature preserved competing derivations. Lovejoy did not choose one word-history and let it decide the meaning. In the published article he makes the restriction explicit: *upādi* must be settled “rather by an examination of its use than by etymological arguments” (134). Contrary passages remain; some evidence is “relevant but not necessarily conclusive”; the matter, he concludes, “cannot be regarded as finally settled” (136). Publication here narrows the question partly by making uncertainty part of the argument.[^8]

That combination of close textual discrimination and broader comparison was already present in Lovejoy’s Harvard coursework. His individual graduate record shows that in 1896–97 he took `Sanskrit 5` and `Semitic 13`, receiving an A in both; the contemporaneous announcements identify the first as Charles Rockwell Lanman’s advanced Pāli course, “The Sacred Books of Buddhism,” reading the *Dhammapada*, *Sutta Nipāta*, and *Majjhima Nikāya*, and the second as Crawford H. Toy’s “History of the Hebrew Religion, with comparison of other Semitic religions.” Charles Carroll Everett’s comparative study of religions, including Buddhism and Hindu philosophies, occupied the same graduate environment.[^3] The point is not that Harvard supplied one unified “method.” It put close textual study, comparison among related traditions, philosophy, and comparative religion within the same institutional field.

Paris widened the objects without dissolving the problem. Notebook 005 has a strong Paris 1898–99 core but was reused later, and Léon Marillier’s official EPHE report names Lovejoy among the *auditeurs ayant pris une part active aux travaux* of his conferences on *Religions des peuples non civilisés*.[^6] Here the objects are no longer primarily Pāli terms but death, ritual, afterlife, sacrifice, and social practice. A differentiated fate after death cannot simply be called moral reward or punishment because it looks like one: the notebook tests manner of death, ritual provision, social status, kinship or group relation, taboo, and moral conduct as rival explanations. Much of that conceptual apparatus is source-owned. Lovejoy’s local work is narrower: ranking sources, reclassifying cases, rejecting proposed genealogies when chronology fails, and leaving some mechanisms unresolved. The material has changed; the inferential question remains recognizable. What does a visible difference warrant one to say caused it?

The same problem reappeared in print at a larger scale. In [“Religion and the Time-Process”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3153919) (1902), Lovejoy sent readers back to his earlier Buddhist article while discussing wider comparisons. The affinity between Aristotle’s theology and Vedānta, he wrote, was “obvious”; the theory that Aristotle therefore came under eastern influence was nevertheless “entirely gratuitous,” because the internal development of the Platonic dialectic supplied an account of the Greek position (446–47). Comparison could hold while genealogy failed. A resemblance still required a separate argument before it could become a claim of historical transmission.[^9]

By 1906, deciding which differences mattered carried explicit political stakes. In “Democracy in the Twentieth Century,” Lovejoy described social-evolutionary stages and racial or national difference as having split Enlightenment “generic Man” “in two directions—longitudinally and cross-wise” (94). Institutions fitted to one stage of culture were, he said, certainly unfitted to an earlier one; racial or national difference might make institutions inappropriate elsewhere, but he refused to say that they “must,” and he warned that race had become an overworked category (94–95). These distinctions then entered his discussion of colonial policy and political franchise. On p. 102 he regretted the previous generation’s “hasty” enfranchisement of Black Americans and sympathized with attempts to disfranchise illiterate Black voters, while withholding a final judgment on those cases (101–2). Finer differentiation here became part of an argument about who was fitted for what institutions and under what conditions.[^10]

The same year also blocks a tidy maturation story. In [“Kant’s Antithesis of Dogmatism and Criticism”](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/XV.58.191), Lovejoy tested a broad inherited contrast against local propositions and rejected the classification when it failed. Reviewing Paul Elmer More’s *Shelburne Essays*, he returned to the *Sutta Nipāta* to resist an over-positive account of Nirvāṇa. Yet in [“The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy”](https://doi.org/10.5840/monist190616324) he moved in the opposite direction, gathering heterogeneous local reports into a broad comparative claim about “primitive philosophy.” Decomposition and generalization were contemporaneous possibilities, not successive stages.[^9]

By the time Lovejoy published [*The Great Chain of Being*](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL7692618M/The_Great_Chain_of_Being), Asian materials remained but had changed historical function. The preface defines the book’s principal object through “Occidental philosophy” and persistent presuppositions of “Western thought.” Buddhism and Vedānta still appear in its account of otherworldliness (30), “primitive Buddhism” recurs as a comparative form (97), and at p. 35 Plato becomes the principal source of an “indigenous” Occidental strain distinguished from “imported Oriental varieties.” In the notebooks of the 1890s, Buddhist and Indian materials had themselves been close textual and historical problems; in *Great Chain*, they more often mark comparisons and boundaries within a predominantly Occidental genealogy. What changed was not simply their presence but the work they were asked to do.[^11]

Returning from the mature book to the notebooks therefore does not reveal a forgotten Buddhist origin of the history of ideas. Wilson had already preserved the early training, the 1898 article, and Paris; Schaffer later retained the Pāli and Sanskrit fact while organizing Lovejoy around evolution, hierarchy, seriality, and *Great Chain*. The formation was known. The notebooks change its evidentiary status: they turn a biographical episode into scholarly work that can be reconstructed in its own disputes, limits, and unfinished judgments.

The odd first item in the *Essays* bibliography thus looks different at the end. A scholarly career is arranged more than once: notes arrange materials into working problems; publication narrows some of them into defensible claims; mature books rearrange earlier materials around new narrative centers; later bibliographies and disciplines make some of those arrangements easier to see than others. The first bibliographical entry now points back not to a hidden origin but to unfinished disputes: Warren’s account of desire and attachment, Senart’s reconstruction of the formula, and the passages that kept *upādisesa* open. For the young Lovejoy, Buddhism was a field in which historical judgments still had to be worked out.
