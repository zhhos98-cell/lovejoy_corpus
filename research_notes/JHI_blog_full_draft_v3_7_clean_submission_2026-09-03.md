# What Was Arthur Lovejoy Doing with Buddhism?

The bibliography appended to Arthur O. Lovejoy’s [*Essays in the History of Ideas*](https://www.press.jhu.edu/books/title/5183/essays-history-ideas) opens with an unlikely Lovejoy.[^1] Its first entry is an [1898 article on two Buddhist technical terms](https://dlbs.liberal.ntu.edu.tw/FULLTEXT/JR-ENG/love.htm), a piece of specialist Buddhist scholarship standing before the later Lovejoy of unit ideas and *The Great Chain of Being*. The bibliography spans 1898–1948, and Lovejoy had already published an essay on Lord Monboddo in 1895.[^1] Its cutoff nevertheless produces a striking retrospective arrangement: the later career begins on the page with Buddhist technical terms.

Writing from Auxerre on 20 October 1898, Lovejoy proposed “to elucidate the theoretical presuppositions and doctrines of Buddhism from a philosophical point of view.” Philologists, he complained, had expounded Buddhist literature while caring little for philosophy; philosophers had reconstructed Buddhist conceptions “more or less out of their own heads” while neglecting “the technicalities of the texts.” He chose Sylvain Lévi for the Buddhist side of that problem; by December, when no Pāli Buddhism course was being offered, he expected instead to read Buddhist texts with Lévi privately.[^2]

The scholarly tasks Lovejoy later contrasted were already concretely adjacent at Harvard. In 1896–97 he took `Sanskrit 5` and `Semitic 13`, receiving an A in both. The course announcements identify the first as Charles Rockwell Lanman’s advanced Pāli course, “The Sacred Books of Buddhism,” reading the *Dhammapada*, *Sutta Nipāta*, and *Majjhima Nikāya*, and the second as Crawford H. Toy’s “History of the Hebrew Religion, with comparison of other Semitic religions.” Charles Carroll Everett’s comparative study of religion, including Buddhism and Hindu philosophies, occupied the same graduate environment.[^5] Textual study, comparative religion, and philosophy sat close together while remaining distinct scholarly tasks. The Paris letter later names the friction from inside his own work.

Among Lovejoy’s papers at Johns Hopkins is a Harvard Cooperative Society notebook labeled in his hand “Sankhya + Buddhism.”[^3] The notebook is a working surface. Warren, Rhys Davids, Childers, Oldenberg, Senart, lexical notes, diagrams, objections, and short interventions accumulate on the same pages. Its interest lies at the joints—where Lovejoy stops copying, redirects a relation, or tests what a source’s proposition will bear. Early in the notebook he states the scale of the problem in unusually broad terms: the “general philosophical position” of Buddhism has to be understood through the technical working-out and “very complicated inter-relation” of the twelve *nidānas*, by “a careful analysis (i.e. a comparison of texts).” At this point the working object is the interrelation of the *nidāna* system itself.

[Henry Clarke Warren](https://buddhadust.net/resources/pdf/warren/warren-on-p-s.pdf) supplied one of the problems. Warren had warned that dependent origination should not be treated as a simple “chain of causation”: different links expressed different kinds of dependence and had to be examined separately (xxvii). Yet when he reached *taṇhā* (desire) and *upādāna* (attachment), he treated their relation as one of identity. Lovejoy copied the judgment and interrupted it: “But not so if we are to follow the present text” (“Sankhya + Buddhism,” MS p. 75). He then set four kinds of attachment beside six forms of desire and asked what different work the two terms were doing.

Émile Senart supplied a second pressure point. In [“À propos de la théorie bouddhique des douze nidânas”](https://play.google.com/store/books/details/M%C3%A9langes_Charles_de_Harlez?id=g849OKp_GrsC), Senart argued that the twelve *nidānas* contained heterogeneous categories assembled from different sources; repetition and mixture could therefore indicate secondary construction (284–88). Lovejoy accepts much of the compositeness problem and refuses Senart’s next move. At the point where Senart’s *ordre plus ou moins accidentel* is under discussion, he writes that the repetitions are intentional and that a “discoverable logical system” governs the arrangement. Borrowing and intelligibility remain separate questions.

The sharper version appears on manuscript p. 123: “Viññāṇa is temporally (?) an antecedent of nāmarūpa, and logically a subdivision of it.” The page also nests *viññāṇa* within the *nāma* side of a classification. The question mark is useful. Temporal priority and logical inclusion occupy the same working sheet as distinct orders: the same term stands in one place in a sequence and another in a classification. A similar separation appears when Lovejoy asks whether *upādāna* is the cause of the fact of continued existence while *karma* is the cause of its manner. That distinction would later become one of the clearest sentences in the 1898 article: karma determines the particular condition of rebirth, while *upādāna* determines rebirth “in itself.”

The path into print shows the object changing rather than merely shrinking. An American Oriental Society circular of 10 April 1897 announced “Critical summary of the argument of the Milinda-pañha.” By the Baltimore meeting two weeks later, Lovejoy was absent and the communication read by title had become, at the level recoverable from the Proceedings, “On the meaning of the Buddhist technical terms upādāna and upādāna-kkhandhā.” In 1898 the published pair changed again: “upādāna and upādisesa.”[^4] The middle term matters. *Upādānakkhandha* moves from title-level object into the Senart controversy inside the first section, while *upādisesa* rises into the title and the second section. In the published article, the *Milindapañha* appears as evidence rather than as the title-level organizing object. These are changes in what the paper is asking each source and term to do.

The notebook also catches Lovejoy drawing a publication boundary. After a broader discussion of Nirvāṇa he writes, “This is all that is required for the purposes of my paper.” Around the sentence sits much more than the printed article eventually carries. A substantial Sāṃkhya/Buddhism chronology branch disappears. A long *khandha–nidāna* worksheet becomes a published remark that the analysis cannot be reviewed there. The notebook’s “Original Elements / Derived Elements” allocation is muted. The article keeps relations that bear directly on the two technical-term problems and sheds much of the historical scaffolding around them. The printed ending narrows the claim further: after arguing for an ethical reading of *upādisesa*, Lovejoy leaves contrary passages standing and says the matter cannot be regarded as “finally settled” (136).

A second notebook changes the scale. Notebook 005 has a strong Paris 1898–99 core but was reused later; its scan pages can contain host-page prose, inserted sheets, folded slips, cancellations, and numbered schemes.[^6] Marillier, Robertson Smith, Frazer, Tylor, Trumbull, Mannhardt and other carriers already supply much of the vocabulary around sacrifice, blood, fetish, magic, afterlife, agrarian rites, and transferable efficacy. What Lovejoy repeatedly does with that material is rearrange the relation among mechanism, bearer, classification, and history. A case can be recast as alimentary rather than ritual; a proposed mechanism can survive only if some socially competent bearer can actually perform it; Grout’s case can be retained while a deferred-payment explanation is rejected; circumcision can resemble sacrificial substitution while the proposed substitute-human-sacrifice genealogy fails.

Chronology becomes especially disruptive. On one set of pages, an attractive ritual genealogy is tested against external dates and the classificatory order ceases to function as its own history. Elsewhere two rival mechanisms remain open because the evidence will not force a choice. A moralized afterlife can sit on top of older journey machinery; sacrifice can fuse components with different historical paths; ancestor cult, local cult, nature cult, and high-god material can inhabit one field at different temporal addresses. The notebook keeps producing present configurations whose components are differently dated, differently functioning, and differently genealogized.

The notebook’s later reuse makes a second temporal grammar especially visible. Elsewhere in 005, *primitive* and “higher stage of culture” can operate within a civilizational ranking; in a 1905 teaching outline Lovejoy writes “Greek primitive” and “N.T. intermediate.” The first grammar can rank populations or cultures on a developmental scale; the second cuts vertically within a tradition. Notebook 005 therefore carries both external staging and internal temporalization.

Later publications place related distinctions at different scales. In [“Religion and the Time-Process”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3153919) (1902), Lovejoy called the affinity between Aristotle’s theology and Vedānta “obvious” while treating eastern influence as “entirely gratuitous” when an internal Greek route was available (446–47). Resemblance could be retained after genealogy was refused.

The scale contrast becomes sharp in 1906. “Democracy in the Twentieth Century” divided Enlightenment “generic Man” longitudinally by social-evolutionary stage and cross-wise by race or nationality, then carried those classifications into colonial and franchise judgments, including Black enfranchisement and disfranchisement. In [“The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy”](https://doi.org/10.5840/monist190616324), impersonal, communicable, quasi-mechanical efficacy was abstracted across heterogeneous materials and presented as a “fundamental concept” of “primitive philosophy” and “savage thought.” The notebooks test relations inside local problems; the 1906 article assembles a relation across heterogeneous fields into a broad explanatory concept, while developmental classification elsewhere enters direct political judgment.

[*The Great Chain of Being*](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL7692618M/The_Great_Chain_of_Being) gives Asian materials another change of address. Its principal historical object is framed through “Occidental philosophy” and “Western thought”; Buddhism and Vedānta remain visible, while “primitive Buddhism” appears as a comparative form. Buddhist and Indian materials that had appeared as local textual, semantic, causal, and genealogical problems in the 1890s later more often mark types or boundaries within a predominantly Occidental genealogy.

The 1898 article is one recomposed extraction from this broader field. Across the AOS announcements and the published article, title-level objects are promoted, demoted, and reallocated; within notebook 004, large branches are marked off at the boundary of what is “required for the purposes of my paper.” Print preserves the two-term argument while much of the surrounding relation-work remains outside it.

Across that larger field, a term could be temporally prior and logically contained; a classificatory order could fail as chronology; a mechanism could remain plausible while its proposed bearer failed the test; a resemblance could persist after a genealogy collapsed; a “primitive” layer could cut vertically within a tradition while “higher stage” language ranked populations or cultures on a developmental scale. These relations did not line up on one axis. Before the unit idea became a declared historical instrument, Lovejoy was already working among objects that would not stay in a single order.
