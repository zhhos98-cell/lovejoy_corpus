The bibliography appended to Arthur O. Lovejoy’s [*Essays in the History of Ideas*](https://www.press.jhu.edu/books/title/5183/essays-history-ideas) gives him an unlikely beginning. Its first entry is an [1898 article on two Buddhist technical terms](https://dlbs.liberal.ntu.edu.tw/FULLTEXT/JR-ENG/love.htm), a piece of specialist Buddhist scholarship published decades before *The Great Chain of Being*.[^1] Lovejoy had in fact already published an essay on Lord Monboddo in 1895. Still, the entry leaves a question worth pursuing: what was the future historian of the great chain of being trying to settle in a dispute over Buddhist terminology?

Nor was this Buddhist episode simply forgotten. Daniel J. Wilson documented Lovejoy’s comparative-religion training, his Sanskrit and Buddhist studies, and his later work in Paris; Simon Schaffer could still [note in 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/007327531004800309) that Lovejoy studied Pāli and Sanskrit there (484). The biographical fact survived. What survived less clearly was the work inside the fact. Among Lovejoy’s papers at Johns Hopkins is a Harvard Cooperative Society notebook labeled in his hand “Sankhya + Buddhism.” Its pages bring us into the disputes behind the published article: whether two Buddhist terms meant the same thing, how the links in a formula fitted together, and what borrowing could explain about the resulting doctrine.[^4]

Writing from Auxerre on 20 October 1898, Lovejoy put the larger difficulty plainly. He proposed “to elucidate the theoretical presuppositions and doctrines of Buddhism from a philosophical point of view.” Philologists, he complained, had expounded Buddhist literature while caring little for philosophy; philosophers had reconstructed Buddhist conceptions “more or less out of their own heads” while neglecting “the technicalities of the texts.” He chose Sylvain Lévi for the Buddhist side of the problem; by December, when no Pāli Buddhism course was being offered, he expected instead to read Buddhist texts with Lévi privately.[^2]

One of the clearest notebook disputes begins with [Henry Clarke Warren](https://buddhadust.net/resources/pdf/warren/warren-on-p-s.pdf). Warren had warned that dependent origination should not be treated as a simple “chain of causation”: its links expressed different kinds of dependence, and each had to be examined separately (xxvii–xxx). His own treatment of *taṇhā* (desire) and *upādāna* (attachment) was more nuanced than a flat identity claim—he described attachment as the “tenacity of desire” and also reported a stage distinction before and after possession. Lovejoy nevertheless compressed that close relation into an identity problem and interrupted it: “But not so if we are to follow the present text” (“Sankhya + Buddhism,” MS p. 75). He then set four kinds of attachment beside six forms of desire and asked what different work the two terms were doing. Warren supplied both the warning and the relation that Lovejoy now pressed harder: following the links separately brought him into disagreement with his guide.[^7]

The relation between the links also mattered to Émile Senart’s account of how the whole formula had been put together. In [“À propos de la théorie bouddhique des douze nidânas”](https://play.google.com/store/books/details/M%C3%A9langes_Charles_de_Harlez?id=g849OKp_GrsC), Senart argued that the twelve *nidānas* contained heterogeneous categories assembled from different sources; repetition and mixture could therefore be evidence of a secondary construction (284–88). Lovejoy conceded much of the premise. The terms need not all be originally Buddhist, the series is not a homogeneous temporal sequence, and materials may have been borrowed. But he refused the further inference that the resulting arrangement had thereby been shown accidental or unintelligible. Hermann Oldenberg had already challenged important parts of Senart’s semantic case in the third edition of *Buddha* (272–73, 443–55), so Lovejoy’s significance is not priority. The question left open by the proposed borrowings was what Buddhist authors had made of them: how had they arranged the parts, and what did that arrangement explain?[^7]

That distinction survives in Lovejoy’s 1898 article. There he names Senart, tests the textual witnesses Senart had cited, and says that the terms of the formula, “perhaps even the ideas,” may be largely borrowed while their “arrangement and application” remain original and characteristic (127, 132). But reading article and notebook together also shows how much was left out. The notebook ranges across Sāṃkhya-Buddhist chronology, psychological classifications, textual criticism, etymology, causation, and competing genealogies. The article is much narrower. Publication did not simply polish the notebook; it reduced the size of some of its claims. The surviving AOS record sharpens that point without giving us a simple manuscript genealogy: *Milindapañha*, *upādānakkhandha*, and *upādisesa* change documentary roles across the 1897–98 title carriers, while the mechanism of revision remains unrecovered.[^5]

Across the manuscript, the same materials are repeatedly reopened under different questions. Lovejoy asks whether two terms are identical in use, what causal work a term performs, what comes before what in time, what belongs inside what as a classification, where a doctrine or term came from, and what a borrowed element does once placed in a larger arrangement. An answer to one question does not automatically answer the others. One page makes this especially visible. On manuscript p. 123, Lovejoy places *viññāṇa* within the *nāma* side of a classification while writing beside it that “Viññāṇa is temporally (?) an antecedent of nāmarūpa, and logically a subdivision of it.” Forced onto one axis, the statements can look contradictory. On the page they answer different questions. The same term can occupy one position in a classification and another in a sequence. The question mark beside “temporally” keeps one relation tentative even as Lovejoy specifies the other. A working sheet gives us a particularly close view of what still had to be settled before these notes could become a published argument.[^4]

The treatment of *upādisesa* shows most clearly what happened when such working problems entered print. In the notebook Lovejoy moves among competing etymologies, textual passages, and stronger possibilities about the term’s relation to death and Nirvāṇa. Oldenberg had already assembled much of the evidence for an ethical-residue reading, while nineteenth-century lexical literature preserved competing derivations. Lovejoy did not let one word-history settle the meaning. The published article explicitly limits what etymology can establish: the meaning of *upādi*, he writes, must be settled “rather by an examination of its use than by etymological arguments” (134). He retains contrary passages, calls some evidence “relevant but not necessarily conclusive,” and ends by saying that the matter “cannot be regarded as finally settled” (136). Readers were given the competing passages alongside an explicit account of how far Lovejoy thought they could carry the argument.[^8]

The range of the notebook’s questions becomes easier to understand in the Harvard setting where Lovejoy studied. His individual graduate record shows that in 1896–97 he took `Sanskrit 5` and `Semitic 13`, receiving an A in both; the contemporaneous announcements identify the first as Charles Rockwell Lanman’s advanced Pāli course, “The Sacred Books of Buddhism,” reading the *Dhammapada*, *Sutta Nipāta*, and *Majjhima Nikāya*, and the second as Crawford H. Toy’s “History of the Hebrew Religion, with comparison of other Semitic religions.” Charles Carroll Everett’s comparative study of religions, including Buddhism and Hindu philosophies, occupied the same graduate environment.[^3] Harvard did not transmit one unified “method.” It placed close textual study, comparison among related traditions, philosophy, and comparative religion within the same institutional field.

In Paris, the comparative side of this training comes to the fore in a second notebook. Notebook 005 has a strong Paris 1898–99 core but was reused later, and Léon Marillier’s official EPHE report names Lovejoy among the *auditeurs ayant pris une part active aux travaux* of his conferences on *Religions des peuples non civilisés*.[^6] The objects are no longer primarily Pāli terms but death, ritual, afterlife, sacrifice, and social practice. A differentiated fate after death cannot simply be called moral reward or punishment because it looks like one: the notebook tests other possible explanations, including manner of death, ritual provision, social status, kinship or group relation, taboo, and moral conduct. Much of the conceptual apparatus is source-owned. What survives as Lovejoy-local is more limited and more useful: ranking sources, reclassifying cases, rejecting proposed genealogies when chronology fails, and leaving some rival mechanisms unresolved. Before treating an unequal afterlife as evidence of moral judgment, he had to consider what ritual, status, or the circumstances of death might explain.

The question of what comparison could establish also followed Lovejoy into print. In [“Religion and the Time-Process”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3153919) (1902), he sent readers back to his earlier Buddhist article while discussing wider comparisons. The affinity between Aristotle’s theology and Vedānta, he wrote, was “obvious”; the theory that Aristotle therefore came under eastern influence was nevertheless “entirely gratuitous,” because the internal development of the Platonic dialectic supplied an account of the Greek position (446–47). Resemblance did not have to become genealogy. The two theologies could illuminate each other while their histories called for separate explanations. As in the Buddhist dispute, identifying a connection between ideas left further work to do on the kind of connection it was.[^9]

By 1906, deciding which differences mattered also carried explicit political stakes in Lovejoy’s writing. In “Democracy in the Twentieth Century,” he described the conjunction of social-evolutionary stages and racial or national difference as having split Enlightenment “generic Man” “in two directions—longitudinally and cross-wise” (94). The two axes were not equivalent. Institutions fitted to one stage of culture were, he said, certainly unfitted to an earlier one; racial or national difference might make institutions inappropriate elsewhere, but he explicitly refused to say that they “must” (94–95). He also warned that race had become an overworked category. Yet these distinctions had political consequences. In the closing pages they entered his discussion of colonial policy and political franchise; on p. 102 he regretted the previous generation’s “hasty” enfranchisement of Black Americans and sympathized with attempts to disfranchise illiterate Black voters, while withholding a final judgment on those specific cases (101–2). Historical differentiation here was not a politically neutral preference for finer distinctions. A more differentiated description of humanity could become part of an argument about who was fitted for what institutions and under what conditions.[^10]

Yet even in 1906, the scale on which Lovejoy grouped his materials remained unsettled. In [“Kant’s Antithesis of Dogmatism and Criticism”](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/XV.58.191), he tested a broad inherited contrast against local propositions and rejected the larger classification when it failed. Reviewing Paul Elmer More’s *Shelburne Essays*, he returned to the *Sutta Nipāta*—the same Buddhist text prominent in his 1898 argument—to resist an over-positive account of Nirvāṇa. Yet in [“The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy”](https://doi.org/10.5840/monist190616324) he moved in the opposite direction, gathering heterogeneous local reports into a broad comparative claim about “primitive philosophy.” In the same year Lovejoy could break down an over-broad historical category and construct broad categories of his own. Decomposition and generalization were contemporaneous possibilities, not successive stages in a tidy intellectual maturation.[^9]

By the time Lovejoy published [*The Great Chain of Being*](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL7692618M/The_Great_Chain_of_Being), Asian materials had not disappeared, but they occupied a different place. The preface defines the book’s principal historical object through “Occidental philosophy” and persistent presuppositions of “Western thought.” Buddhism and Vedānta remain visible in its account of otherworldliness (30), and “primitive Buddhism” recurs as a comparative form (97). At p. 35 Lovejoy calls Plato the principal source of the “indigenous” Occidental strain of otherworldliness while distinguishing it from “imported Oriental varieties.” The narrative center is therefore Western without the Asian material simply vanishing. In the notebooks of the 1890s, Buddhist and Indian materials had themselves been close textual and historical problems; in *Great Chain*, they more often serve as comparisons within a predominantly Occidental genealogy. The close textual disputes that had once occupied his notebook now sit outside the main story the book asks its readers to follow.[^11]

Returning from the mature book to the notebooks therefore does not reveal a forgotten Buddhist origin of the history of ideas. Wilson had already preserved the early training, the 1898 article, and Paris; Schaffer could preserve the Pāli and Sanskrit fact while telling a different story about Lovejoy through evolution, hierarchy, seriality, and *Great Chain*. The formation was known but differently classified. What the notebooks alter is its evidentiary status: they let us reconstruct the scholarly work performed inside a biographical episode that later histories could mention without making it analytically central.

The odd first item in the *Essays* bibliography thus looks different at the end. A scholarly career is arranged more than once. Notes arrange materials into working problems; publication narrows some of those problems into claims an author is prepared to defend; mature books rearrange earlier materials around new narrative centers; later bibliographies and disciplines decide which of those arrangements become easiest to see. The first bibliographical entry now leads back to a specific set of unfinished disputes: Warren’s relation between two terms, Senart’s reconstruction of a formula, and the passages that kept the meaning of *upādisesa* open. For the young Lovejoy, Buddhism was a field in which such historical judgments still had to be worked out.
