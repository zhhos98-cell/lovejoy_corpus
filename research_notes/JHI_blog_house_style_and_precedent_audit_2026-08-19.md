# JHI Blog precedent / house-style audit for the Lovejoy 004 pitch

Date: 2026-08-19
Status: active editorial note

## Bottom line

The proposed Lovejoy piece fits an existing JHI Blog mode very well: a vivid first-order historical or archival puzzle that quickly opens onto a methodological or disciplinary problem. The Blog explicitly seeks well-researched think pieces of c. 2,000 words, including new research and scholarly controversies, and welcomes novel/off-beat proposals.

However, there is one important recent-overlap risk: in February 2026 the Blog published Bhadrajee Hewage, `The Bookseller and the Buddha: Error, Empire, and the Problem of Origins`, a substantial essay on early nineteenth-century Buddhist Orientalism, the professionalization of Indology, colonial knowledge infrastructures, and changing regimes of evidence. A Lovejoy pitch should therefore NOT sound like another essay about `Buddhism + Orientalism + origins + rise of scientific Indology`.

Our differentiation should be explicit:

- object: Lovejoy's archival notebook + his 1898 intervention;
- historical problem: a live 1890s Sāṃkhya/Buddhism controversy over what comparison could prove;
- larger payoff: disciplinary self-history / methodological prehistory of intellectual history and history of ideas;
- Buddhism functions as the concrete evidentiary scene, not the substantive destination of the essay.

## Closest stylistic precedents

### 1. Disha Karnad Jani, `On the Aggregate in Intellectual History` (2026)

Very close structural precedent for our desired writing.

Opening move:
- begins with a memorable anecdote about Deborah Valenze and Eric Hobsbawm;
- uses that one first-order scene to introduce a larger methodological genealogy;
- does not begin with abstract methodological literature;
- after the hook, states a concise first-person historiographical claim (`I offer here one account... I suggest...`);
- spends most of the essay historicizing a methodological development through concrete historical actors and disciplinary debates.

Lesson for Lovejoy:
- `Skinner → weird Lovejoy notebook` can perform the same function as `Hobsbawm → hundred women preachers`;
- move from anecdote/object to a historical account of a methodological problem, not a theoretical manifesto.

### 2. Shireen Hamza, `Annulling the Marriage of Two Men: A Marginal Note in a Yemeni Manuscript` (2020)

Strong precedent for the archival-oddity cold open.

Opening move:
- first person, scene-setting archive narrative;
- one surprising marginal note creates the question;
- physical/source detail establishes why the object is evidentially strange;
- the larger interpretive stakes emerge only after the reader has been made to care about the object.

Lesson:
- the Lovejoy notebook itself can be introduced materially and narratively before explaining the full controversy;
- do not front-load bibliography or historiography.

### 3. Daniela Helbig, `Ruminant Machines: a twentieth-century episode in the material history of ideas` (2019)

Strong precedent for notebook/material practice → history-of-ideas methodology.

Opening move:
- starts from Lichtenberg's notebooks and note-taking practice;
- asks how material writing tools participate in thought;
- uses a concrete documentary practice to cross from history of ideas into a methodological conversation with media theory.

Lesson:
- JHI Blog is comfortable with a physical notebook serving as an argument about how intellectual history itself should understand ideas/method.

### 4. Lea Cantor and Jonathan Egid, `Looking for an Ethiopian Lucretius` (2026)

Strong precedent for `specific nineteenth-century Orientalist controversy + new archival evidence + larger historiographical stakes`.

Opening move:
- immediately identifies the controversy and its received chronology;
- states exactly what new archive changes;
- then narrates research process and documentary discovery;
- maintains a clear difference between what archive establishes and what broader historiographical story it changes.

Lesson:
- our 004/1898 proof should be equally explicit and bounded;
- but unlike this companion piece, ours should not become a Quellenforschung appendix.

### 5. `Towards a Great Pluralism: Quentin Skinner at Ertegun House` (2017)

Useful precedent that JHI Blog readers/editors are comfortable with explicit Skinner/history-of-ideas self-reflection. The piece treats Skinner's distinction between intellectual history and history of ideas as a live disciplinary question rather than only secondary historiography.

Lesson:
- opening with Skinner is legible to the venue;
- avoid a long exegesis of 1969; Skinner should be a framing device.

### 6. `Can There Be a Global Intellectual History of Caste?` (2020)

Useful precedent for first-order non-Western archive → methodological critique of intellectual history. The author uses vernacular South Asian archives to argue back into global intellectual-history methodology.

Lesson:
- the Blog accepts methodological arguments generated from South Asian intellectual material rather than treating `method` as exclusively Euro-American metahistory;
- our Bankim/Mitra/transregional proof-problem correction therefore fits the venue, but must stay proportionate.

## The most important near-overlap: Hewage 2026

`The Bookseller and the Buddha: Error, Empire, and the Problem of Origins` is very close in topical vocabulary.

It already does:
- Buddhist Orientalism;
- nineteenth-century transformation of regimes of evidence;
- amateur/speculative versus professional/philological Indology;
- colonial translation and intermediary infrastructures;
- geography/origins as a historical-knowledge problem;
- methodological discontinuity that turns out to be less clean than disciplinary memory suggests.

Therefore our essay should avoid the following pitch language:
- `the origins of Buddhist studies`;
- `the making/professionalization of Indology` as main claim;
- `how Orientalism became scientific`;
- generic `colonial knowledge produced Buddhism` framing;
- `problem of origins` in title/subtitle.

Our piece is distinct if centered on:

> **the future founder of the history of ideas participating in a dispute over levels of historical inference, and what that changes about the disciplinary genealogy of intellectual history itself.**

In other words, Hewage moves:

`an erroneous Buddha geography → regimes of Orientalist knowledge`.

We move:

`a strange Lovejoy notebook → a live proof controversy → forgotten methodological prehistory of history of ideas`.

This distinction should be visible in the pitch by sentence 2 or 3.

## Current JHI Blog house style

Official guidance:
- think pieces around 2,000 words including citations/endnotes;
- new research, scholarly controversies, and think pieces are explicitly welcome;
- `novel and off-beat` proposals are explicitly invited;
- standard American English / Chicago style;
- citations normally in-text/parenthetical, including page number where available;
- online sources should be hyperlinked on first citation;
- endnotes only when useful for clarification/secondary bibliography;
- at least one image, preferably public-domain.

Observed prose tendencies in successful examples:

1. **Cold open before apparatus.** Anecdote, surprising source, historical claim, or strong conceptual puzzle comes first.
2. **Intervention by paragraph 2–4.** They usually tell the reader what the case changes relatively early.
3. **First-order nouns dominate.** People, books, manuscripts, institutions, controversies. Methodological abstractions are attached to these rather than floated independently.
4. **Selective specialist detail.** Foreign-language terms are allowed but only when doing argumentative work.
5. **Explicit pivot sentences.** Common pattern: `Yet...`, `The point is...`, `What this reveals...`, followed by the wider payoff.
6. **Large argument, narrow carrier.** A small case is routinely allowed to raise a field-level question if the causal/evidentiary bridge is visible.
7. **No need to conceal first person.** `I argue`, `I suggest`, or archive-discovery narrative is normal, though not mandatory.
8. **Section headings are acceptable**, but 3–5 substantive movements are better than a mini-journal article with many technical subsections.

## Recommended house-style adaptation for our draft

### Opening 180–220 words

Skinner should occupy 2–4 sentences only.

Then physical/archive cut:

`Among Lovejoy's papers at Johns Hopkins is a notebook labelled “Sankhya + Buddhism.”`

Explain why this is stranger than it sounds: not an isolated youthful curiosity, but a working dossier tied to his 1898 technical publication and a live international controversy.

By the end of paragraph 2, state the puzzle:

> Why was the future founder of history of ideas learning to make historical arguments inside a controversy over the evidentiary limits of comparison?

### Middle

Use only one controversy and one or two manuscript moves. The ideal sequence:

- Garbe diagnostic similarity;
- opposing/limiting positions (Rhys Davids / Senart / Oldenberg compressed);
- Lovejoy's scale distinction (`element/doctrine` versus `system as a whole`);
- 1898 publication as public proof of participation.

Then leave Buddhism.

### Widening

Müller's classical/Oriental binary + Harvard Greenough/Lanman overlap shows disciplinary differentiation within philology rather than sealed camps.

Bankim/Mitra can appear in a compact paragraph only if needed to prevent a Europe-method / India-data model.

### Close

Return to Skinner, but make the object of contextualization Lovejoy's formation and the discipline's self-history.

Do not end on `Skinner was wrong`.

Possible tonal endpoint:

> Contextualizing Lovejoy changes the context of the history of ideas' own origin story.

The `chirality/mirror rotation` idea is best retained as an optional final afterimage or separate later essay; it risks stealing the ending from the stronger archival/transregional claim if made too explicit here.

## Editorial verdict

**Fit: high.** The venue has published multiple pieces that move from a surprising first-order object or anecdote to a methodological question in intellectual history.

**Novelty risk: manageable but real.** The February 2026 Hewage essay makes `Buddhism + Orientalism + history of evidence` a recently used topical package. The Lovejoy pitch should therefore advertise itself as a history-of-intellectual-history / methodological genealogy piece from the beginning.

**Best stylistic models:** `On the Aggregate in Intellectual History` for architecture; `Annulling the Marriage of Two Men` for archival cold open; `Ruminant Machines` for notebook → methodology; `Looking for an Ethiopian Lucretius` for bounded archival revision; `Can There Be a Global Intellectual History of Caste?` for South Asia → methodological reflexivity.
