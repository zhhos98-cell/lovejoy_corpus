# Batch 69 — Source criticism is not enough: Jarvis, Keating, Brinton, Marshall/Breeks, and 005’s question-relative evidentiary license

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced
Scope: 005 only; follow the corrected Keating collation in Batch 68 by asking what, if anything, is distinctive about Lovejoy’s source criticism when his own source literature was already source-critical.

## Core result

The strongest historiographic claim has shifted again.

It is no longer defensible to make `source criticism` itself the distinctive Paris/Lovejoy innovation. Three period controls now show that source weighting, contamination awareness and criticism of preconceived theories were already built into the comparative literature around 005:

- **Keating (1824)** explicitly distinguishes reports according to missionary contact and warns that borrowed Christian notions make original doctrine difficult to recover;
- **Jarvis (1819/1820)** warns that reports of Native religion are distorted by observers’ modes of thinking and preconceived theories, yet still advances very strong claims about universal retributive immortality and primeval religious unity;
- **Brinton (1868/1876/1896)** explicitly says comparative American mythology depends on careful selection of authorities and assigning them relative weight, while criticizing Jarvis for forcing the evidence into a primitive-civilization / degeneration scheme.

The Toda/Nilgiri cluster on 005 PDF p.26 adds a fourth control: Lovejoy himself writes that the relevant authorities are poor/weak while still retaining Marshall and Breeks in the working comparison. A contemporary review by E. B. Tylor likewise separates Marshall’s ethnological contribution from theoretical material unlikely to command assent.

The consequential Lovejoy operation is therefore more specific:

> **source criticism → question-relative evidentiary license.**

A source can be admissible for one task and weak for another. A datum can enter a descriptive classification without proving common mechanism; a recurring form can be genuine without proving common origin; a contaminated report can preserve usable material without carrying a developmental conclusion; a possible transmission route can remain historically possible without becoming evidence.

This is the same inferential structure N. W. Thomas later makes explicit when he says classificatory sections are of unequal value as evidence for a historical proposition.

## 1. Keating 1824: the source Lovejoy reads already contains the missionary-contact critique

Direct primary collation now fixes Lovejoy’s p.16 reference as **Keating, vol. I, p.393**, not p.343.

Keating says Dakota future-state beliefs may be received with less diffidence because the Dakotas had had little intercourse with missionaries, while immediately acknowledging that Christian doctrines had probably crept into some credences. The following material includes a land of souls, punishment, reunion, hunting/planting, chastity, infanticide, suicide and manner-of-death distinctions.

Lovejoy’s p.16–18 sequence is therefore not merely using Keating as raw ethnography. It is reading an ethnographic author who has already placed a reliability warning inside the report.

The difference lies downstream. Lovejoy refuses to let the reported distribution of good/bad destinations simply establish `moral eschatology`; he asks whether apparently moral penalties are instead produced by:

- manner of death;
- ritual or taboo;
- clan/social obligation;
- borrowed Christian elements.

The source-critical question is inherited from the source; the **redistribution of explanatory burden** is Lovejoy’s working operation.

---

## 2. Keating pp.111–112 gives an even cleaner counterexample to a priority claim

In his Potawatomi discussion Keating explicitly says that the time for correct information has passed: missionary notions and intercourse with white people have made it difficult to distinguish genuine/original doctrine from later material.

Yet just before this warning he still argues that belief in the soul and a future state of reward and punishment probably preceded white contact. His warrant includes both general prevalence and the uniform judgment of observers thought especially conversant with Indigenous life.

Keating thus combines:

1. awareness of contamination;
2. explicit source discrimination;
3. recovery of a pre-contact stratum;
4. a fairly strong general historical conclusion.

This is crucial for the paper. **Knowing that a source is contaminated does not by itself determine how much historical inference one may draw from it.**

---

## 3. Jarvis 1819/1820: source-bias criticism plus universal retributive immortality

Samuel Farmar Jarvis delivered *A Discourse on the Religion of the Indian Tribes of North America* to the New-York Historical Society on 20 December 1819; it appeared in print in 1820.

Jarvis begins with unusually explicit source criticism. He says that observers’ modes of thinking have tinged what they report; speculation about the peopling of America produced misrepresentations and imaginary affinities; and factual relations could be perverted by attachment to preconceived theories. He therefore warns readers to approach writings on Native religion with critical caution.

But the same discourse later reaches extremely strong synthetic conclusions. Jarvis says, in substance, that no nation had been found lacking the general principle of **retributive immortality**, and later says the tribes agree without exception on a future state of retribution for deeds done in the body.

The conjunction is analytically valuable:

> **reporter-bias criticism + anti-preconception rhetoric ≠ restricted universal inference.**

Jarvis therefore supplies an early American control parallel to the later Steinmetz/W. I. Thomas case.

### Relation to 005 p.19

Lovejoy’s p.19 contains an `1819` reference in exactly the future-life / missionary-contamination problem field. Jarvis is consequently a high-value chronological candidate/control. **The manuscript referent is not yet proved to be Jarvis.** Do not write `Lovejoy cites Jarvis` until the handwriting/source chain is resolved.

Even if the p.19 `1819` proves to refer elsewhere, Jarvis remains methodologically useful because he shows the evidentiary structure available in the American literature Lovejoy’s problem field inherited.

## 4. Brinton: `careful selection of authorities` and relative evidentiary weight are already explicit method

Daniel G. Brinton is physically present in 005: PDF p.46 / MS p.71 is a bibliographic slip for *The American Race* (New York, 1891).

A separate Brinton work, *The Myths of the New World*, was available in editions of 1868, 1876 and 1896. Its bibliographical note says that everything in the subject depends on careful selection of authorities and that Brinton will assign a **relative amount of weight** to the works he most frequently cites.

He then uses Jarvis as his first historiographic example. Brinton argues that Jarvis’s assumption of a primitive civilization/primeval truth caused him to misread Native religion as degeneration from a purer faith rather than historical development.

This supplies an important field-level control:

- source ranking was not novel;
- criticism of theory-laden ethnographic reading was not novel;
- American comparative scholarship could already separate `authority value` from `theory imposed on facts`.

Do not infer that Lovejoy read Brinton’s bibliographical note merely because *The American Race* is in 005. The point is disciplinary ecology: Brinton belongs directly to Lovejoy’s source world, and the practice of weighting authorities was available within that world before the Paris year.

## 5. PDF p.26: `poor authorities` can remain usable authorities

A high-resolution reread of PDF p.26 materially narrows the previously unresolved note. The lower-left section is discussing the **Todas / Dravidian** problem and then writes, with exact syntax still partly difficult, `Poor authorities ... v. Marshall, A Phrenologist among the Todas ... & Breeks, Primitive Tribes ...`.

The two works are securely identifiable as:

- William E. Marshall, *A Phrenologist amongst the Todas; or, The Study of a Primitive Tribe in South India* (1873);
- J. W. Breeks, *An Account of the Primitive Tribes and Monuments of the Nīlagiris* (1873).

The important operation is now secure even if one connecting noun remains unreadable:

> **poor/weak authority does not equal unusable datum.**

Lovejoy keeps the material in play but marks the source base as carrying reduced weight.

That is exactly what a weighted evidence set looks like in working notes.

## 6. Tylor’s 1873 review of Marshall provides a contemporary external control

E. B. Tylor reviewed Marshall’s *A Phrenologist amongst the Todas* in *Nature* 9 (11 December 1873), 99–101.

Tylor’s judgment is methodologically useful because it separates two levels of the book. He says, in substance, that Marshall advances several points of ethnology even though much of the theoretical part is unlikely to gain acceptance.

Thus the very source Lovejoy later places under a `poor authorities` warning had already been received through a distinction between:

- empirical/ethnological contribution;
- theoretical interpretation.

Again, the aim is not to prove Lovejoy read Tylor’s review. It is a period control showing that **selective evidentiary use of a theoretically weak source** was a recognizable scholarly practice.

## 7. The notebook-wide pattern is stronger than the three reporter examples

The final comparative-religion pages of 005 show the same question-relative weighting even when reporter contamination is no longer the explicit issue.

### PDF pp.106–107

Present ceremoniality, publicity or fixed ritual form is poor evidence for original sacramental motive. Alleged religious cannibalism can collapse under closer scrutiny.

### p.108

Eating selected organs for special efficacy can belong to sympathetic magic rather than sacrifice or communion.

### p.109

Lovejoy explicitly worries that uncertain cannibal testimony can too easily be converted into an evolutionary theory; enemies, relatives and selected organs require separate explanations.

### p.110

The general theory that animal sacrifice substitutes for an earlier human victim is called speculative; only a few cases securely demonstrate the transition, and other social/economic causes can explain decline of human sacrifice.

### pp.112–115

Circumcision, beating, cutting and other initiation injuries cannot be grouped under a single partial-human-sacrifice origin simply from formal resemblance. Similar injuries can have different ritual histories.

### p.117

The summary redistributes the whole sacrificial field: alimentary and expiatory/propitiatory forms are common; sacramental union is comparatively exceptional.

### p.119

Selected-body-part transfer is treated through sympathetic/transferable efficacy, while Lovejoy explicitly allows that exact historical motive may sometimes be unrecoverable.

The recurring rule is therefore:

> **similarity licenses comparison before it licenses explanation; explanation licenses classification before it licenses genealogy.**

## 8. A four-level matrix for `weighted comparison`

The evidence now suggests a practical way to describe Lovejoy’s research grammar without turning it into a timeless abstract method.

### Level 1 — witness admissibility

Can the report be used at all?

Variables: reporter, date, missionary/contact history, competence, internal contradiction, explicit `poor authority` warning.

### Level 2 — classificatory admissibility

Can the observed act/belief be grouped with other formally similar cases?

A `yes` here does not establish common cause.

### Level 3 — mechanism inference

Does the case operate through communion, propitiation, provision, punishment, sympathetic efficacy, incorporation, purification, social continuity, etc.?

Outward morphology does not decide this level.

### Level 4 — genealogical/developmental inference

Does similarity establish common origin, survival, borrowing, substitution, universal primitive stage or historical sequence?

This requires the strongest warrant and is where 005 most repeatedly blocks easy transitions.

One datum can therefore be:

`admissible at L1` → `useful at L2` → `ambiguous at L3` → `insufficient at L4`.

That is what `question-relative evidentiary license` means in concrete archival terms.

## 9. N. W. Thomas now becomes an even better institutional control

Batch 66 recovered Thomas’s 1900 self-description of his 1898 Marillier-era Welsh research. Thomas says a comparative category can legitimately contain material with multiple possible origins and that different sections have **unequal value as evidence** for the historical proposition of former European totemism.

The homology with 005 is now sharper:

- descriptive/classificatory inclusion can be retained;
- historical-origin inference can be downgraded;
- evidence is weighted relative to the question being asked.

Do not convert this into direct pedagogical transmission. But it strengthens the claim that the Marillier conference environment can be associated with a **research grammar of redivision and question-relative proof**, not with the invention of elementary source skepticism.

## 10. Consequence for the Paris argument

The paper should avoid:

> Paris taught Lovejoy to criticize ethnographic sources.

The evidence now makes a better sentence possible:

> **Lovejoy entered Paris with access to source-critical traditions already embedded in American philology and ethnography and in the ethnographic books themselves. What the Marillier environment helps us explain is not the birth of skepticism but the systematic redivision of what different kinds of evidence were permitted to prove.**

This formulation also fits the 004→005 transition. The evidentiary object changes from textual witness to ethnographic report, but the notebook increasingly separates:

`presence of a datum` from `semantic/mechanistic identity` from `historical dependence`.
