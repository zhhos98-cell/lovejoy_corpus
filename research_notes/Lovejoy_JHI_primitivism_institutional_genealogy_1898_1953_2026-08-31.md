# Lovejoy → JHI: `primitivism`, cross-field migration, and an institutional genealogy, 1898–1953

Date: 2026-08-31  
Status: **ACTIVE ARTICLE-TARGET SWEEP / DIRECT INSTITUTIONAL EVIDENCE + PRIMARY PROGRAMMATIC CONTROL / NO CAUSAL CONTINUITY CLAIM**

## Core article hypothesis

The strongest current JHI-scale problem is no longer simply that the young Arthur O. Lovejoy worked on Buddhism before becoming a historian of ideas. It is that one can now reconstruct a sequence in which problems first encountered in Buddhist philology and comparative religion — classification, temporal priority, causal relation, source mediation, cross-cultural aggregation, and the warrant for moving between different ordered series — later reappear inside the institutional and programmatic formation of the Hopkins history-of-ideas tradition.

This must **not** be written as:

`Buddhist studies caused the unit-idea method`.

The defensible historical question is instead:

> **How were relation-sensitive and comparative problems from Lovejoy's early Orientalist / comparative-religion work selectively reclassified, narrowed, and reaggregated as objects and procedures of interwar `history of ideas`?**

The best current bridge-object is **`primitivism`**.

# I. 1898: Buddhist technical work is already relation-sensitive historical analysis

Primary:

Arthur O. Lovejoy, `The Buddhistic technical terms upādāna and upādisesa`, *Journal of the American Oriental Society* 19 (1898), 126–136.  
JSTOR: https://www.jstor.org/stable/592475  
DOI: 10.2307/592475.

Repository archive control remains notebook 004, especially MS p.123 / PDF p.42, where `viññāṇa` can be logically/classificatorily contained within `nāma/nāmarūpa` while being treated as temporally antecedent with a separate confidence status.

The important early operation is therefore not one doctrine but **typed relation jurisdiction**:

`semantic / classificatory relation`
!=
`temporal relation`
!=
`causal relation`
!=
`historical derivation / borrowing`.

Existing JHI Blog evidence already establishes the archive and 1898 publication connection. This new branch asks how far that practice belongs to a prehistory of the later history-of-ideas programme.

---

# II. 1906–1914: `primitive` becomes an aggregation and bridge-license problem

Existing direct-primary controls in this repository show:

- 1906 `Primitive Energetics` extracts an impersonal-power mechanism from heterogeneous ethnographic materials and promotes it toward `the fundamental concept of primitive philosophy`;
- 1914 Lovejoy retrospectively says Leuba's `dynamism` is essentially / precisely the same theory as his 1906 `primitive energetics`;
- but in the same 1914 review he distinguishes a strongly warranted **non-animistic** content/type proposition from the chronological proposition that it was **pre-animistic**, which he calls probable but not demonstrable;
- the 1914 review also distinguishes (a) analyst stipulation, (b) common elements in historical uses of a term, and (c) analytical description of complex observable phenomena, preferring (c).

Thus by 1914 the central issue is already:

`CATEGORY / TYPE`
!=
`CHRONOLOGICAL PRIORITY`
!=
`NOMINAL DEFINITION`.

This supplies a strong actor-level bridge to the later history-of-ideas concern with distinguishing names, ideas, logical relations, historical operation, and changing combinations.

Do not infer a single stable method from 1898 through 1914. The current claim is recurrence plus increasing explicitness.

---

# III. 1923: institutionalization begins with an explicit Occidental jurisdiction

Direct archival finding-aid control:

Johns Hopkins University, `History of Ideas Club records`, founded 1923 by Lovejoy, George Boas, H. C. Lancaster and colleagues.  
https://aspace.library.jhu.edu/repositories/3/resources/1029

The constitution defines the field as historical study of the development and influence of general philosophical conceptions, ethical ideas, and aesthetic fashions **in occidental literatures**, together with their relations to philosophy, science, and political/social movements.

This is methodologically important because it institutionalizes:

`one idea/tendency`
→ `multiple disciplinary manifestations`
→ `development + influence across fields`,

while simultaneously drawing a geographical/civilizational jurisdiction around `occidental literatures`.

That boundary should be treated as historical evidence, not merely criticized retrospectively.

# IV. 1924–1934: `primitivism` becomes a recurrent Club research programme

Direct source:

Johns Hopkins History of Ideas Club, *Studies in Intellectual History* (1953), Appendix B, `Speakers and Their Topics, 1923–1952`, open-access Project MUSE.  
https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/1/oa_edited_volume/chapter/2450983

The meeting sequence is striking:

- **13 Nov. 1924** — Lois Whitney, `An Early Scotch Primitivist`;
- **23 Nov. 1926** — Gilbert Chinard, `Nationalistic Primitivism in Eighteenth Century England`;
- **22 Nov. 1928** — Arthur O. Lovejoy, `Notes on Primitivism in Antiquity`;
- **8 Mar. 1934** — Paul E. Dumont, `Primitivism in Ancient India`.

The 1934 India talk is particularly important because it shows the actual Club practice moving beyond the constitution's formal `occidental literatures` phrasing.

The safest current formulation is:

> **The Club's formal jurisdiction was Occidental, but its research practice could recruit non-Occidental materials when they served a transregional problem such as primitivism.**

This is not yet evidence that the field became globally symmetrical. It may instead document a specific asymmetry in which Indian material entered as a supplementary/comparative extension of a problem whose main historiographical address remained Western.

# V. 1934 seminar → 1935 publication: a direct institutional publication edge

The strongest new institutional edge is now secure.

Paul E. Dumont's 1934 Club talk `Primitivism in Ancient India` became the supplementary essay **`Primitivism in Indian Literature`** in:

Arthur O. Lovejoy and George Boas, *Primitivism and Related Ideas in Antiquity* (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins Press, 1935), pp. 433–447 (pagination varies slightly in later citations/reprints).

Bibliographic controls:

- Folger catalog: https://catalog.folger.edu/record/22434
- 1935/1965 record: https://openlibrary.org/books/OL5952624M/Primitivism_and_related_ideas_in_antiquity
- later literature cites Dumont, `Primitivism in Indian Literature`, pp.433–447: https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0185-30582015000200009&script=sci_arttext

The same volume also includes W. F. Albright, `Primitivism in Ancient Western Asia`, followed by Dumont's India essay and Lovejoy's `Some Meanings of 'Nature'`.

Therefore there is a directly traceable chain:

`History of Ideas Club seminar problem`
→ `Ancient India comparative extension`
→ `published supplementary essay inside the Documentary History of Primitivism`.

This is stronger than a generic claim that the Club was interdisciplinary.

# VI. 1935 `Primitivism`: inclusion and asymmetry coexist

Contemporary bibliographic/review evidence describes the Documentary History as a history of reflection on civilization and primitivism **chiefly / throughout Western civilization**, while the first volume simultaneously incorporates supplementary essays on Ancient Western Asia and Indian literature.

This produces a potentially important object structure:

`WESTERN / OCCIDENTAL MAIN HISTORICAL ADDRESS`
+
`NON-WESTERN COMPARATIVE / SUPPLEMENTARY MATERIAL`.

The paper should ask whether this is best understood as:

1. expansion of a Western history-of-ideas programme;
2. comparative infrastructure imported from Orientalist expertise;
3. reclassification of non-Western intellectual materials as supplementary evidence for a category addressed primarily to Western civilizational self-understanding;
4. some combination of the above.

Do not decide this from table of contents alone. Directly read Dumont's essay, the volume preface, and the editorial framing before making the stronger claim.

# VII. 1940: `primitivism` is explicitly in JHI's founding programme

Primary:

Arthur O. Lovejoy, `Reflections on the History of Ideas`, *Journal of the History of Ideas* 1.1 (Jan. 1940), 3–23.  
JSTOR issue: https://www.jstor.org/stable/i327235  
Article DOI: 10.2307/2707007.

An authorized Spanish translation is openly available and has been page-checked in the 2026-08-31 web sweep; use the original English for publication quotation once direct text/image is controlled.

The central programmatic claims are:

- departmentalization by subject, period, nationality or language does not correspond to the real movement of historical phenomena;
- ideas/preconceptions/categories/postulates/metaphors/analogical modes can migrate across conventional disciplinary jurisdictions;
- understanding one manifestation can require following causal antecedents/consequents beyond a single field;
- the journal exists as a liaison / cross-fertilization infrastructure among these otherwise separated inquiries.

Most important for the present project: Lovejoy explicitly lists among exemplary broad ideas/doctrines suitable for JHI-style study:

`evolution`
`progress`
**`primitivism`**
`theories of human motivation`
`views of human nature`
`mechanistic / organic conceptions`
`determinism / indeterminism`
`individualism / collectivism`
`nationalism`
`racism`.

Therefore `primitivism` is not an external topic later attached to the JHI tradition. It belongs to the journal's founding programme.

# VIII. 1940 should be read as relation-sensitive migration, not free-floating idea travel

A crude reading of `ideas are migratory` risks producing exactly the caricature later associated with Lovejoy: timeless atoms freely hopping across contexts.

Both Lovejoy's programme and current historiographical reassessment complicate that reading.

Nico Mouton, `An Apologia for Arthur Lovejoy's Long-Range Approach to the History of Ideas`, *History and Theory* 62.2 (2023), 272–295, DOI 10.1111/hith.12298, emphasizes Lovejoy's own rule that logical/psychological analysis generates **tentative hypotheses**. The historian must return to historical data to determine whether reconstructed logical relations actually operated historically.

Open access:
https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/hith.12298

This matches the strongest internal result from the Lovejoy repository:

`ANALYTICAL RELATION`
!=
`HISTORICAL OPERATION`.

Thus the article can revise the standard genealogy at a deeper level. The early Buddhist notebook's typed relations are relevant not because they reveal a proto-unit-idea, but because they show Lovejoy confronting the same basic problem later made programmatic for history of ideas:

> **How can one move analytically across categories, fields, times, and source traditions without allowing evidence for one relation to settle another?**

# IX. 1950–1953: primitivism remains a method test inside the JHI/Club tradition

The 1953 History of Ideas Club volume supplies a useful retrospective control.

George Boas, `Some Problems of Intellectual History`, *Studies in Intellectual History* (1953), pp.3–21, open access Project MUSE:  
https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/1/oa_edited_volume/chapter/2450973

Boas explicitly returns to `primitivism` as a classification problem. He discusses Philip Merlan's JHI note `Lucretius—Primitivist or Progressivist` (*JHI* 11.3, June 1950, 364–368; DOI 10.2307/2707737) and asks whether an idea can be historically present in a text even if the author did not consciously intend to treat that problem.

Boas also stresses:

- same word != same idea;
- one word can name many logically distinct ideas;
- shared label does not license a hidden timeless essence;
- complex ideas can require decomposition into logically independent propositions;
- logical reconstruction must not be confused with mind-reading or with the actor's conscious inference;
- the historian faces a persistent gap between general class and particular historical instantiation.

This shows `primitivism` functioning not just as a subject of the early School but as a **test case for the epistemology of history-of-ideas classification**.

# X. Strongest current JHI article architecture

## Possible title

**Reclassifying the Primitive: Arthur Lovejoy's Buddhist Philology and the Making of the History of Ideas**

Alternatives:

- `Before the Unit-Idea: Arthur Lovejoy, Buddhist Philology, and the Prehistory of the History of Ideas`
- `From Buddhist Categories to Primitivism: Arthur Lovejoy and the Formation of the History of Ideas`
- stronger / riskier: `The Occidentalization of the History of Ideas: Arthur Lovejoy from Buddhist Philology to JHI`

## Thesis ceiling

Safest current thesis:

> **Lovejoy's mature history-of-ideas programme did not simply emerge from a self-contained history of Western philosophy. His 1890s Buddhist work and 1906 comparative-religion research placed him inside scholarly practices where semantic identity, logical classification, temporal priority, causal sequence, textual derivation and cross-cultural analogy had to be separately warranted. During the interwar institutionalization of history of ideas, `primitivism` became one site in which these problems were reaggregated: the Hopkins Club repeatedly studied primitivism, extended it to Ancient India, published that work as supplementary material in the 1935 documentary project, and Lovejoy explicitly named primitivism as a model JHI problem in 1940. The continuity is therefore best sought not in a timeless method transmitted intact from Orientalism to intellectual history, but in a changing jurisprudence of classification, comparison, migration and historical warrant.**

## More ambitious version to test

> **The institutionalization of history of ideas selectively reclassified an earlier comparative/Orientalist problem-space. Non-Western materials could enter the Club and the primitivism project, yet they often did so as comparative or supplementary material inside an Occidental main address. Lovejoy's own Buddhist formation therefore supplies a way to reconstruct not only the origins of a method but the changing jurisdiction through which `history of ideas` constituted its proper historical objects.**

This second claim requires direct reading of the 1935 preface + Dumont essay + any planning correspondence before promotion.
