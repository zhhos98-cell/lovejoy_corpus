## 2. Cameron closes the first ethnographic packet almost item by item

Verney Lovett Cameron, *Across Africa* / Henriette Loreau's French translation *À travers l'Afrique* (Paris: Hachette, 1881), chapter XVIII, supplies a distinctive Manyéma packet.

At/near Moéné Bougga, Cameron says the inhabitants:

- are cannibals;
- eat not only men killed in combat;
- also eat persons who die of sickness;
- macerate corpses in running water until the flesh is almost putrefied;
- consume the bodies with little further preparation.

This is too distinctive to treat the corresponding p.55 case material as Lovejoy-generated ethnography.

### Ownership

`CAMERON / MOÉNÉ BOUGGA`
->
`BATTLE-DEAD + SICK-DEAD + RUNNING-WATER MACERATION`.

Source ownership: S3. Direct physical transmission/citation on p.55 remains image-dependent if the page does not visibly name Cameron.

## 3. Cameron gives the **opposite** sex/taste direction

The same Cameron passage immediately records a cannibal song. In the French 1881 text it says, in substance:

- the flesh of man is good;
- the flesh of woman is bad;
- female flesh is resorted to when provisions are scarce / when a man is unavailable.

Thus Cameron cannot be used to support the current editorial relation:

`FEMALE FLESH GASTRONOMICALLY SUPERIOR`
+
`MALE FLESH PREFERRED`.

If p.55 really contains that relation, the immediate carrier must be something other than this Cameron passage, or the manuscript/image reading must be rechecked.

This is a source-level contradiction, not permission to overwrite the manuscript with Cameron.

---

## 4. The opposite comparative convention existed independently before Lovejoy

The field itself contains abundant pre-/near-contemporary reports running in the other direction.

Nineteenth-century Fiji material records that women's flesh was preferred to men's; other Pacific accounts explain the preference by greater tenderness. An 1863 account of Fiji says people rejoiced in women's flesh because it `ate well`; later travel compilations preserve the same female/tender preference tradition. Early twentieth-century recollections and surveys continue it, but the important point for p.55 is that the **female-better/tender comparative proposition was already available in the pre-1899 ethnographic field**.

Therefore the p.55 conflict is not simply:

`CAMERON SOURCE`
vs.
`IMPOSSIBLE MANUSCRIPT READING`.

A second ethnographic carrier with the opposite sex/taste proposition is historically plausible.

But plausibility is not identification.

## 5. Steinmetz helps define the classification field but does not close the sex/taste carrier

Rudolf Steinmetz's 1896 *Endokannibalismus* and its contemporary review literature organize cannibalism by motives and objects. A contemporary *American Journal of Sociology* review summarizes the tabular scheme as including motives such as:

- lack of food;
- longing for meat;
- special relish for human flesh;
- animistic belief;

with women, children, invalids, the aged and criminals among principal objects.

This matters because p.55's move from `alimentary` to a possible non-alimentary selection rule sits inside an already explicit motive-classification field.

However, the focused search did **not** recover from Steinmetz a clause matching the p.55 structure:

`female flesh tastes better, yet male flesh is selected because of male power/value`.

Steinmetz therefore remains field control, not the recovered immediate carrier of the gender/taste paragraph.

## 8. The `power/value` inference must be downgraded pending image control

The current clean summary says male preference `therefore requires some explanation beyond taste` and connects it provisionally with the superior power/value attributed to men.

This is analytically plausible because the contemporary cannibalism literature repeatedly links consumption of particular persons/body parts to acquisition of strength, courage, life-force, or other qualities. Lumholtz's 1889 Australian material, for example, links kidney/kidney-fat consumption to acquisition of the slain person's strength; other late-nineteenth-century sources locate courage or force in heart/liver/blood/body parts.

But p.55 needs a stricter distinction:

- **field availability** of strength/power explanations: F/A0;
- **p.55 exact inference from sex/taste contradiction to male power/value**: not yet diplomatically secured as Lovejoy's wording;
- immediate carrier of that explanation: unresolved.

Until the page is re-opened, do not cite the clean summary's `therefore` as a manuscript logical connective.

Safe formulation:

> **The page is image-secure for an alimentary classification and for a male/female selection contrast. The current editorial explanation that male selection overrides female gastronomic superiority because of male power/value remains a diplomatic/source-boundary HOLD.**
