# Lovejoy 1907 `Kant's Classification of the Forms of Judgment` — formal-logical + historical audit

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: **PRIMARY-REPRINT/SECONDARY-RECONSTRUCTION CONTROL / CORE OPERATION SECURE / FULL ORIGINAL PAGE COLLATION STILL DESIRABLE**

## Core result

Arthur O. Lovejoy's `Kant's Classification of the Forms of Judgment`, *Philosophical Review* 16 (1907), 588–603, should no longer be represented merely as a prelude to his later interest in formal logic.

The paper already combines two operations that the project has usually treated separately:

1. **formal-logical classification audit:** does a stated classificatory principle actually divide the possible forms consistently, non-arbitrarily and exhaustively?;
2. **historical reconstruction of the classification itself:** how did Kant alter the logical classifications available from predecessor German logicians, and what does that alteration reveal about the actual direction of his philosophical construction?

Thus the 1907 paper is simultaneously logical criticism and history of logic / historical reconstruction.

The strongest current formulation is:

> **Lovejoy does not merely ask whether Kant's table is true. He asks whether its divisions follow from a consistent principle and whether Kant's own historical claim about the table's derivation from formal logic is borne out by the earlier logical field.**

This is a particularly important bridge between the 1906 historical rubric critique and the 1910 direct formal-logic paper.

## I. Evidentiary status of the current reconstruction

The original 1907 article is bibliographically secure and was reprinted in Moltke S. Gram, ed., *Kant: Disputed Questions* (1967; later eds.), pp.269–283 / equivalent pagination in later edition.

Current control rests on:

- Daniel J. Wilson's annotated bibliography, which summarizes Lovejoy as testing Kant's classification with the `narrow-minded spirit of the formal logician` and finding ambiguity / shifts of meaning;
- modern Kant scholarship that reproduces and analyzes Lovejoy's objections at proposition level, including the quantity, quality and modality sections;
- a 2026 history-of-Kant paper that reproduces Lovejoy's p.590 conclusion concerning the historical origin of the classification principle;
- the known reference structure of the 1907 paper, including P. Hauck's 1906 study of the genesis of Kant's table, Kant's *Logic*, and the relevant *Critique of Pure Reason* passages.

The operation-level reconstruction below is strong. Exact publication-facing quotation should still be checked against the 1907 original or a facsimile of the 1967 reprint.

## II. The opening historical problem: formal logic as an object of history

A modern full discussion of Lovejoy's critique reports that he opens by arguing that Kantian logic must be studied in the context of the German logicians of Kant's period if Kant's philosophy is to be understood and historically evaluated.

This matters because Lovejoy's `formal logician` standpoint is not ahistorical. The paper asks what forms of judgment were available in predecessor logics, what Kant changed, and what those changes reveal about the motives of the *Critique*.

P. Hauck's 1906 `Die Entstehung der kantischen Urteilstafel. Ein Beitrag zur Geschichte der Logik` is central. Hauck argued that Kant did not simply inherit the table and then derive the categories from it; rather, the needs of transcendental philosophy helped shape the table of judgments.

Lovejoy accepts the historical problem but pushes its consequence further. On the current reconstruction, if the operative principle of classification did not in fact come from the recognized formal logics, then the public order of derivation in the *Critique* cannot be treated as a transparent report of the constructive order.

This is already a historiographical move:

`actor's explicit genealogy of a classification`
≠ automatically `historian's reconstruction of how the classification was produced`.

---

## III. Quantity: test whether the classificatory principle licenses exactly three classes

The quantitative section is one of the clearest controls on Lovejoy's analytical procedure.

Lovejoy argues that Kant shifts between a formal-logical and a more mathematical sense of `quantity`. If the latter sense is used, the tripartite classification into singular / particular / universal does not follow uniquely.

He presses the point by asking why additional degrees such as `Most S are P` or numerically specified pluralities could not form separate classes if the amount of objects spoken about is what controls the division.

Whatever the merits of the objection — later commentators defend Kant here — the methodological operation is clear:

`state the classificatory principle`
→ `generate additional possible cases under that principle`
→ `ask whether the canonical list is exhaustive and non-arbitrary`.

This anticipates the positive procedure in the 1910 opposition paper, where Lovejoy explicitly enumerates all five possible denotative relations and derives inference only from the resulting case-space.

---

## IV. Quality: a coordinate class must be distinguished by a consistent criterion

On judgments of quality, Lovejoy argues that Kant has not consistently distinguished `infinite` judgments from affirmative and negative judgments.

The important project-level point is again methodological rather than verdict-level. Lovejoy asks whether the same differentiating criterion actually separates the proposed coordinate classes.

Thus:

`different label`
≠ `demonstrated logical class`.

A separate class requires a relation or property that discriminates it consistently from the neighboring classes.

This is structurally continuous with the later anti-umbrella rule in `The Thirteen Pragmatisms`: a named distinction is not yet a logical partition.

---

## V. Modality: ambiguity creates incompatible classification principles

The modality section gives an even cleaner example of semantic discrimination feeding logical diagnosis.

Lovejoy reconstructs at least two senses at work in Kant's account of modality:

1. modality as something like the subjective degree / mode of affirmation in relation to thought;
2. modality as a conditional relation between truths.

He argues that the two principles do different logical work and produce incompatible consequences. In the second reading, relation and modality risk collapsing into one another; apodeictic judgment does not fit neatly into the same pattern as problematic and assertoric judgments.

The method is:

`one term / one rubric`
→ `discriminate distinct operative meanings`
→ `trace the consequences of each meaning separately`
→ `show that the umbrella classification depends on their illicit interchange`.

This is considerably more specific than generic `clarification of ambiguity`.

---

## VI. Historical reversal: the table may validate the categories rather than generate them

A 2026 reconstruction reproduces Lovejoy's 1907 p.590 claim that the principle actually used in Kant's table does not come from any known formal logic and is instead an artificial construction made afterward to validate the scheme of categories that Kant appears to derive from it.

Publication-grade wording requires direct facsimile verification, but the historical claim is well attested in the reception literature.

If correct, the inferential structure is striking:

`Kant's presented order:`
formal logic table → categories

`Lovejoy/Hauck historical reconstruction:`
transcendental/category requirements → alteration of judgment table → retrospective presentation as formal-logical basis.

This is not merely logical criticism. It is a reconstruction of **construction order versus justificatory order**.

That distinction belongs directly beside Lovejoy's later concern with history, genealogy and present systematic function: the logical role a classification plays in a finished system does not by itself establish its historical origin.

---

## VII. 1906 → 1907 → 1910

The Kant sequence now looks increasingly coherent at the level of operation without requiring a teleological method-origin story.

### 1906 `Kant's Antithesis of Dogmatism and Criticism`

A large historical rubric is decomposed into several propositions about predecessors, novelty, criterion and method; local historical checking makes the macro opposition collapse.

### 1907 `Classification of the Forms of Judgment`

A canonical logical table is audited against:

- the consistency of its classificatory principles;
- possible cases omitted by those principles;
- semantic shifts among the governing terms;
- the historical logics from which Kant claims or appears to derive the table;
- the reconstructed direction of philosophical construction.

### 1910 `Opposition in Formal Logic`

Lovejoy moves from criticism to a positive case-space method: enumerate all possible denotative relations, map propositions to compatible cases, suspend judgment among surviving possibilities, and derive only what the compatibility relations license.

Safe synthesis:

> **Between 1906 and 1910 Lovejoy repeatedly turns inherited classificatory wholes into explicit relation problems: first by historical decomposition, then by formal-logical audit, and finally by exhaustive case-space construction.**

This recurrence matters for the history of the later `unit-idea`, but direct causal continuity remains to be demonstrated.

---

## VIII. Why this matters for the `unit-idea` problem

The 1907 paper strengthens the competing hypothesis that a `unit` in Lovejoy's mature method should not automatically be read as an ontological atom.

Here an analytical unit is created by asking:

- what is the operative distinction?;
- what cases does it actually discriminate?;
- which proposed classes remain genuinely independent?;
- which are products of equivocation or an externally imposed architectonic?;
- what historical reconstruction explains the resulting table?

This suggests a potentially important genealogical path:

`classification as received whole`
→ `logical decomposition into discriminable relations/classes`
→ `historical reconstruction of why this configuration exists`
→ later `historical complex → analytical units → recurrence/recombination`.

The last arrow is a hypothesis, not a closed descent relation.

## IX. Evidence ceiling

### Secure / strongly controlled

- the 1907 article explicitly approaches Kant's classification from a formal-logical standpoint;
- ambiguity and shift of meaning are central targets;
- Lovejoy audits quantity, quality, relation and modality rather than merely objecting to one proposition;
- modern proposition-level reconstructions document his quantity, quality and modality objections;
- Hauck 1906 is a central contemporary historical control on the genesis of the judgment table;
- Lovejoy links evaluation of Kant's logic to historical comparison with predecessor German logicians;
- the paper questions whether Kant's classificatory table follows coherently from a single formal-logical principle.

### Strongly attested but publication quotation still needs direct facsimile check

- Lovejoy's p.590 conclusion that the operative classification principle comes from no known formal logic and is constructed to validate the category scheme;
- exact wording of the `narrow-minded spirit of the formal logician` line;
- exact placement of each quoted formula in the original 1907 pagination.

### Open

- Lovejoy's immediate source edition for every predecessor logician beyond Hauck/Kant references;
- whether the paper directly cites or uses Venn, Schröder, Jevons, Keynes, Joseph or other nineteenth-century logical authors — the secure Venn/Schröder edge presently belongs to the 1910 paper;
- the pedagogical or reading route by which Lovejoy acquired his formal-logical repertoire;
- direct descent from this Kant audit into mature history-of-ideas terminology.

### Do not claim

- Lovejoy's 1907 critique is necessarily correct about Kant;
- Kant's table is simply arbitrary as a settled historical fact;
- Lovejoy was already practicing Fregean/Russellian analytic philosophy;
- the unit-idea method is fully present in 1907;
- formal-logical audit alone explains Lovejoy's historical method.
