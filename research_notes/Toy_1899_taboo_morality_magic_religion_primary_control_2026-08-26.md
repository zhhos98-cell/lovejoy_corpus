## I. `Taboo and Morality`: antecedent presence is not causal role

Toy opens against Frazer and especially Jevons's claim that taboo protected or effectively generated the sentiment of moral obligation. His most important methodological sentence occurs before the ethnographic argument proper.

Toy concedes that, in one sense, every antecedent in a line of historical advance is a necessary condition of what follows. He immediately adds that another antecedent might have been substituted and that, in a **complex antecedent**, one must determine:

- the `active element of advance`;
- the `merely formal and accessory condition`.

He then asks which role taboo actually played.

This is a direct causal-role partition:

`X is historically antecedent / co-present`

≠

`X is the active causal element producing Y`.

For the present project this is substantially stronger than thematic similarity. Toy is explicitly refusing to infer causal efficacy from antecedent presence alone.

## II. Prevalence and transmissibility are separated from the target proposition

Toy next brackets two questions that could easily dominate a comparative-religion argument:

- universality of taboo;
- transmissibility of taboo.

He allows wide prevalence as a working assumption but says transmissibility is not the point at issue for the inquiry into taboo's **moral influence**. Transmissibility may help explain the persistence/influence of the institution, yet it does not settle the relation between taboo and morality.

Thus Toy directly partitions:

`distribution / transmissibility`

from

`causal contribution to moral sentiment/code`.

This is a proposition-scope control: evidence relevant to one historical question is not automatically decisive for another.

## III. Moral obligation precedes taboo in Toy's reconstruction

Toy's positive reconstruction denies the Jevons sequence. He argues that the sentiment of obligation preceded the institution of taboo. Taboo, for Toy, already presupposes:

- conscious relations with unseen powers;
- special relations between powers and objects/acts;
- an organized religion of a relatively advanced kind.

He instead treats the moral and religious attitudes as independently recognizable developments from human experience. The same basic sentiments of dependence/deference operate in different relational directions:

- `manward` activity, aided by sympathy → morality;
- `godward` activity → religion.

Toy then gives the distinction a regulatory form:

> taboo deals with duties toward gods; ordinary morality deals with duties toward men.

This is not a modern neutral sociology. Toy's language is developmental, progressivist and normatively ranked. But within that framework he is explicitly distinguishing two systems by **relation and source of obligation**, not by outward resemblance of prohibitions.

## IV. Two regulation systems can collide, coalesce, and reinforce without sharing an origin

Toy describes taboo and ordinary morality as `two systems of regulations which arise in different ways`. They can:

- collide, as pity for the sick conflicts with a taboo against touching the sick;
- coalesce, when taboo is used to protect land, marriage or other human rights;
- reinforce one another without one creating the other.

His property/marriage examples are especially clear. Toy says taboo did not create or suggest the underlying right; a current religious belief was used to **enforce an existing right**. Religious taboo can reinforce a non-religious moral sentiment whose historical basis is elsewhere.

The inferential structure is:

`mechanism A reinforces outcome Y`

≠

`mechanism A originated Y`.

This is directly relevant to the repository's recurring distinction between genesis, function, selector and later superposition.

## V. Toy's conclusion: concomitance, conflict and coalescence rather than single-source genealogy

Toy's summary is explicit:

- taboo is not the creator of moral sentiment or moral code;
- it is a **concomitant** of moral life;
- it can oppose natural morality;
- it can coalesce with it;
- it can foster obligation as positive law while also obstructing intelligent/moral obedience as arbitrary law.

His proposed `real moral force` is sympathetic social intercourse, which rationalizes or rejects taboo under experience, observation and reflection.

This is a differentiated relation model rather than a one-source genealogy.

### Period-language warning

The article also contains strong late-nineteenth-century progress language: `uncivilized peoples`, unequal societal powers of observation/thought, `peoples of progress`, rational versus irrational development. These statements are direct evidence for Toy's own evolutionary hierarchy and should not be silently converted into Lovejoy's political semantics. They are context for the American comparative-religion ecology, not a proxy for MD-001.

## VI. `Relation between Magic and Religion`: reject three total genealogies

Toy begins the second article by identifying three widely held relations between religion and magic:

1. magic as degraded religion;
2. magic as the germ of religion;
3. magic and religion as independent, mutually unrelated systems.

He says these positions rely in part on restricted historical surveys, arbitrary definitions and mistaken interpretations. More importantly, he refuses the assumption that early historical formations already possessed clean oppositions:

- natural / supernatural;
- gods / demons;
- friendly / unfriendly powers;
- religion / magic as mutually exclusive spheres.

His historical model begins from an undifferentiated/composite field and asks how later distinctions emerge.

## VII. Analytic-scope firewall: Toy removes `sympathetic magic` from the article

The article's footnote supplies an unusually clean scope control. Toy says what is commonly called `sympathetic magic` — rainmaking by sprinkling water, injuring a person through an image, acquiring qualities by eating a body part, name/body-part control — is **outside the present inquiry**.

He assigns those procedures instead to a `savage conception of natural law`, calling them specimens of `savage science`, and reserves `magic` in this article for **the power to control supernatural beings**.

Whatever one thinks of the historical category, the analytical operation is direct:

`same inherited label: magic`
→ divide by operative mechanism
→ exclude one mechanism from the current proposition
→ define the remaining object narrowly.

This is one of the strongest direct American controls now available for mechanism-sensitive category restriction.

## VIII. Power, moralization and differentiation

Toy then reconstructs a vague early field of power/life in things. He stresses that the effects attributed to this power were initially helpful or harmful without a settled logical or moral order. Reliable discrimination between beneficial and injurious phenomena appears only through experience.

He likewise treats the distinction between friendly `gods` and unfriendly `demons` as relatively late. Earlier beings are grouped more generally as agencies affecting life; later social and religious organization progressively differentiates them.

This matters because the visible outcome (`helpful`, `harmful`, `friendly`, `hostile`) is not treated as proof that an already stabilized moral/social category existed at the earliest stage.

## IX. The magician is split by function

Toy's magician is initially a master of occult influences and only later, when these influences become personalized, a controller of supernatural beings. The early magician can control good or bad powers and can be benevolent or malevolent while still being a recognized member of the religious community.

Toy then divides this composite function longitudinally:

- the friendly-communion side develops toward priest/prophet;
- the coercive side becomes increasingly anti-social/disreputable and eventually prohibited.

This is a **functional redivision inside one inherited actor-category**. The later institutional distinction is reconstructed from a prior composite role rather than projected unchanged backward.

## X. Local explanation cannot automatically become universal origin

Near the end Toy allows that, in some relatively advanced societies, `magic` may result from a less-developed population's superstitious interpretation of a higher imported religion. He immediately limits the range of that explanation: even if it works there, it cannot explain sorcery among the `savage tribes` where such a transmission route is implausible.

Thus:

`one plausible historical mechanism in one class of cases`

≠

`universal genealogy of the category`.

Toy's final conclusion rejects all three opening total models. The earliest known beliefs/practices contain germs of both religion and magic, which develop side by side and are differentially selected/organized as social organization changes.

## XI. AOS institutional/print ecology: direct but not an attendance edge

The 1899 AOS proceedings show that `Relation between Magic and Religion` was read at the Society's Cambridge meeting in a session expressly devoted to the **Section for the Historical Study of Religions**. In the same session:

- Charles Carroll Everett presented `The Psychology of the Vedanta and Sankhya Systems`;
- Toy presented `The Relation between Magic and Religion`;
- Morris Jastrow presented on the historical study of religions in American colleges/universities;
- Edward Washburn Hopkins presented on the economics of religion;
- other comparative-religion papers followed.

The proceedings also list Toy and Charles R. Lanman among AOS vice-presidents and Jastrow as secretary of the religion section.

This establishes a concrete American institutional ecology in which Indic philosophy, comparative religion, magic/religion classification and the disciplinary status of religious history were processed together.

Do **not** infer from this that Lovejoy attended the Cambridge session. The current AOS membership chronology is a separate control and membership itself is only a proxy for scholarly participation.
