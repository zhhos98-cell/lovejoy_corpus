## Core result

The full 1904 article materially strengthens the already documented 1898–1904 India/otherworldliness line.

The important new point is not merely that Lovejoy mentions Schopenhauer or Deussen. In pp. 144–148 he **uses Vedānta as a technical comparator inside the architecture of his own metaphysical analysis**. He first distinguishes two questions often collapsed under the vocabulary of immanence/transcendence:

1. whether the **being / “that”** of finite things is included in the Absolute;
2. whether the determinate **nature / “what”** of finite things is included in the Absolute.

He then gives Vedānta as an explicit example of a system in which the first inclusion is affirmed and the second denied: the universe may be Brahman in being while the determinations that make the universe what it is are not predicates of Brahman. The Mundaka Upanishad and the characterization of Brahman as without properties are deployed as evidence for the distinction. Schopenhauer is immediately redescribed as “modern Vedāntism.”

This means that by 1904 Indic material is not confined in Lovejoy’s corpus to the object-history of Buddhism or to a civilizational contrast. It has become an **analytical instrument for classifying Western metaphysical systems**.

At the same time, the article ends with a sharply asymmetrical civilizational judgment. Lovejoy says that a metaphysics which resolves contradiction by degrading temporal experience into illusion lapses into “metaphysical otherworldliness,” in which genuine reality and worth become the blank negation of common temporal experience, and concludes that no “sober Occidental mind” need refute such a philosophy. Thus the same article both **imports Vedānta into the technical comparative apparatus** and **marks illusionist otherworldliness as something from which an Occidental philosophical subject can distance itself**.

## 1. pp. 145–147: Lovejoy splits the immanence problem into two logically distinct questions

Lovejoy says that debates over whether a monistic Absolute is “immanent” or “transcendent” usually confound two problems. One concerns the **ontological locus or extension** of ultimate reality, the inclusion or exclusion of the mere being, the “that,” of phenomena. The other concerns **ontological kind**, the inclusion or exclusion of the determinate nature and content, the “what,” of phenomena.

This distinction matters because a system can claim that all finite beings are in the Absolute while also denying that their concrete predicates, plurality, temporality, and difference belong to the Absolute’s own nature.

That is a much more exact formulation than the generic immanence/transcendence contrast. It also aligns with Lovejoy’s recurrent proof discipline elsewhere in the pre-1905 corpus: he separates propositions that later interpreters often treat as one question.

Primary control: Lovejoy 1904, printed pp. 145–147.

---

## 2. p. 147: Vedānta is the explicit worked example of `that included / what excluded`

Lovejoy’s first named example of the distinction is Vedānta. He invokes the Mundaka Upanishad’s claim that “all this is Brahman” and immediately specifies what he thinks the statement does **not** mean: the determinations that make the universe what it is are not thereby properties of Brahman, because Brahman is characterized as without properties.

The analytical structure is therefore:

`finite things: esse / “that”` → included in Brahman

while

`finite things: determinate “what”` → excluded from Brahman’s own nature.

Lovejoy then calls Schopenhauer a modern Vedāntist and applies the same distinction to Will and representation.

This is stronger evidence than a decorative Oriental comparison. The Vedānta example performs logical work in the paper: it clarifies a distinction subsequently used to read Bradley, Neo-Platonism, Bruno, and Spinoza.

Primary control: printed p. 147.

Evidence ceiling: the article demonstrates Lovejoy’s use of a Vedāntic representation; it does not by itself identify which edition or translation of the Mundaka Upanishad he consulted for this sentence.

## 3. p. 144: Deussen and Māyā already sit inside Lovejoy’s map of modern metaphysical positions

Before formalizing the `that/what` distinction, Lovejoy tests the Erdmann interpretation of Spinoza. If Spinoza’s differentiated attributes and modes were only products of finite intelligence, Lovejoy says, Spinoza would fall into the same broad ontological family as Schopenhauer and **Paul Deussen**; natura naturata would become an analogue of the world as representation.

The unresolved question would then be what spreads the veil of **Māyā**, producing the apparent multiplicity that obscures primordial unity.

This passage is useful because Deussen is not introduced as a source for Indian doctrine in isolation. He is placed in a comparative lineage by which Lovejoy classifies a possible reading of Spinoza. Combined with the explicit Vedānta discussion three pages later, this is direct evidence that the Indic/Schopenhauer/Deussen complex remained active in Lovejoy’s technical philosophical vocabulary in 1904.

Primary control: printed p. 144.

---

## 4. pp. 146–147: the metaphysical distinction is explicitly tied to a practical-religious issue of temporal life

Lovejoy says the religiously important issue is not simply whether ultimate reality is metaphysically one or many. The live practical question is whether the supreme interests of spiritual life stand apart from the common activities and relationships of natural life, or are realized by entering more fully into **human business and temporal endeavor**.

Thus the article converts the ontology of immanence/transcendence into a value question about ordinary temporal life. This is a direct continuation of the structure already explicit in **“Religion and the Time-Process” (1902)**: metaphysical otherworldliness is not only a theory about what is most real, but a theory about what sort of temporal existence is genuinely valuable.

Primary control: printed pp. 146–147.

---

## 5. pp. 148–150: Lovejoy’s historical method treats contradiction as a motor of philosophical reconstruction

Lovejoy rejects the idea that metaphysics is primarily driven by a “passion for parsimony.” He instead describes much of the history of metaphysics as a repeated flight from actual or apparent **self-contradiction**. Philosophers reconstruct systems because inherited concepts cease to coexist peacefully and begin to conflict with their own assumptions or applications.

This is methodologically important for the later history-of-ideas project. His history is not a succession of labels. It asks why particular combinations of concepts become unstable and what new systems are generated when thinkers attempt to repair the instability.

This is exactly the mode of explanation the paper then applies across Neo-Platonism, medieval theology, Bruno, and Spinoza.

Primary control: printed pp. 148–150.

---

## 6. pp. 167–170: direct influence is acknowledged but analytically subordinated to a longer tradition

Lovejoy says the close affinity between Bruno and Spinoza is secure and that Spinoza read Bruno, so some direct influence should be admitted. Yet he immediately says that the **degree of direct influence is not the most important question**, because both thinkers appropriated dialectical methods, technical terms, and concepts from a much longer continuous Neo-Platonic tradition.

The evidentiary architecture is therefore layered:

`direct reading / influence` is one historical relation,

but

`shared inherited conceptual repertoire` can explain similarity even when the exact magnitude of one-to-one borrowing is uncertain.

This is highly compatible with the proof-level distinctions already documented in Lovejoy’s Buddhist and 1904 review work. Historical affinity, direct influence, and inherited tradition are not interchangeable explanatory claims.

Primary control: printed pp. 167–168.

---

## 7. pp. 170–173: contradiction must be reconstructed, not merely detected

Near the end Lovejoy states a methodological rule with unusual clarity. It is insufficient simply to record a philosopher’s claims without exposing their contradictions, but it is also insufficient merely to point out inconsistencies. Adequate analysis must show their **inevitability**, their **internal articulation**, their **dialectical sources**, and the philosopher’s own varying attitudes toward them.

His reading of Spinoza is therefore deliberately double-sided: Spinoza holds genuinely incompatible propositions, but the contradiction is not random incompetence. Both sides arise from the same underlying principle and therefore become historically and logically intelligible.

This is an early, direct warrant for a later Lovejoyan style of history: contradiction is an object to be explained by reconstructing the conceptual machinery that makes it recur.

Primary control: printed pp. 170–173.

---

## 8. pp. 173–174: the article ends by returning to `metaphysical otherworldliness`

Lovejoy’s final “moral” is directed against contemporary attempts to conceive an Absolute that is simultaneously genuinely absolute and inclusive of all reality. He argues that the contradiction between transcendence and inclusion repeatedly drives such systems toward an illusion theory.

The terminal diagnosis is **“metaphysical otherworldliness”**: what is truly real and genuinely valuable becomes not the fulfilment or explanation of common temporal experience but its blank negation.

This is a major continuity point. The 1904 Bruno–Spinoza article does not merely happen to share subject matter with the 1902 “Religion and the Time-Process.” It explicitly returns to the same analytic vocabulary at the point where it extracts a philosophical lesson from the historical reconstruction.

Primary control: printed pp. 173–174.

---

## 9. p. 174: the phrase “sober Occidental mind” creates a real civilizational asymmetry

The closing sentence says that this way of philosophizing is one which no **“sober Occidental mind”** need even undertake the easy task of refuting.

The safest reading is precise:

- the sentence does not say that all Oriental philosophy is illusionist;
- it does not say that Vedānta is the only target of the final polemic;
- the article’s historical examples are overwhelmingly Western and include Neo-Platonists, Schoolmen, Bruno, Spinoza, Bradley, and contemporary idealist monists.

But the adjective **Occidental** is still doing classificatory work. Lovejoy identifies the philosophical standpoint from which thoroughgoing metaphysical otherworldliness can be dismissed as specifically Occidental, even after using Vedānta positively as a technical comparator earlier in the article.

That produces a genuine asymmetry:

`Indic material can enter the comparative machinery of analysis`

while

`the authoritative judging subject is finally named Occidental`.

This deserves to be kept separate from stronger claims about race, empire, or political ideology unless additional evidence directly supplies them.

Primary control: printed p. 174.

## 10. Wilson 1982 independently places the article inside the genealogy of Lovejoy’s later history of ideas

Daniel J. Wilson’s annotated bibliography supplies a useful secondary control, but it should remain secondary to the 1904 text.

In entry 22 Wilson summarizes the Bruno–Spinoza article as tracing Neo-Platonic ideas through Plotinus, the Middle Ages, Bruno, and Spinoza, emphasizing both **otherworldliness** and **plenitude**. He explicitly says that, in the structure of its argument, its examples, and its concluding moral, the 1904 article **anticipates *The Great Chain of Being***.

Wilson’s introduction makes the connection even more explicit. He describes Lovejoy’s earliest history-of-ideas essays as attempts to understand the religious rejection of a temporal world and says that **“Religion and the Time-Process” (1902)** and **“The Dialectic of Bruno and Spinoza” (1904)** introduced Lovejoy to the complex of Platonic ideas later embodied in the chain of being.

This gives a historiographically useful distinction:

- **primary claim**: the 1904 article itself demonstrably contains the relevant analytical operations and vocabulary;
- **secondary genealogy**: Wilson retrospectively identifies these early works as formative predecessors of the later chain-of-being project.

Wilson controls: introduction, OCR JSON pp. 25–26 of the scan; primary-source entry 22, printed p. 14.

Wilson’s primary-source sequence is itself useful as a corpus map. The immediate run is:

- 1904 — “Religious Instruction in Non-Sectarian Colleges and Universities”;

This does not prove that these publications form a single unified project. It does show that the 1904 Bruno–Spinoza article sits in the same very narrow chronological window as Lovejoy’s work on international ethics, academic freedom/non-sectarian instruction, French/current philosophy, and pre-Darwinian evolution, after the 1902 temporal-religious synthesis and the 1898 Buddhist philology.
