# Batch 83 — Leuba 1912 does not merely critique Lovejoy: he re-proves the impersonal-power problem through child psychology

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced
Scope: deepen Batch 81's strongest early reception case, James H. Leuba, *A Psychological Study of Religion* (1912), by asking whether Leuba only accepts/rejects Lovejoy's theory or whether he reorganizes the proof itself.

## Core result

The close read materially upgrades Leuba from `method-aware critic` to a case of **reproof in reception**.

Leuba directly cites Lovejoy 1906, accepts the broad problem of a non-personal power prior to or independent of animism, but rejects Lovejoy's stronger claim that Wakonda/Manitou/Mana operates according to `quite regular and intelligible laws`. He then does something more consequential: rather than remain inside Lovejoy's linguistic/ethnographic proof packet, Leuba **changes evidentiary channel**.

After summarizing the philological/historical evidence through Lovejoy and Irving King, Leuba explicitly says he will not reproduce that evidence. For the problem of origins, the psychologist turns instead to **the child in order to supplement anthropological data**. He then asks what the earliest explanatory concept of a child is and argues from infant/child causal cognition that non-personal cause need not be derived from personification.

The sequence is therefore:

`Lovejoy / King: ethnographic + linguistic evidence for non-personal power`

→ Leuba accepts a minimal category but contests mechanical regularity

→ historical priority / psychological origin remains a stronger proposition

→ **new independent warrant: developmental psychology of the child**

→ Leuba argues that non-personal causation can arise independently of animistic personification and probably precedes it.

This is strikingly homologous to Batch 80's 005→1906 transformation:

- Lovejoy did not allow a mechanism inferred from custom simply to inherit that custom as its only warrant; he re-established selected mechanisms through native vocabulary/grammar.
- Leuba does not allow the ethnographic presence of impersonal power simply to settle its psychological origin; he supplements ethnography with child-development evidence.

The strongest formulation is:

> **The reception of `primitive energetics` generated its own warrant migration. Leuba retained the proposition-space while changing the evidence needed to decide it.**

Or, more generally:

> **reproof itself proved transmissible, even when Lovejoy's answer was not.**

This must remain a reception-structure claim, not a direct claim that Leuba consciously learned the `reproof` method from Lovejoy.

## 1. Direct Lovejoy citation and the first evidentiary split

Primary source:

James H. Leuba, *A Psychological Study of Religion: Its Origin, Function, and Future* (New York: Macmillan, 1912), chapter IV, especially printed pp.74–81.

Google Books full-view record: `C9oHkL4aGQ4C`.

Leuba introduces Lovejoy explicitly:

- `In the Monist for 1906, Arthur O. Lovejoy offers a criticism of Marett which deserves attention.`
- footnote: Arthur O. Lovejoy, `The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy`, *Monist* XVI (1906), 357–382.

He reproduces Lovejoy's contrast with Marett: the preanimistic datum is not fundamentally the abnormal/portentous but a force working under `quite regular and intelligible laws`, capable of study and control; hence `Primitive Energetics`.

Leuba immediately partitions the proposition:

- a non-personal power such as Wakonda/Manitou/Mana: **accepted as a serious ethnographic category**;
- `quite regular and intelligible laws`: **questioned**;
- definite stable relation between ritual means and effects: **not demonstrated**;
- therefore `mechanical Power`: **rejected/downgraded**;
- `magical force`: **preferred**.

This recapitulates the proof-level distinction already found in Lovejoy himself: existence of a category and characterization of its causal mode are different propositions.

---

## 2. Lovejoy becomes an evidence survey in Leuba's apparatus

Leuba then introduces Irving King's chapter VI, `The Mysterious Power`, as bringing together philological and other evidence for Manitou, Orenda, Wakonda, Mana and related non-personal potency.

His footnote is unusually revealing. A reader interested in the point, Leuba says, will find a **good summary of the evidence** either:

- in King's chapter VI;
- or in **Lovejoy's shorter article quoted above**.

Thus the 1906 paper is received in two capacities simultaneously:

1. as a theoretical claim about `primitive energetics`;
2. as a compressed evidentiary dossier.

This is precisely why Leuba can reject one higher-level conclusion while retaining the paper as a useful evidence source.

The source and the proposition have already been separated in reception.

---

## 3. Leuba explicitly declines to repeat the linguistic/historical dossier

After discussing Codrington and other historical/ethnographic material, Leuba says he will not attempt to set before the reader all the linguistic and historical evidence that might show belief in non-personal forces prior to animism.

This is methodologically important. He does not simply stop because the evidence is already known. His own target proposition is partly different.

Leuba's two theses are:

1. belief in non-personal powers is **not a derivative of animism nor merely a first step toward it**; the two beliefs have independent origins;
2. non-personal causation probably appeared before animism.

The second thesis is historical/developmental; the first is psychological/genealogical. A distributional survey of ethnographic words cannot by itself settle either origin problem.

The evidentiary burden therefore rises.

---

## 4. New warrant channel: `the psychologist ... turns naturally to the child`

Leuba's transition is explicit: the psychologist who seeks knowledge concerning origins turns naturally to **the child to supplement anthropological data**.

This is the crucial reception operation.

The book then asks:

> what are the first explanatory concepts of the child?

Leuba argues that an elementary idea of power/cause need not initially mean a personal power. At its simplest, `power` means whatever produces something. Before a young child has formed a reflective personified causal theory, it can already respond to the wind or another physical event as `that which` pushes, moves, or produces an effect.

He gives a child example: a child wanting to be lifted up to `see the wind`. Leuba asks why this should be taken to imply expectation of a human/animal causal agent. It is simpler, he argues, to suppose the child expects some thing/process associated with the observed push/movement.

### Inferential use

Child psychology is not merely illustrative. It is recruited as an **independent genetic control**:

`earliest child causal cognition`

→ shows logical/psychological possibility of non-personal cause without antecedent personification

→ supports independent origin of non-personal-power belief

→ makes temporal priority over animism psychologically plausible.

This is the strongest evidence yet that Lovejoy's reception could trigger a change in proof object rather than merely a change in terminology.

---

## 5. Leuba then refuses a unitary origin of god-ideas

The next chapter broadens the same methodological stance. Leuba argues against the assumption that god-ideas have one unitary source and proposes several independent origins of unseen personal beings.

His working propositions include:

- gods can arise from several different ideas of superhuman beings;
- these beings can have independent origins;
- the attributes of gods differ by origin;
- historical gods are often composite or `mongrel gods`, combining traits from distinct sources.

This is not a direct Lovejoy reception claim. It is relevant because it shows the larger inferential environment into which Lovejoy's non-personal-power problem is received: **plural origins + recombination rather than one universal genealogy**.

The homology with Lovejoy 005/1902 is substantial but should remain comparative unless direct borrowing evidence appears.

---

## 6. Leuba gives his own version of proposition-relative evidence

The book repeatedly distinguishes:

- whether a non-personal causal idea exists;
- whether it is mechanically regular;
- whether it is chronologically prior to animism;
- whether one concept is genetically derived from the other;
- whether an observed magical practice depends on dynamism at all.

These are not interchangeable propositions.

For example, Leuba explicitly says that some magical practices can be accounted for without either animism or dynamism, while other magic depends on non-personal power. He consequently refuses a unitary origin even for magic.

The structure is again proof-relative:

`practice exists`

≠

`practice requires impersonal-power ontology`

≠

`impersonal-power ontology derives from / leads to animism`

≠

`historical priority proven`.

This is very close in form to the Lovejoy ladder, though Leuba's substantive psychology and functional definition of religion are his own.

---

## 7. What Leuba receives and what he replaces

### Retained from Lovejoy / shared with the 1906 problem

- non-personal efficacy as a serious primitive category;
- need to distinguish personal from impersonal power;
- criticism of Marett's `supernormal` emphasis;
- Lovejoy's evidence packet as useful summary;
- recognition that causal characterization is an additional proposition.

### Rejected or modified

- `quite regular and intelligible laws` as an adequate characterization of the ethnographic evidence;
- `mechanical` as opposed to `magical` force;
- Lovejoy's preferred nomenclature `manitouism` is not Leuba's endpoint.

### Added / substituted

- functional-psychological differentiation of religion and magic;
- child-development evidence as an independent control on causal origin;
- an explicit multiple-origin model for personal divine beings;
- stronger concern with the psychological genesis of explanatory ideas.

This is reception by **recomposition**.

## 8. Connection to Batch 79: sources migrate by evidentiary job in reception too

Batch 79 showed source-function migration inside Lovejoy:

- Codrington custom/source dossier → semantic witness;
- MacDonald custom evidence → Hetherwick grammar for a stronger proposition;
- custom-rich sources deferred when publication requires lexical proof.

Leuba now repeats the same kind of functional reallocation at a higher level:

- Lovejoy/King become **summary sources for ethnographic existence evidence**;
- child psychology becomes the source channel for **genetic/origin evidence**;
- the same broad category is therefore carried by different witnesses for different propositions.

Formula:

> **ethnographic authority for existence; developmental psychology for genesis.**

That is a cleaner description than saying Leuba combined anthropology and psychology.

---

## 9. Connection to Batch 81: reception disaggregation now becomes reception reproof

Batch 81's model separated four components:

1. category;
2. causal thesis;
3. evidence dossier;
4. proof architecture.

Leuba's close read now shows that the fourth component does not simply `partially survive` in a passive sense. It becomes productive:

- Lovejoy's paper is accepted as evidence survey;
- Lovejoy's causal conclusion is contested;
- a stronger origin claim is identified as requiring another form of evidence;
- a new evidence channel is supplied.

Therefore Leuba's reception code should be upgraded from:

`proof architecture: PARTIAL PRESERVATION`

to:

`proof architecture: ACTIVE REPROOF / WARRANT SUBSTITUTION`.

Again, this is an analytical description of Leuba's published argument, not a pedagogical transmission claim.

---

## 10. Article-level payoff

A strong sentence is now possible:

> **Lovejoy's earliest readers did not simply decide whether `primitive energetics` was true. Leuba treated the 1906 article as a useful summary of ethnographic evidence while refusing its strongest causal characterization; when he turned from the existence of non-personal power to its psychological origin, he changed evidence altogether, supplementing anthropology with child development. Reception itself therefore reproduced the distinction between proposition and warrant.**

And a shorter analytical formula:

> **Lovejoy re-proved custom through language; Leuba re-proved anthropology through developmental psychology.**

The second sentence is rhetorically strong but should be followed by the first or another qualification, because it does not imply direct methodological borrowing.

## 11. Evidence cautions

1. Leuba's direct Lovejoy citation is secure in the 1912 published book.
2. The exact wording of Lovejoy's `quite regular and intelligible laws` is quoted by Leuba; publication-grade quotation should still be checked against a facsimile page if used verbatim.
3. The Google Books bibliographic object is full-view; the current searchable extraction used a public OCR reproduction as a locator/reading aid for pp.74–81.
4. `reproof`, `warrant migration`, `active reproof`, and `ethnographic authority for existence; developmental psychology for genesis` are our analytical formulations.
5. Do not infer that Leuba learned child-comparison method from Lovejoy. Developmental recapitulation/comparative child psychology had broader contemporary roots.
6. Do not collapse Leuba's distinction between `magical` and `mechanical` into Marett's distinction; Leuba's functional definition of religion/magic is separately argued.
