# Martin Guerre and the historical manufacture of a retrievable person — Bertillon, filing, and the reference-state problem

Date: 2026-09-03  
Status: **HISTORICAL RECURSION CONTROL / COMPARATIVE HISTORY OF KNOWLEDGE / NON-GENEALOGICAL**  
Companion: `martin_guerre_reference_state_manufacturing_witness_jurisdiction_nist_france_identite_2025_2026.md`

## Result

The `reference-state manufacturing` thesis is not merely a digital-age projection. Bertillon's late nineteenth-century identification system already reorganized person-knowledge around a three-part operation:

`registration` → `retrieval` → `comparison`.

This is the historical hinge that makes the Martin Guerre comparison more precise.

The key innovation was not simply a more accurate bodily sign. Bertillonage manufactured **retrievable prior persons**. A body encountered at time `t2` could be measured, converted into a standardized descriptor, used to search a systematically ordered archive made at `t1`, and then compared against the stored record. The system therefore moved evidentiary work upstream and made later re-identification depend on an earlier enrollment/inscription event.

The deep contrast is:

`Martin Guerre: reconstruct a prior person from socially distributed historical traces after continuity is disputed`

versus

`Bertillon: create a standardized retrievable record while the person is physically available, so later continuity disputes can be answered by archive search and comparison`.

This is the pre-digital form of the same temporal reorganization that current NIST identity proofing makes explicit.

## 1. From bodily resemblance to a searchable archive

Bertillonage combined:

- standardized bodily measurements;
- detailed descriptions of physical features and distinctive marks;
- frontal and profile photographs;
- a prescribed notation system;
- cards bearing those data;
- a filing and cross-indexing scheme that allowed a new measurement set to retrieve possible prior matches.

The US National Library of Medicine's *Visible Proofs* history of forensic identification describes Bertillon's system as the first integrated police method combining physical measurement, photography, and record-keeping. The measurements were converted into a formula referring to an individual, recorded on cards with frontal/profile portraits, then systematically filed and cross-indexed for retrieval.

That final archival step is decisive. A measurement becomes useful for re-identification only when it can function as an **address into an archive**.

The Science Museum's surviving Bureau of Identification cards make the material form visible: photographs, physical descriptors, measurements, facial peculiarities, and an institutional record number coexist on one standardized carrier.

Thus the historical object is not `the measurement` or `the mug shot` alone. It is an apparatus:

`body` → `measurement/description/photo` → `standardized card` → `index position` → `future retrieval`.

## 2. The reference state becomes deliberately future-oriented

This apparatus changes the temporal orientation of evidence.

A remembered scar in the Martin Guerre proceeding is evidence because someone recalls an earlier body and claims continuity with the claimant now present. The earlier state survives contingently in memory or scattered records.

A Bertillon card is produced **for the possibility of future re-encounter**. Its epistemic value is prospective at the moment of inscription even though it will later serve retrospective identification.

The system therefore manufactures what can be called a **future-addressable past**.

At `t1`, the institution anticipates a possible future question:

`Have we encountered this person before?`

It records the body in a form designed to be searchable at `t2`.

This is more exact than saying modern identification `records identity`. It produces **an archive optimized for later sameness judgments**.

## 3. Why the filing system matters more than photographic realism

Photography alone does not solve the retrieval problem. A large unsorted collection of faces is difficult to search, especially before automated image comparison. Bertillon's system worked by reducing bodily information into dimensions and classes that could place a record in a retrievable location.

This point has long been stressed in the critical literature on police photography: the power of the photographic identification archive comes from classification and filing, not merely from the photograph's visual resemblance.

The NLM account gives the operational sequence clearly: measurements were made into a formula, the formula referred to a single individual, cards were systematically filed/cross-indexed, and the stored photographs then helped confirm tentative matches.

A recent historical study of identification practices in colonial Vietnam describes the same logic: officers measured a newly encountered body, searched the archive of previously convicted individuals for a corresponding set of measurements, and used the photograph as a final confirmation. It cites Allan Sekula's well-known formulation that the central artifact was the filing cabinet rather than the camera.

For this dossier, the methodological point is:

> **Identification at distance requires not only a durable trace but an information architecture capable of retrieving the relevant prior trace.**

`reference-state manufacturing` therefore has two inseparable parts:

1. producing a record;
2. making that record addressable later.

## 4. Martin Guerre as a retrieval failure before retrieval systems

This lets us redescribe the sixteenth-century case without anachronistically calling it `bad biometrics`.

The problem was not that the court lacked bodily information. It received abundant descriptions and claims. The problem was that there was no standardized archive in which one could submit the current claimant's attributes and retrieve an independently generated prior record of Martin at useful resolution.

The court therefore had to perform something like **distributed retrieval through people**:

- locate people who had known Martin;
- elicit remembered features and episodes;
- compare those memories with the claimant;
- compare witnesses with one another;
- weigh intimacy, kinship, reputation, and contradiction;
- aggregate the result juridically.

Human witnesses functioned partly as **living indices to an absent past**, but each index had its own memory, incentives, relationships, and access conditions.

Bertillonage externalized part of this retrieval function into cards and filing rules.

This is a much sharper structural comparison than `eyewitness versus science`.

## 5. Registration is an epistemic intervention

A Bertillon record does not passively capture an already self-evident person. It constructs the institutional identity object that later searches can recover.

The process selects:

- which bodily dimensions count;
- how they are measured;
- how categories are divided;
- how photographs are standardized;
- which marks are noteworthy;
- how records are numbered;
- how cards are filed;
- how close a measurement must be to trigger comparison;
- which final signs confirm a match.

Thus registration is not merely storage. It is **model construction**.

The individual becomes institutionally retrievable by being decomposed into a finite set of standardized relations between body and archive.

This connects directly to the earlier `retrospective protocolization` finding. Bertillonage does prospectively to living bodies what later digital-ID writers can retrospectively do to Martin Guerre: convert heterogeneous personhood into a finite protocol of comparable slots.

The difference is that Bertillon's protocol is used to manufacture the record before the future dispute.

## 6. Fingerprints change the identifier, not the whole epistemic architecture

Fingerprint identification displaced much of bertillonage because anthropometric measurement was labor-intensive, technically demanding, and vulnerable to measurement variation. The NLM account notes that even trained officers could produce different measurements and that bodies could change with age. Fingerprinting spread after workable classification systems made prints usable at scale.

The phrase `classification system` is essential. A unique fingerprint pattern is operationally weak if institutions cannot file and retrieve records efficiently.

So the transition should not be written:

`fallible measurements` → `infallible fingerprints`.

A better sequence is:

`standardized inscription + archive + retrieval architecture`

with

`the preferred bodily index changing from anthropometric vector to fingerprint pattern`.

Simon Cole's *Suspect Identities* is particularly important here because it rejects the retrospective myth that fingerprints simply arrived as self-evident scientific truth. Institutional adoption, classification, expertise, administrative convenience, and public claims to certainty all mattered.

The reference-state architecture persists even when the carrier changes.

## 7. Registration → retrieval → comparison as a durable operator

We can now identify a trans-period operator that survives substantial technological change.

### Bertillon

`measure/photo at t1`

→ `file standardized card`

→ `measure unknown/repeat offender at t2`

→ `retrieve candidate card by classification`

→ `confirm against photograph/features`.

### Fingerprint bureau

`capture prints at t1`

→ `classify and file/index print record`

→ `capture prints at t2`

→ `retrieve candidate record`

→ `compare ridge detail`.

### Contemporary digital proofing

`prior issuer proofs/enrolls subject`

→ `issues evidence / authoritative record`

→ `later CSP collects applicant evidence`

→ `validates against issuing/authoritative source`

→ `verifies ownership`

→ `creates subscriber state`

→ `future authentication / identity matching`.

The changing technologies should therefore be nested inside the more durable information operation:

**registration → addressability → retrieval → comparison → authorized re-identification.**

## 8. From one archive to recursive networks of reference states

Bertillon's system is comparatively centralized: the institutional problem is to create and retrieve a record within a police archive or interoperable police system.

Contemporary digital identity is more recursive and federated. A CSP may validate a driver's license against the issuing DMV, inherit attributes from another authoritative source, create its own subscriber account, bind authenticators, and later federate an assertion to a relying party.

Thus the nineteenth-to-twenty-first-century shift is partly:

`single institutional archive as reference state`

→ `network of institutions inheriting and reissuing reference-state authority`.

This is why `recursive reference-state manufacturing` is still needed as a separate modern operator. Bertillon provides the strong historical control for **prospective registration and retrieval**, while NIST/France Identité reveal the later layering and federation of reference states.

## 9. Record provenance: an important difference

A Bertillon card stores what was measured and observed, plus institutional identifiers and photographs. Contemporary NIST subscriber accounts are explicitly required to retain the **proofing pathway** itself: evidence issuer, proofing type, validation/verification methods, exception/referee use, applicant references, assurance level, authenticators and validated attributes.

This suggests a further historical development:

`record of the person`

→ `record of the person plus record of how the identity claim was warranted`.

This is not absolute; historical bureaucracies also preserved procedural metadata, signatures, clerks, dates, and chains of custody. But modern digital standards make proof-path provenance an explicit component of the reusable identity object.

That should be tested historically rather than assumed as a clean break.

## 10. The archive solves one problem by creating another

Reference-state systems increase re-identification capacity but also create new epistemic failure modes:

- measurement error;
- incorrect initial enrollment;
- duplicate or merged records;
- classification error;
- false retrieval;
- bureaucratic mismatch;
- stolen or forged documents;
- compromised credentials;
- biased or unstable biometric comparison;
- inaccessible records;
- records that persist after the person or circumstances change.

The relevant history is therefore not increasing certainty but **changing error topology**.

Martin Guerre's court risks accepting the wrong person because social evidence is distributed and contested. Bertillon's bureau can fail because the reference record or measurements are wrong or cannot be retrieved. Digital identity can fail because a false claimant is successfully enrolled, because inherited evidence is wrong, or because credentials/reference infrastructure are compromised.

Each regime shifts where uncertainty accumulates.

## 11. New analytical term: historical addressability

### Definition

**Historical addressability** = the capacity of an institution to transform a person encountered in the present into a query that can retrieve an earlier institutional representation of that same person.

This concept captures what Martin Guerre lacked and Bertillonage deliberately built.

A reference state without addressability can exist but remain practically useless. A village may contain memories of a person, but the process of discovering which memories matter is costly and socially contingent. Bertillon's classification system turns bodily measures into an archive address. Modern identifiers, account numbers, cryptographic keys, biometrics, and identity-matching services do related work at different scales.

Historical addressability should therefore be separated from:

- **identity** — the person/category claimed;
- **recording** — production of a trace;
- **accuracy** — correctness of that trace;
- **authentication** — proof of entitlement in a later transaction.

It is specifically about the **retrieval relation between present claimant and prior state**.

## 12. A revised history-of-knowledge sequence

The Martin Guerre control can now be expressed as a sequence of operations rather than technologies:

### Artigat

`distributed prior knowledge`

→ `continuity breaks through absence`

→ `court reconstructs reference state ad hoc`

→ `human/social retrieval`

→ `comparison + juridical aggregation`.

### Bertillon / early scientific police identification

`controlled encounter at t1`

→ `standardized bodily inscription`

→ `classified/filed reference record`

→ `future body converted to query`

→ `archive retrieval`

→ `comparison / confirmation`.

### Digital identity

`prior institutional records/evidence`

→ `resolution + validation + verification`

→ `new enrollment/subscriber state`

→ `authenticator binding / federation`

→ `future remote authentication or identity matching`

with explicit proof-path provenance and exception handling.

The long transformation is therefore not simply:

`memory → paper → biometric → digital`.

It is:

**`distributed recollection → manufactured addressability → recursively reusable assurance`.**

## 13. Why this matters for the twenty-first-century reappearance

This historical control explains why Martin Guerre is so attractive to digital-ID writers. The case is not just a colorful example of fraud. It dramatizes what identification infrastructures are designed to precompute:

- a prior, independent reference;
- a way to retrieve it;
- a method for comparing claimant and reference;
- a rule for deciding how much correspondence is enough;
- an authority capable of making the result consequential.

Later writers can therefore project modern protocols backward because the affair already contains the negative form of the same problem.

The history-of-knowledge task is to resist treating that negative form as evidence that sixteenth-century actors were `doing primitive MFA`. They were solving a relation that can later be re-described in MFA terms, but the evidentiary jurisdictions and social ontology were different.

## 14. Strongest compressed formulation

**Bertillonage confirms that the decisive historical transformation between Martin Guerre and digital identity is not the invention of an objectively identifying body. It is the manufacture of a retrievable prior person. The Artigat court had to discover its reference state while adjudicating the claim, using people as living indices to an absent past. Bertillon's late nineteenth-century system deliberately captured measurements, photographs and distinctive marks during a controlled encounter, reduced them to standardized cards, and filed them so that a later body could be converted into a query, retrieve a candidate past record, and be compared against it. Fingerprinting changed the bodily index but retained the registration–classification–retrieval architecture; current digital identity adds recursive institutional inheritance, proof-path provenance, and reusable subscriber states. The long history is therefore better expressed as distributed recollection → manufactured historical addressability → recursively reusable assurance.**

2. **Record provenance:** determine how much nineteenth-/early-twentieth-century identification cards and dossiers recorded the procedure/agent/date of enrollment and comparison. This tests whether NIST's proof-path provenance is genuinely new in degree or merely newly standardized.
