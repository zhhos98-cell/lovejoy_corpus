# Lovejoy — semantic drift of `logical` and jurisdiction across contexts, 1902–1941

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: **CANONICAL SEMANTIC CONTROL / CORRECTION TO UNIFORM-METHOD LANGUAGE**

## Question

The live question is not simply whether Lovejoy used `logic` or `logical analysis`. It is whether the same vocabulary and the same operation meant the same thing when he moved among formal logic, philosophical controversy, history of philosophy, philology/religion, political classification, and mature history of ideas.

This dossier therefore distinguishes **word recurrence** from **method identity**.

> The recurrence of `logical` is evidence of an actor-level analytical vocabulary. It is not by itself evidence that one stable formal-logical technique migrated unchanged across genres.

Read with `Lovejoy_logic_object_vs_logical_method_context_matrix_2026-08-28.md`. That file controls the three-way distinction between logic-as-object, actor-explicit logical method, and analyst-imputed logic-shaped practice.

## 1. 1902: `logical import` = conceptual consequence, contrasted with temperament and transmission

In `Religion and the Time-Process`, Lovejoy's object is philosophical/theological history, not formal logic. `Logical import` marks what follows from or belongs to the conceptual content of a position. Lovejoy simultaneously distinguishes this from the temperamental attraction of a doctrine and from historical transmission or Oriental borrowing.

Thus the local relation-set is at least:

`conceptual/logical consequence`
≠ `psychological/temperamental affinity`
≠ `historical transmission`.

The important point is already jurisdictional: one relation does not prove another.

Coding: `A2 explicit-logical-method / P+H`.

---

## 2. 1904: `logically and historically` = two coordinates of philosophical location

In `The Dialectic of Bruno and Spinoza`, Lovejoy decomposes what is conventionally treated as one problem into distinguishable questions and closes by locating Spinoza `both logically and historically`.

Here `logical` does not mean formal proposition calculus. It names a conceptual/dialectical location within a problem-space, while `historically` names source, succession, transmission, or position in an intellectual development.

Local relation-set:

`logical/dialectical location`
≠ `historical/genealogical location`.

Coding: `A2 / P+H`.

---

## 3. 1907: formal-logician standpoint inside the history of logic

`Kant's Classification of the Forms of Judgment` is a mixed case. Lovejoy subjects a canonical logical classification to formal-logical scrutiny while reconstructing its historical relation to predecessor German logics.

Here `logical` has a stronger technical sense than in 1902/1904 because the object itself is a logical table and the test concerns classificatory consistency, ambiguity, and completeness.

Yet the paper is also historical. It therefore already prevents a simple equation:

`logical audit` = `historical explanation`.

Coding: mixed `A1 / L+H`.

---

## 4. 1908: `logically independent` = anti-transfer among propositions under one movement-name

In `The Thirteen Pragmatisms`, logical independence functions as a rule about argumentative transfer. Pragmatist contentions may share a historical label while acceptance or refutation of one does not automatically carry over to the others.

Local function:

`shared historical name`
≠ `logical dependence`
≠ `shared argumentative fate`.

This is a proposition-level use of `logical`, not a claim that the historical movement itself is a formal-logical object.

Coding: `A2 / P`.

---

## 5. 1909: `logical analysis and historical comparison` = explicitly coordinated but distinct procedures

`Pragmatism and Realism` provides the strongest early actor-level bridge. A historically real stream of tendency is first retained as a historical object and then subjected to logical analysis and historical comparison. Compatible constituents may be recombined, and historical novelty can lie in the compound rather than the ingredients.

The wording is decisive because Lovejoy **coordinates** two procedures instead of identifying them:

`historical reference`
→ `logical analysis`
+ `historical comparison`
→ `compatibility/recombination`
→ claim about historical distinctiveness.

If logical analysis already exhausted historical analysis, `historical comparison` would be redundant. The text therefore supports methodological pluralism more strongly than a one-method migration story.

Coding: `A2 / P+H`.

---

## 6. 1910: formal logic = denotative relation-space and inference

In `The Treatment of 'Opposition' in Formal Logic`, logic itself is the object. Lovejoy uses Vennian resources and relation-cases to revise the traditional doctrine of opposition.

Local function:

`possible denotative relations`
→ `compatibility/incompatibility of propositions`
→ `valid inferential consequences`.

This is the strongest technical-formal use in the sequence and should serve as a **control case**, not as the default meaning of every later occurrence of `logical`.

Coding: `A1 / L`.

---

## 7. 1911: formal implication has a bounded jurisdiction

`Existence and Formal Logic` is the most important anti-unification control. Lovejoy distinguishes:

`formal implication`
≠ `meaning/intelligibility`
≠ `verification/evidence`
≠ `existential or historical truth`.

Formal logic can specify formal relations without settling whether a proposition about the world or past is true, evidentially supported, or even presently verifiable.

This means the corpus itself contains an actor-level warning against treating formal logic as a universal method that automatically governs all historical inference.

Coding: `A1 / L`, with explicit jurisdiction boundary.

---

## 8. 1915: `unit` becomes historiographical scale, not visibly a formal-logical atom

Lovejoy's review of Thilly discusses the historian's choice of unit and a scale from system to problem to separate ideas/dialectical motifs recurring in combinations. The operation is decompositional, but the object is historiography/history of philosophy.

Here the relevant question is not whether the unit can be represented analytically. It is what licenses recurrence or identity across historical contexts.

Coding: primarily `A3 / H` unless actor-explicit logical vocabulary is present in the specific passage under examination.

This is precisely where `formal-logical unit` must not be silently substituted for `historiographical unit`.

---

## 9. 1936: `logical self-consciousness` and tacit presupposition

In *The Great Chain of Being*, Lovejoy asks the historian to recover implicit assumptions and mental habits that historical actors themselves may not have scrutinized with `logical self-consciousness`.

Here `logical` is metacognitive/analytical: explicit examination of assumptions and implications. It is neither the object of formal logic nor direct evidence that unit-ideas are formal-logical atoms.

The passage instead establishes a distinction between:

`actor's explicit doctrine`
≠ `tacit presupposition / mental habit`
≠ `historian's analytical reconstruction`.

Coding: `A2 / H` at the level of analytical reconstruction, with caution about actor attribution.

---

## 10. 1940: historical circulation and disciplinary permeability

`Reflections on the History of Ideas` emphasizes that the departmentalization of the history of thought does not correspond to real cleavages among intellectual phenomena; ideas migrate and interact across disciplinary provinces.

This supplies a mature reason for cross-domain history, but it does **not** by itself make the relations among ideas logical relations. Migration, circulation, influence, interaction, implication, and psychological association remain analytically distinguishable.

The mature programme is therefore cross-disciplinary without being relation-monist.

Coding: `H`, relation status to be specified claim by claim.

---

## 11. 1941: Lovejoy explicitly partitions logical, psychological, and historical/genetic relations

`The Meaning of Romanticism for the Historian of Ideas` supplies the strongest mature control for the present branch.

After semantic discrimination of the ideas associated with recurrent terms, Lovejoy tells the historian to examine **three different kinds of relation** among ideas:

1. logical;
2. psychological;
3. historical, especially genetic.

The crucial epistemic qualification is that logical and hypothetical psychological relations are initially products of the historian's analysis/construction. They function as tentative hypotheses. The historian must then return to historical evidence to determine whether the reconstructed logical relations were actually operative and whether the supposed psychological relations can be observed in historical actors.

Therefore:

`analyst-level logical implication/opposition`
≠ `actor-recognized implication`
≠ `historically operative relation`
≠ `historical/genetic connection`.

An unrecognized implication may itself become a negative historical fact requiring explanation; it does not automatically become a doctrine held by the actor.

This is the mature statement that most strongly vindicates the context correction. Lovejoy does not present `logical` as a universal name for historical relation. He explicitly treats logical, psychological, and historical/genetic relations as differently typed and differently warranted.

Coding: `A2 / H`, but at a meta-methodological level: the historian uses logical analysis while simultaneously policing its historical jurisdiction.

## 12. Semantic map: the word `logical` does several jobs

Current working taxonomy:

| Sense | Typical context | What `logical` is doing | What it does **not** establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| `L1 formal` | 1907/1910/1911 logic papers | proposition/class relations, implication, opposition, classificatory rigor | empirical or historical truth |
| `L2 conceptual import` | 1902 | consequence/content of a doctrine | temperament or transmission |
| `L3 dialectical location` | 1904 | position of a doctrine in a conceptual problem-space | historical source/genealogy |
| `L4 independence/compatibility` | 1908–09 | whether propositions stand/fall together; whether components can recombine | identity of a historical movement by itself |
| `L5 analytical self-consciousness` | 1936 | explicit scrutiny of assumptions/presuppositions | actor recognition of every implication |
| `L6 historian-constructed logical relation` | 1941 | hypothesis about implication/opposition among ideas | historical/genetic operation until evidence is checked |
| `L7 analyst-imputed logic-shaped` | notebooks/1906/etc. | our description of relation partition or warrant control | actor-level use of logic unless separately evidenced |

The same adjective therefore ranges from technical formal logic to conceptual analysis to historiographical hypothesis. A lexical hit for `logical` must always be assigned a local sense before it can support genealogy.

## 13. Revised continuity claim

The evidence now supports a more differentiated hypothesis than `Lovejoy carried a logical method into history`.

Possible person-level continuity:

> **Lovejoy repeatedly separates relation-types and restricts what one kind of analysis can warrant; the local content and technical meaning of `logical` change with the object and disciplinary setting.**

Possible deeper metarule:

> **typed relations + inferential jurisdiction**: do not let implication become genealogy, affinity become transmission, semantic resemblance become identity, or classificatory inclusion become chronology without an additional warrant.

This metarule is compatible with multiple disciplinary sources and local techniques. It is therefore a better target for genealogy than a single transported formal-logical procedure.

But even this continuity remains to be historicalized. Two endpoints or structural recurrence do not demonstrate one continuous causal lineage.

---

## 14. What would actually prove portability?

A strong portability claim requires at least one of the following:

1. actor-explicit transfer language (`the same method`, `as in logic`, etc.);
2. recurring technical vocabulary used with demonstrably stable meaning across genres;
3. shared citations/source genealogy across the contexts;
4. pedagogical or institutional evidence linking a learned technique to later use;
5. a sufficiently distinctive operation whose recurrence cannot be explained by the ordinary repertoire of each local field.

Absent such evidence, similarity remains comparison rather than transmission.
