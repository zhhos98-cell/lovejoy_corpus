# Morris Jastrow Jr. 1893 — pre-Lovejoy Harvard source-specialism architecture

Date: 2026-08-26
Status: **DIRECT PRIMARY / PRE-LOVEJOY HARVARD INSTITUTIONAL-METHOD CONTROL / PERIOD PROGRESSIVISM FIREWALL ACTIVE**

## Core result

Morris Jastrow Jr.'s `Recent Movements in the Historical Study of Religions in America`, *The Biblical World* 1.1 (January 1893), 24–32, is now directly controlled from the public-domain scan. It materially strengthens the Harvard side of the Lovejoy project because it predates Lovejoy's arrival at Harvard in 1895 and already describes the exact institutional configuration into which he would later enter.

Jastrow presents Harvard's historical study of religions as a **general-comparative layer supplemented by source-bound specialisms**:

- Charles Carroll Everett: regular lectures on the Comparative History of Religions;
- Crawford H. Toy: Hebrew religion, comparatively related to other Semitic religions;
- Charles Rockwell Lanman: Sacred Books of Buddhism;
- further religion-specific work distributed among Greek, Germanic, Christian and ancient Near Eastern specialists.

Lovejoy's independently documented 1896–97 course trajectory later crosses precisely Everett, Toy and Lanman.

The strongest result is therefore chronological as well as institutional:

> **Before Lovejoy arrived at Harvard, the three teachers who would structure his comparative-religion, Hebrew-religion and Buddhist-text training were already publicly represented as parts of a distributed history-of-religions ecology joining general comparison to source-specialist study.**

This removes the chronology problem attached to the 1899 Jastrow control. The 1899 article remains useful as a later explicit `axiom` formulation, but the architecture itself is direct-primary visible by January 1893.

## I. Historical study versus philosophical system-building

Jastrow opens by distinguishing religion as an old subject of speculation from religion as a recent object of historical investigation. He says earlier hostility or confessional attachment had prevented the `impartial and broad investigation of facts` required for historical study, and that even after a historical frame of mind emerged, religion remained too closely bound to philosophical systems such as Hegel's for its specifically historical aspect to come fully forward.

He attributes the newer field's momentum chiefly to two kinds of work:

1. research into the history and literatures of the ancient Orient;
2. investigation of widely distributed institutions and customs connected with religious phenomena.

This is an early actor-declared pairing of **textual/Oriental source work** and **cross-cultural institutional/anthropological evidence**.

## II. Paris `Sciences Religieuses` is already the concentrated institutional comparator

Jastrow surveys European institutionalization and singles out France:

- 1880 chair for the History of Religions at the Collège de France;
- 1886 `Section des Sciences Religieuses` at the École des Hautes Études with a substantial faculty;
- *Revue de l'Histoire des Religions*;
- Musée Guimet.

He explicitly says American development is partly responding to European activity.

This is six years before Lovejoy's documented 1898–99 participation in Marillier's EPHE Vth Section seminar. Thus the Paris infrastructure later entered by Lovejoy was already a visible institutional model in American discipline-building discourse before his Harvard training began.

Do not infer that Jastrow caused Lovejoy's Paris decision.

## III. Harvard in 1893: general comparison + single-religion/source specialisms

Jastrow calls Harvard the first American university, so far as he knows, to introduce the subject into its curriculum.

His description is structurally precise:

- Everett had for years lectured on the **Comparative History of Religions**;
- the `general aspects` had more recently been **supplemented by courses of a special character dealing with a single religion or subdivision**;
- Toy taught Hebrew religion with comparison to other Semitic religions, plus Islam/Qurʾān;
- Lanman taught the Sacred Books of Buddhism;
- Allen taught Greek religion/worship;
- Kittredge taught Icelandic sagas/Edda and Germanic mythology;
- Emerton treated early Christianity versus paganism;
- Lyon's courses substantially treated Babylonian/Assyrian religions.

This is not a retrospective reconstruction from course catalogues. A contemporary advocate explicitly describes the structure as:

`general comparative history`
+
`religion-specific specialist work`.

## IV. Lovejoy later enters exactly this pre-existing three-teacher configuration

Independent Lovejoy evidence already confirms that in 1896–97 he studied:

- comparative religion / psychological basis of religion with Everett;
- Hebrew religion with Toy;
- Sanskrit and sacred Buddhist texts with Lanman.

The 1893 Jastrow article therefore changes the interpretation of those courses. They should not be narrated merely as three individual interests Lovejoy happened to combine.

Safe historical formulation:

> **Lovejoy's course combination reproduces an institutional architecture that a contemporary observer had already described before his arrival: Everett supplied the general/comparative layer, while Toy and Lanman represented source-bound religion-specific work.**

This is institutional structure, not evidence that the three teachers coordinated Lovejoy's curriculum or jointly taught a common method.

## V. Pre-Lovejoy source jurisdiction is explicit in Jastrow's conclusion

The article ends by advocating departments modeled on the French school through the cooperation of as many specialists as possible.

Jastrow directly states:

- general and comparative aspects must not be neglected;
- they gain their value for the student only in connection with careful and prolonged study of a **particular religion**;
- such special study implies **knowledge of the sources**;
- guidance through that study lies within the **province of the specialist**.

This is the key direct-primary result.

The relation is:

`particular historical religion`
→ `careful prolonged study`
→ `knowledge of sources`
→ `specialist jurisdiction`

while

`general/comparative aspect`
→ acquires value through relation to that first-order study.

Thus the source-jurisdiction architecture recovered in Jastrow 1899 is not a new late formulation produced after Lovejoy's 1898 paper. Its basic form is already public in 1893.

## VI. The 1899 control becomes a sharpening, not the origin

Jastrow 1899 later states more formally that:

- it is `almost an axiom` that one person cannot properly cover the historical study of religions;
- source domains must be assigned to Indologists, Semitic/Egyptian/etc. specialists and anthropologists;
- no particular religion should be studied except through direct recourse to sources;
- general/comparative study occupies another level.

The best before/after reading is now:

### 1893

`general comparison + prolonged particular study + source knowledge + specialist province`.

### 1899

same architecture made more explicit as a university division of labor and stated through `axiom` language.

Do not claim a linear unchanged doctrine without further comparison of Jastrow's intervening writings. For the Lovejoy project, 1893 is enough to establish pre-existing availability and institutional structure.

## VII. Cambridge History of Religions Club predates Lovejoy

Jastrow records that during winter 1891–92 a **History of Religions Club** was formed at Cambridge, largely composed of Harvard professors and members of learned institutions in Boston. Meetings were monthly, with papers followed by general discussion.

This directly confirms that the Harvard/Boston history-of-religions ecology had a recurring learned-society form before Lovejoy's arrival.

It upgrades the significance of the independently known Harvard University Archives `History of Religions Club records, 1892–1952` as a high-value institutional source series.

No current evidence establishes Lovejoy membership or attendance. Do not infer it from his coursework.

## VIII. American Committee and Toy's organizational position

Jastrow also records the formation of the `American Committee for Lectures in the History and the Comparative Study of Religions` after meetings in Philadelphia and New York.

The permanent committee elected:

- Crawford H. Toy as chairman;
- Morris Jastrow as secretary.

Other named supporters/participants include Lanman, Brinton, Haupt, Lyon and others.

The planned lecture circuit was intended to take history-of-religions research to several cities and publish the lectures.

This directly predates the later lecture sequence already used elsewhere in the repo: Rhys Davids on Buddhism, Brinton on `primitive peoples`, Cheyne on Jewish religion, etc.

Again, institutional availability does not establish Lovejoy attendance at any lecture series.

## IX. A material-display branch: museum as developmental tableau

Jastrow calls the museum an indispensable adjunct to the study of religions and says its ideal form should present a **tableau of the course taken by religious rites in their development**. He points to the U.S. National Museum / Smithsonian and a University of Pennsylvania loan exhibition spanning Egypt, India, China, Japan, America and Islam.

This is potentially relevant to the larger project on stratification/display: historical development could be materially organized in museum space as well as narratively in texts.

For the Lovejoy corpus this remains contextual only. No direct Lovejoy museum-use or uptake edge is established by this article.

## X. Historical method and disciplinary setting

Jastrow's survey of the School of Applied Ethics says its underlying motives could differ, but `the method to be pursued remains the same`: the historical point of view governed both the selection and treatment of subjects. Toy headed its Department of Religions and gave an eighteen-lecture introductory course on the history, aims and method of the science of History of Religions, supplemented by specialist courses including Buddhism.

This supplies another direct pre-Lovejoy example of the field being conceived as:

`general disciplinary/method introduction`
+
`specialist religion-specific components`.

The existence/content of Toy's eighteen lectures beyond Jastrow's synopsis is not directly recovered here; do not reconstruct their method from Toy 1899 or 1913 texts.

## XI. Period progressivism is built into the programme

Jastrow's concluding confidence in historical research rests partly on what he calls the `steadily upward tendency of religious thought`, commensurate with the general advance of mankind.

Elsewhere the American curriculum he surveys includes Brinton's `Elements and Evolution of Primitive Religions`, and the museum ideal explicitly organizes rites by a developmental course.

Thus specialist/source discipline coexists with a large developmental totalization.

This is crucial to the current project model:

> **fine-grained source specialization did not oppose civilizational staging. The same institutional programme could demand specialist source competence at the local level while placing religions and rites inside a progressive developmental history at the general level.**

This is a direct historical analogue to the later project tension `decomposition can generate / coexist with totalization`.

Do not infer that Lovejoy adopted Jastrow's progressive hierarchy. Lovejoy's own exact 1906 political/racial/stage semantics remain blocked by MD-001.
