# Batch 23 — 1898 was not the end: Lovejoy's strict-core Buddhist/Indian-philosophy continuity, 1898–1907

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced

## Research move

This batch deliberately recentres the project on **Arthur O. Lovejoy as an Orientalist**, rather than allowing the Marillier/005 workshop reconstruction to become the research object in its own right. Notebook 005 remains important evidence for one comparative-religion formation, but the central historical question is now: **how long, in what forms, and at what levels of specialization did Lovejoy continue to work with Asian religions and Indian philosophy after his 1898 Buddhist philological publication?**

A useful historiographical foil has surfaced. Klaus Karttunen's *Persons of Indian Studies* includes Lovejoy as a person connected with Indian studies, cites his 1898 JAOS article, and then states that he **“Apparently never returned to Buddhist studies.”** That formulation is understandable if `Buddhist studies` means another dedicated specialist philological article. It is too compressed if read as intellectual disengagement from Buddhism or Indian philosophy. The current evidence establishes a sequence through at least 1907.

## 1. 1898: specialist Buddhist philology, not a casual comparative citation

Lovejoy's article:

> Arthur O. Lovejoy, “The Buddhistic Technical Terms upādāna and upādisesa,” *Journal of the American Oriental Society* 19 (1898), 126–136.

The full primary text is available in the NTU Buddhist Digital Library. Its level of practice is clearly specialist:

- it examines Pāli technical vocabulary and the relation of `upādāna`, `upādi/upādisesa`, the skandhas and dependent origination;
- it cites and disputes Senart, Oldenberg, Childers, Rhys Davids and Warren;
- it works directly through passages in the Sutta Piṭaka / Saṃyutta Nikāya and Sutta Nipāta;
- it distinguishes Buddhist psychology and soteriology from Vedāntic metaphysical interpretation;
- it explicitly argues that borrowed terms or ideas can form an original and characteristic arrangement.

This is strict-core Orientalist evidence in the narrow sense: textual terminology, Indian philosophical differentiation, source languages and a specialist learned-society venue.

## 2. 1901: Buddhism becomes teaching material

Daniel J. Wilson's annotated bibliography records a primary item that remains physically unrecovered:

> *Syllabus: The Philosophy of Buddhism*. The Washington University Association, 1901.

Wilson gives four lecture titles:

1. `The Place of Buddhism among the Philosophies of India`;
2. `Two Essential Formulas of Buddhism`;
3. `Two Essential Formulas of Buddhism`;
4. `Buddhist Ethics and its Goal`.

Even at bibliographic level, this matters. Three years after the JAOS paper Lovejoy was not merely remembering a former philological interest: he was organizing Buddhism pedagogically **inside the philosophies of India**, around doctrinal formulas and ethical/soteriological ends.

Evidence limit: the syllabus itself has not yet been recovered, so Wilson remains the locator for its wording and structure. The Washington University Association *Bulletin* began only later, making a standalone syllabus/lecture handout the more likely physical form.

## 3. 1902: Buddhism and Vedānta become a first comparative case in category-history

Lovejoy's full primary article `Religion and the Time-Process`, *American Journal of Theology* 6 (1902), 439–472, has already been recovered in the project; JSTOR independently identifies the article and page range as freely accessible.

The important continuity is authorial rather than merely thematic. Lovejoy makes **Vedānta and Buddhism** the first large comparative case in a paper about historically changing categories/presuppositions and explicitly sends the reader back to his own 1898 `upādāna/upādisesa` article.

Thus the 1898 paper was still part of his active argumentative apparatus in 1902. What changes is genre and level:

> **1898 — technical Buddhist philology → 1901 — Buddhist teaching → 1902 — comparative history of philosophical/religious categories.**

This is a transformation of Orientalist training, not a disappearance of it.

## 4. 1907: current Vedānta scholarship remains inside Lovejoy's reviewing practice

PhilPapers confirms a two-page Lovejoy review:

> Arthur O. Lovejoy, review of Paul Deussen, *Outline of the Vedānta System of Philosophy, According to Shankara*, *Journal of Philosophy, Psychology and Scientific Methods* 4.1 (1907), 23–24.

The reviewed English work is associated with the James Haughton Woods / Catharine Bird Runkle translation. A public-domain route to the 1907 journal volume is known through HathiTrust / Internet Archive, but Lovejoy's exact review text still awaits direct extraction.

Wilson's secondary synopsis says Lovejoy regarded the English outline as a useful `convenience` for teachers of Hindu philosophy and urged scholarship on **Rāmānuja's Vedānta** rather than continued concentration on Śaṅkara. Until the primary two pages are extracted, the exact wording must remain secondary.

If confirmed, the analytical significance is substantial: Lovejoy is not treating `Vedānta` as an indivisible doctrine. He is asking for differentiation within the tradition, specifically beyond the dominance of Śaṅkara/Advaita. That is strict-core evidence of engagement with current Indian-philosophy scholarship nine years after the 1898 Buddhist article.

## 5. Historiographical correction: define continuity by forms of practice

Karttunen's `Apparently never returned to Buddhist studies` should therefore be retained as a useful **historiographical limit statement**, not simply dismissed.

### Narrow reading that remains defensible

After 1898 we have not yet identified another dedicated article in which Lovejoy performs the same kind of Pāli/Buddhist philological argument as the JAOS piece. He did not become a career Buddhologist or Indologist.

### Broader reading that the evidence now corrects

He did continue to mobilize Asian religions and Indian philosophy through several distinct scholarly practices:

| Year | Practice | Orientalist evidentiary level |
|---|---|---|
| 1898 | Pāli/Buddhist technical philology in JAOS | strict / highest |
| 1898–99 | Paris text-reading + comparative religion (Lévi/Marillier) | strict + adjacent |
| 1901 | four-lecture Buddhism syllabus | strict teaching evidence, primary artifact pending |
| 1902 | Buddhism/Vedānta in category-history, with self-reference to 1898 | strict intellectual continuity, broader genre |
| 1906 | `primitive philosophy`, mana/manitou/Bantu efficacy | adjacent comparative-religion transformation |
| 1907 | review of Deussen on Śaṅkara; probable Rāmānuja intervention | strict Indian-philosophy review evidence |

The best current formulation is therefore:

> **Lovejoy did not pursue a continuous professional career as a Buddhologist, but his Orientalist formation did not end with the 1898 JAOS article. From 1898 through at least 1907 it remained visible in specialist publication, teaching, comparative category-history, and review of current Indian-philosophy scholarship.**

This is a stronger and more falsifiable thesis than either `Lovejoy was an Orientalist` as a permanent identity or `Lovejoy abandoned Buddhist studies after 1898` as a clean rupture.

## 6. What now matters most

The project should prioritize evidence that distinguishes **continuity of specialist practice** from mere later allusion:

1. recover the actual 1901 *Philosophy of Buddhism* syllabus;
2. extract the primary 1907 Deussen review, especially the exact Śaṅkara/Rāmānuja wording;
3. inspect Wilson's transcriptions / Lovejoy correspondence for 1898 Paris textual training and later references to Buddhism, Sanskrit, Pāli, Vedānta, Deussen, Rāmānuja and Indian philosophy;
4. search Lovejoy's lecture notes and course materials after 1899 for `Buddhism`, `India`, `Hindu philosophy`, `Vedanta`, `Sankhya`, `Pali`, and `Sanskrit`;
5. only after this strict-core sweep should the project decide where Marillier/005 belongs in the final argument.
