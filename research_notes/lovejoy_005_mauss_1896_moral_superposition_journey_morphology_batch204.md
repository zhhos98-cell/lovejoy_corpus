## Core result

A primary-text control in Marcel Mauss’s 1896 critique of Steinmetz forces another narrowing of the originality claim for Lovejoy 005.

Earlier repository syntheses correctly established that Marillier’s `continuation vs retribution` problem sits behind the first thirty pages of 005, but they still treated one move as a comparatively Lovejoy-specific extension: the **historical morphology of moralization** in which old non-moral journey, destination, trial, or exclusion motifs are later re-used as machinery for sorting the good and the bad.

Mauss 1896 already formulates that move in public print before Lovejoy’s Paris year. In the critical section of `La religion et les origines du droit pénal d’après un livre récent`, Mauss argues that some North-American moral eschatologies can be genuinely indigenous/autochthonous without therefore being primitive. He explicitly says that older survivals can have moral beliefs **superposed** upon them. His examples then become exceptionally close to Lovejoy 005: an old journey to the other world, two roads, a struggle, and a bridge to cross can later serve to separate the wicked from the good. Mauss treats the older journey machinery as the earlier layer and the moral sorting as the later use of that machinery.

This does **not** prove Lovejoy read Mauss. It does show that the transformation model itself belonged to the live controversy that Marillier’s 1898–99 conference explicitly resumed against Steinmetz, with Lovejoy officially listed among the auditors who took an active part.

The revised allocation is therefore:

> `MORAL SUPERPOSITION ON OLDER ESCHATOLOGICAL MACHINERY = PRE-LOVEJOY FIELD-LEVEL PRECEDENT`  
> `SYSTEMATIC DETERMINANT TAXONOMY + DESERT THRESHOLD + REPEATED MECHANISM DECOMPOSITION = STRONGER LOVEJOY-SPECIFIC CANDIDATE`

---

## 1. The exact Mauss move that matters

In the 1896 critical section, Mauss is answering Steinmetz’s attempt to use differentiated post-mortem destinies against the continuation theory associated with Tylor and defended empirically by Marillier.

The relevant sequence is not merely `continuation rather than retribution`.

Mauss makes a sharper historical distinction:

1. a moralized belief can be **autochthonous** and still be historically **secondary** rather than primitive;
2. older religious/mythic structures may survive beneath it;
3. moral beliefs can be superposed on those older structures;
4. pre-existing journey machinery — two routes, struggle, bridge-crossing — can be reassigned to distinguish good from bad.

This is a model of **reuse under moralization**, not only a static binary between non-moral and moral systems.

Immediately afterward Mauss returns to Marillier’s empirical controls: apparent counterexamples may reflect Christian, Buddhist, or Hindu influence; Sanskrit `naraka` can expose borrowing; and differentiated afterlives remain compatible with continuation when present inequalities, ritual fulfillment, or manner of death determine the condition of the shade.

Thus the 1896 printed controversy already joins three operations later visible together in 005:

- historical layering/superposition;
- source criticism for imported moral categories;
- causal discrimination among ritual, mode-of-death, status, and moral desert.

## 3. Why the EPHE 1898–99 report closes the contextual gap

Marillier’s official report for the Monday conference of 1898–99 says its essential task was to establish that no moral element enters the origin of non-civilized eschatology. It explicitly names the target: **Steinmetz’s objections** to Marillier’s earlier `Survivance` memoir. It also says the course added new supporting facts especially from the two Americas.

The same official paragraph lists:

> `Auditeurs ayant pris une part active aux travaux : MM. Lovejoy, W. S. Andrews et Lorette.`

This matters because Mauss’s 1896 moral-superposition argument occurs inside precisely that Steinmetz controversy and uses North-American evidence. The safe claim is contextual rather than textual:

> Lovejoy’s pp.24–27 morphology belongs inside a dispute where moral superposition on older eschatological structures was already an available analytical solution.

We still do not claim that a given notebook sentence records Mauss, or that Lovejoy personally read Mauss’s article.

## 5. What remains distinctive in 005 after the correction

The correction is useful because it makes the stronger claim narrower and more defensible.

### 5.1 The `desert` test is unusually explicit

Lovejoy repeatedly refuses to count punishment/reward as moral until the allocation depends on **desert** rather than mode of death, ritual condition, social membership, bodily status, inherited rank, or imported terminology.

### 5.2 Borderline cases are redistributed by operative determinant

Suicide is not simply a moralized death-category. It can be clan injury, taboo/interdict, social exclusion, mode-of-death classification, or later moral reinterpretation. Batch 203 already showed that clan-solidarity/non-moral taboo analysis has a strong Marillier-side precedent; the Lovejoy increment lies increasingly in the repeated redistribution procedure rather than ownership of any one distinction.

### 5.3 The same decomposition migrates across domains

The early eschatology block asks what actually selects a post-mortem outcome. The later sacrifice/anthropophagy block asks what relation or efficacy actually produces a ritual outcome. The 1906 `Primitive Energetics` article then abstracts impersonal/direct/transferable efficacy at a higher conceptual level.

That repeated cross-domain migration remains a stronger developmental claim than saying Lovejoy invented moral layering itself.

## 6. Revised genealogy

The best current chain is:

> **Marillier 1893/94:** continuation rather than retribution; rank/status and the world of the dead are not originally moralized  
> **Mauss 1896 in the Steinmetz dispute:** autochthonous ≠ primitive; moral beliefs can be superposed on older survivals; old journey/two-road/bridge motifs can be repurposed for moral sorting; contact/Indic borrowing and ritual/manner-of-death controls remain necessary  
> **Marillier EPHE 1898–99:** explicit renewed refutation of Steinmetz, new American evidence; Lovejoy active participant  
> **Lovejoy 005:** serial determinant taxonomy, strict desert threshold, borderline-case decomposition, source criticism, and historical layering applied as one working apparatus  
> **Lovejoy 1906:** mechanism abstraction in `Primitive Energetics`.

This genealogy is now stronger because it allocates fewer generic evolutionist/field operations to Lovejoy and isolates the procedure that actually recurs across his notebook.

## 7. Evidence discipline

### Secure

- Marillier’s 1893 official summary says the other world continues earthly life, rank follows earthly status rather than moral merit, and survivance is not founded on moral conceptions.
- Mauss 1896 explicitly distinguishes autochthonous moral beliefs from primitive ones and describes moral beliefs as superposed upon older survivals; he gives the journey/two-road/struggle/bridge complex as older machinery later used to separate bad from good.
- Mauss’s same discussion identifies Christian and Hindu/Buddhist contamination and cites Marillier for ritual/manner-of-death determinants.
- Marillier’s 1898–99 report explicitly frames his conference as refuting Steinmetz and names Lovejoy as an active auditor.
- 005 pp.24–27 explicitly classifies differentiated destinations, hell, journey obstacles and trials as potentially non-moral/earlier and later ethically reshaped.

### Strong inference

- The moral-superposition model was part of the immediate argumentative field in which Lovejoy’s early 005 pages were produced.
- `half-moral` is best treated as Lovejoy’s local classificatory compression unless/until the full Marillier text yields an equivalent label or sequence.

### Not proved

- Direct Lovejoy reading of Mauss 1896.
- Verbatim transmission from Marillier’s oral seminar into Lovejoy’s pages.
- Lovejoy priority for the complete five-part taxonomy before full Marillier 1893/94 collation.
