# Batch 69 — AOS 1897–99 institutional biography + 1907 Deussen review: membership closes; specialist judgment survives the venue shift

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced
Scope: close the two P0/P1 questions left open in batch 68: (1) whether the 1897–99 American Oriental Society proceedings actually place Lovejoy inside the Society institutionally, and (2) the exact JSTOR object and full primary text of Lovejoy’s 1907 review of Deussen on Śaṅkara.

## Core result

Both searches hit, and they change the evidentiary posture materially.

First, Lovejoy **was elected a corporate member of the American Oriental Society in April 1897**. His Buddhist paper was on the Baltimore meeting program as paper no. 30 and was read by title at the final session. The revised 1897 member list gives `ARTHUR ONCKEN LOVEJOY (Harvard University), Oakland, Cal. 1897.` The 1898 and 1899 member rolls continue to list him as a corporate member. Thus the earlier formulation `JAOS publication without an AOS institutional biography` is now obsolete.

Second, the exact JSTOR object for the 1907 Deussen review is stable `2011394`, DOI `10.2307/2011394`, and the full two-page text is recoverable. The review is substantially stronger than a bibliographic notice. Lovejoy assesses the state of Vedānta scholarship, distinguishes Śaṅkara from Rāmānuja, diagnoses the Western reception of Advaita, knows the recent Thibaut translation, and explicitly calls for Indian systems to be treated as contributions to **technical philosophy rather than theosophy**, with attention to arguments about consciousness, subject/object, and self-consciousness.

The resulting historical pattern is now clearer:

> **not continuous professional Buddhology, but continuing Orientalist practice across changing genres — with specialist institutional location fading before specialist Indic-philosophical judgment disappears.**

That still should not be rewritten as an identity claim such as `Lovejoy remained an Orientalist`. The evidence is better expressed at the level of scholarly practice, competence, genre, and institutional location.

## 1. 1897: Lovejoy enters the AOS institutionally

### Primary proceedings object

`Proceedings of the American Oriental Society, at Its Meeting in Baltimore, Maryland, April 22d, 23d, and 24th, 1897`, *Journal of the American Oriental Society* 18 (1897), 375–390.

JSTOR stable:
`https://www.jstor.org/stable/592308`

Internet Archive full-volume OCR:
item `pt2journalofamer18ameruoft`

### Election is explicit

At the fourth and final session the proceedings state that persons recommended by the Directors were `duly elected`; under `As CORPORATE MEMBERS` appears:

> `A. O. Lovejoy, Oakland, Cal.`

The revised May 1897 membership list then gives the full identification:

> `ARTHUR ONCKEN LOVEJOY (Harvard University), Oakland, Cal. 1897.`

This is the missing institutional fact. Lovejoy did not merely place an article in the Society’s journal. He became a corporate member of the AOS in 1897 while at Harvard.

### His Buddhist paper was part of the same annual meeting program

The alphabetized list of papers contains no. 30:

`Mr. Arthur O. Lovejoy, Harvard University; On the meaning of the Buddhist technical terms ...`

The OCR truncates the last terms, but the subject and later publication make the connection to the 1898 `upādāna and upādisesa` article transparent. The proceedings say that paper no. 30 `(by Lovejoy)`, together with several other remaining papers, was `read by title, and with or without a brief statement of their contents` as the meeting approached adjournment.

### Physical attendance remains unresolved, and should remain unresolved

Two facts must not be collapsed.

1. The opening attendance list names `members` present at one or more sessions. Lovejoy was not yet a member when that list was constituted; he was elected only at the final session. His omission therefore cannot by itself prove physical absence.
2. His paper belongs to a residual block described as `for the most part, papers of members not present`. `For the most part` does not identify which specific authors were absent.

Safe claim:

> **Lovejoy had a paper formally in the 1897 AOS program; it was read by title at the meeting; at the same meeting he was elected a corporate member. His own physical attendance is not established.**

This is already enough to establish an AOS institutional biography without inventing oral presentation or attendance.

## 2. 1898: membership continues, while annual-meeting activity drops out

The 1898 Hartford proceedings are in *JAOS* 19, the same volume in which Lovejoy’s finished Buddhist article appears.

The revised May 1898 member list states:

> `ARTHUR ONCKEN LOVEJOY (Harvard University), 1689 Cambridge St., Cambridge, Mass. 1897.`

Thus membership continued after the 1897 election and his address had shifted from Oakland to Cambridge.

The Hartford meeting (14–16 April 1898) explicitly prints the members in attendance at one or more sessions. Lovejoy is absent from that list. The complete paper list likewise has no Lovejoy paper. Because he was already a corporate member before this meeting, this negative evidence is stronger than in 1897.

At the same time *JAOS* 19 publishes his eleven-page technical article, `The Buddhistic technical terms upādāna and upādisesa`, pp. 126–136, under Harvard affiliation.

The institutional picture in 1898 is therefore:

> **AOS member + active JAOS specialist author, but no recorded Hartford attendance and no meeting paper.**

This is precisely why `publication`, `membership`, and `meeting participation` need separate columns in the project chronology.

## 3. 1899 Cambridge: still a member, but absent from the recorded meeting program

### Exact proceedings object

`Proceedings of the American Oriental Society, at Its Meeting in Cambridge, Mass., 1899`, *JAOS* 20 (1899), 361–371.

JSTOR stable:
`https://www.jstor.org/stable/592344`

Internet Archive full-volume OCR:
item `in.gov.ignca.24524` (`Journal of the American Oriental Society vol.20 2nd half`)

The annual meeting was held in Cambridge on 6–7 April 1899. The proceedings state that the following members were `in attendance at one or more of the sessions` and print the attendance list. Lovejoy is not on it.

The proceedings then give a complete numbered list of thirty-four papers presented to the Society. Lovejoy is not among the authors. This is not a keyword-index accident: the list can be followed continuously from no. 1 through no. 34.

Yet the December 1899 member roll still contains:

> `ARTHUR ONCKEN LOVEJOY, 1689 Cambridge St., Cambridge, Mass. 1897.`

The OCR is noisy, but name, Cambridge address, and election year are clear.

Therefore:

> **By 1899 Lovejoy retained AOS corporate membership but has no recorded attendance and no paper in the Cambridge annual meeting.**

This is especially useful because the meeting was geographically local and because its Friday afternoon included an explicit `section for the Historical Study of Religions`. The session included Charles C. Everett on the psychology of Vedānta and Sāṃkhya, Crawford H. Toy on magic and religion, Morris Jastrow Jr. on the historical study of religions in American colleges and universities, and other comparative-religion papers. Lovejoy’s absence from the attendance/paper lists therefore sharply separates the surrounding AOS intellectual ecology from his own documented participation.

Do not turn this into a claim that he had disengaged intellectually from Indic material. The later publication record now directly contradicts that stronger inference.

## 4. Institutional trajectory, 1897–99

The three-year AOS sequence can now be stated with unusual precision:

| year | AOS membership | meeting paper | recorded attendance | specialist publication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1897 | **elected corporate member** | **yes**, Buddhist technical terms, no. 30; read by title | unresolved | paper not yet in final JAOS article form |
| 1898 | **member roll** | none recorded | none recorded | **JAOS 19, 126–136**, technical Buddhology |
| 1899 | **member roll** | none among 34 papers | none recorded | no new Lovejoy Oriental article located in this volume |

This replaces the binary question `was Lovejoy in the AOS?` with a more useful institutional biography:

> **He entered the AOS in 1897 through election plus a Buddhist paper, remained a corporate member through at least 1899, but the annual-meeting record does not show sustained meeting participation after that entry.**

AOS institutional affiliation is therefore secure; sustained AOS institutional activity is not.

## 6. The 1907 review shows specialist judgment, not merely topical interest

The two-page text is far denser than Wilson’s short bibliography synopsis suggested. At least six operations matter.

### 6.1 Field-state judgment

Lovejoy treats Deussen’s older Vedānta work as still authoritative and Woods’s study with Deussen at Kiel and with Indian pundits as a guarantee of accurate handling of German and Sanskrit technical vocabulary. He then judges the state of the field: Śaṅkara has been sufficiently, even redundantly, expounded in English and German; what competent specialists should now produce are translations and analyses of less-known systems.

That is a judgment about the distribution and adequacy of specialist literature, not simply a response to one book.

### 6.2 Doctrinal discrimination between Śaṅkara and Rāmānuja

Lovejoy calls Rāmānuja’s system a kind of `concrete idealistic monism` and sets it against what he takes to be the sterility of Śaṅkara’s abstract monism and the logical evasiveness of māyā. Whether or not one accepts the evaluation, the review clearly distinguishes rival Vedāntic positions rather than treating `Vedānta` as an undifferentiated Oriental doctrine.

### 6.3 Reception history of Advaita

He explains the disproportionate Western prestige of Śaṅkara through two mediating formations: Deussen’s Schopenhauerian enthusiasm and the propagandizing activity of disciples of Ramakrishna. His target is the monopoly by which one extreme abstract form of Vedāntic monism had come to stand for Indian pantheism and for the Upaniṣads as a whole.

This is a small but recognizable history-of-reception argument: a Western canon of `Indian philosophy` is being explained through translators/interpreters and religious networks, not treated as transparent representation.

### 6.4 Current bibliographic command

Lovejoy knows that Thibaut’s translation of Rāmānuja’s commentary on the Vedānta Sūtras was available in *Sacred Books of the East*, vol. 48 (1904). He uses the existence and scale of that translation to argue for a more accessible account of Rāmānuja.

The chronology matters: the review is engaging a translation only a few years old, not recycling only 1890s Harvard reading.

### 6.5 Comparative-philosophical mapping

He maps Indian systems onto live Anglo-American philosophical problems: Rāmānuja is compared to Neo-Kantian tendencies and even to Royce; Śaṅkara can at moments be compared with T. H. Green. The comparison is not merely decorative. Lovejoy identifies shared problems concerning consciousness, subject/object relations, and self-consciousness.

This is a change of scholarly operation from the 1898 article. The earlier article works by Pāli usage, canonical passages, etymological alternatives, and philological discrimination. The 1907 review works by system comparison, field diagnosis, reception history, and translation of Indian arguments into contemporary philosophical problem-spaces.

### 6.6 Explicit program: Indian systems as technical philosophy

The most important methodological passage is the closing program. Lovejoy asks for a historical and critical survey of Indian philosophy that would treat Hindu systems as contributions to **technical philosophy rather than theosophy**, and would analyze the reasons leading philosophers to conclusions rather than merely inventory doctrines. He argues that the reasoning of Śaṅkara and Rāmānuja can be formulated in intelligible Western philosophical terms and compared profitably with contemporary reflection.

This is stronger than `Lovejoy retained an interest in Vedānta`.

Safe claim:

> **In 1907 Lovejoy still possessed enough current Indic-philosophical competence to judge the state of Vedānta scholarship, distinguish rival systems, identify recent translations, criticize the Western canonization of Advaita, and prescribe a comparative-philosophical research program.**

## 7. What the Deussen review does *not* prove

It should not be made to carry the evidentiary burden of the 1898 JAOS article.

The review does not show Lovejoy independently collating Sanskrit manuscripts, adjudicating textual variants, parsing Pāli/Sanskrit forms, or contributing a new philological solution. It explicitly relies on the specialist authority of Deussen, Woods, native pundits, and Thibaut’s translation.

So the strongest continuity is not:

`1898 professional Buddhologist → 1907 same kind of professional Buddhologist`.

It is:

> **1898 specialist Buddhist philology → 1907 portable Indic-philosophical competence redeployed as comparative philosophical and historiographical judgment.**

The specialist **mode** changes. The competence has not vanished.

## 8. Revised four-layer model

Batch 68 separated `Oriental publication / AOS institutional participation / later Indic continuity / broader history-of-religions work`. Batch 69 now allows the second layer to be subdivided more precisely.

### Layer 1 — Oriental specialist publication

Secure: 1898 technical Buddhological article in *JAOS*.

### Layer 2a — AOS institutional affiliation

Secure: corporate election in 1897; member rolls in 1897, 1898, 1899.

### Layer 2b — AOS annual-meeting participation

Limited/discontinuous: a Buddhist paper is formally on the 1897 program and read by title; physical attendance remains unresolved. No recorded attendance or paper in 1898 and 1899.

### Layer 3 — later Indic continuity

Now much stronger: the 1907 Deussen review is not merely bibliographic residue. It contains current field-state, doctrinal, bibliographic, reception-historical, and comparative-philosophical judgments.

### Layer 4 — broader history-of-religions work

Remain separate from Layer 3. The 1902 and 1907 religion/history publications show migration into broader comparative/historical genres, but they should not be used to inflate all such work into Indology.

## 9. Historiographical wording after Batch 69

The user’s proposed formula is the right level of safety:

> **not continued professional Buddhology, but continued Orientalist practice across changing genres.**

A slightly more diagnostic version for eventual prose would be:

> **What persisted after the 1890s was not a continuous professional career in Buddhology, but a portable Orientalist competence redeployed across changing genres: first Buddhist philology within the AOS/JAOS orbit, later comparative-historical and philosophical judgment on Indian religion and philosophy in broader venues.**

A second version, if `Orientalist` itself needs qualification, is:

> **The specialist locus changed before the specialist competence disappeared: Lovejoy ceased to publish as a Buddhological specialist, while practices formed in Oriental/Indic scholarship continued to be redeployed in comparative religion and philosophy.**

The first wording has more historical bite; the second is safer against making `Orientalist` a stable personal identity.

## 10. Immediate consequences for the project narrative

1. Delete the batch-68 negative proposition that AOS membership remained unproved. It is now proved.
2. Preserve the distinction between membership and attendance: the first is secure, the second is not continuous.
3. Treat 1897, not 1898, as Lovejoy’s first currently documented **institutional entry into the AOS**, because election and paper-program participation both occur in the 1897 Baltimore proceedings.
4. Treat the 1898 article as the finished specialist-publication moment, not the beginning of AOS contact.
5. Treat 1907 as evidence of a change in epistemic operation: textual/philological adjudication gives way to comparative-system judgment and reception critique.
6. The strongest continuity claim is now about **practice and portable competence**, not about a continuous professional identity.

## Bottom line

> **Batch 69 closes both open JSTOR questions. Lovejoy entered the American Oriental Society as a corporate member in 1897, with a Buddhist paper on the annual-meeting program, and remained on the member rolls through at least 1899, although recorded annual-meeting activity drops out after the 1897 entry. A decade later, his 1907 Deussen review shows that specialist Indic-philosophical judgment survived outside a specialist Oriental venue. The cleanest historical description is therefore not a stable identity (`Lovejoy remained an Orientalist`) and not a vanished field (`Buddhology ended in 1898`), but a migration of Orientalist practice and competence across genres and institutions.**
