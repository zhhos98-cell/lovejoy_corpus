# Martin Guerre in the twenty-first century — reappearances as a history-of-knowledge problem

Date: 2026-09-03  
Status: **COMPARATIVE HISTORY-OF-KNOWLEDGE SIDECAR / NON-GENEALOGICAL / DOES NOT ENTER THE LOVEJOY EVIDENCE CHAIN**  
Repository function: comparative method control under `research_notes/`; use for thinking about warrant, identification, media, historical proof, and epistemic portability.  
Scope: twenty-first-century scholarly, pedagogical, infrastructural, and theatrical reappearances of the Martin Guerre affair. This note does **not** reopen the sixteenth-century case, adjudicate Bertrande de Rols's actual knowledge, or treat later uses as evidence for the historical actors.

## Working thesis

The twenty-first-century afterlife of Martin Guerre is better described as a sequence of **epistemic relocations** than as a simple reception history. The case keeps returning because its narrative core — a community must decide whether one embodied claimant is the same person who departed years earlier — can be detached from the sixteenth-century lawsuit and reused wherever a discipline needs a compact test of **recognition, identification, proof, mediation, and the authority to know**. In each relocation, the noun *identity* becomes less important than the verb-like operations by which identity is made knowable: witnesses recognize; bodies are read; documents authenticate; historians infer through reticent sources; images claim evidentiary force; audiences are made to witness; adaptations allocate private knowledge to characters; digital repositories remediate old printed witnesses; cognitive theory decomposes the tracking procedures by which a person is followed through time.

This makes Martin Guerre unusually useful for a history of knowledge. The recurrent question is not only `Who is Martin Guerre?` but, more precisely:

> **By what procedure, from which traces, under whose authority, and at what evidentiary resolution can a claim that this person is Martin Guerre become warranted knowledge?**

The twenty-first-century sequence therefore has a strong family resemblance to a warrant-routing problem:

`person / claimant` → `signs and memories` → `recognition` → `comparison` → `authorized judgement`

but the carriers and authorities change:

`village witnesses / court` → `body + paperwork / identification regime` → `historian + archive / proof` → `image / media theory` → `cognitive model / tracking explanation` → `performance form / gendered knowledge and consent` → `digital surrogate + metadata / remote source inspection` → `audience + paratext / reflexive work identity`.

The strongest historical claim is therefore not that twenty-first-century writers agree about what early modern identity *was*. They do not. It is that Martin Guerre has become a **portable epistemic object**: a bounded case whose evidentiary architecture can be redeployed to test different theories of how knowledge of persons and pasts is produced.

## 1. 2001–2013: from the history of identity to the history of identification

A decisive twenty-first-century relocation occurs in the literature on identification practices. Jane Caplan and John Torpey's edited volume *Documenting Individual Identity: The Development of State Practices in the Modern World* (2001), including Valentin Groebner's chapter on late medieval and Renaissance identification, belongs to a broader historiographical move away from treating identity primarily as interiority or self-fashioning and toward studying the practical media and institutions by which persons are **described, recorded, authenticated, and made administratively legible**. Groebner developed that programme in *Who Are You? Identification, Deception, and Surveillance in Early Modern Europe* (German 2004; English 2007): the governing question is not existential but operational — who are you, and how can you prove it? Documents, seals, signatures, bodily marks, offices of record, and the impostor appear together because systems for authenticating persons generate their own possibilities of counterfeit and doubling.

Miriam Eliav-Feldon's *Renaissance Impostors and Proofs of Identity* (2012) similarly organizes imposture through evidentiary technologies. Its architecture moves from forms of dissimulation toward `The Body as Evidence`, `Judging by Appearances`, and `Paperwork: Identification Documents`. This is exactly the shift required to make Martin Guerre into a history-of-knowledge object: the interesting variable is no longer merely whether Arnaud du Tilh possessed a socially constituted identity, but which **signs were thought to travel reliably from a person to a judgement about that person**.

Vincent Denis makes the reuse explicit in `Individual Identity and Identification in Eighteenth-Century France` (2013). He opens a history of identification with Martin Guerre and stresses the heterogeneous materials that the judges and local witnesses had to compare: accent, the size of sabots, scars and bodily marks, and remembered characteristics. That move is methodologically revealing. Martin Guerre becomes a baseline case for a world in which identity knowledge is distributed across local memory and embodied signs rather than centralized documentary systems. The affair can then be used to historicize the later rise of state registration without treating modern identification as the timeless solution to an unchanged problem.

**Knowledge-history significance:** this literature changes the unit of analysis from `self` to `identification operation`. It therefore also changes what counts as a historical explanation. Instead of asking whether sixteenth-century villagers possessed a modern concept of individuality, it asks how an identification claim was assembled from signs, witnesses, papers, and institutional procedures. Martin Guerre becomes less a philosophical curiosity about sameness and more a calibration case for the **infrastructure of warranted person-knowledge**.

## 2. 2009 onward: digital remediation changes the source situation

The affair also reappears through infrastructure rather than interpretation. A 1561 copy of Jean de Coras's *Arrest memorable du Parlement de Tolose* from the Bavarian State Library was digitized by Google on 29 June 2009 and is now remotely inspectable. Tolosana, the digital library of Toulouse higher-education institutions, provides digitized early editions of Coras, including downloadable scans and, for at least one record, OCR-based full-text search.

This is a different kind of afterlife. The printed source that twentieth-century readers often encountered through editions, quotations, Davis's narration, or library-mediated consultation becomes a manipulable digital object: a reader can move between metadata, scan, download, and machine-searchable text. Access therefore shifts from `historian reports source` toward `reader can inspect surrogate`, and some previously expensive checks become ordinary desktop operations.

The gain should not be confused with evidentiary identity. A digital surrogate is not the edition as such, OCR is not diplomatic transcription, and searchability can hide rather than solve differences in spelling, typography, pagination, annotation, issue, and edition. The important history-of-knowledge point is narrower: **digitization changes the distribution and cost of source inspection without abolishing source criticism**. Martin Guerre's twenty-first-century return is consequently also a return of Coras as data-bearing infrastructure.

## 3. 2014–2015: the case becomes portable outside historiography

Nicolas J. Bullot's `Agent tracking: a psycho-historical theory of the identification of living and social agents` (published online 2014; issue 2015) is an unusually clear case of disciplinary export. Bullot uses Arnaud du Tilh's impersonation to distinguish recognition-based, heuristic-based, and explanation-based agent tracking. Villagers may recognize a claimant or match him against socially available heuristics; a more reliable identification requires reconstructing the causal history that explains the persistence of the target agent. Martin Guerre is thus converted into an **epistemological model case** for how a tracker follows a person through time and how socially scaffolded heuristics can produce collective error.

This is more than citation. The legal-historical episode is stripped down into an explanatory architecture that can travel into philosophy of biology, cognitive science, and epistemology:

`appearance / memory match` → `heuristic satisfaction` ≠ `causal-history reconstruction` → `explanation-based identification`.

Lisa Saltzman's *Daguerreotypes: Fugitive Subjects, Contemporary Objects* (2015) performs another relocation. Its introduction begins with Martin Guerre and uses the case to rethink photography as a technology of identification, moving among impostors, historians, detectives, images, fiction, and documentary truth. The case becomes a hinge by which the evidentiary promise of photography can be examined: an image may seem to secure identity, yet photography itself can stage, duplicate, fabricate, and circulate uncertain subjects. Here Martin Guerre no longer functions mainly as an early modern social actor. He becomes an analytic figure for the unstable relation between **visual evidence and the guarantee of identity**.

**Knowledge-history significance:** by the mid-2010s Martin Guerre is demonstrably epistemically portable. A single historical case supports models of administrative verification, cognitive tracking, and photographic evidence because each field can preserve the same minimal problem — identifying a persisting person through incomplete signs — while changing the mechanism that is supposed to warrant the answer.

## 4. 2020–2021: public microhistory treats media as epistemic instruments

The public-microhistory literature makes a further move from representation as dissemination to representation as **knowledge-making practice**. In `Art, Collaboration and Multi-Sensory Approaches in Public Microhistory: Journey with Absent Friends` (2020), Martin Guerre is called a pioneering example of public microhistory because Natalie Zemon Davis developed the material both as historical scholarship and in relation to cinema. The article cites Davis's retrospective description of cinema as something like a laboratory or thought experiment rather than a truth-telling machine: the constraints of staging force the historian to imagine features of action and situation that prose can leave unarticulated.

That formulation matters. Film is not merely a popular wrapper around an already finished historical finding. It becomes an **epistemic instrument with different affordances and failure modes**. It can force questions, expose missing causal links, spatialize relations, and make historians commit to possible sequences of action; it can also exceed the warrant of the archive. The line between historical imagination and historical proof therefore becomes part of the case rather than a methodological embarrassment external to it.

The continued use of *The Return of Martin Guerre* in microhistory teaching and methodological discussion extends the same function pedagogically. Students do not only learn what happened at Artigat; they learn to ask what a historian may infer from silence, how a source author's position shapes evidence, why narrative closure can outrun archival closure, and how a microhistorical case moves between scholarly and public media. The case is therefore transmitted as a **training object for source criticism and controlled imagination**.

## 5. 2023: forty years later, Davis's book itself becomes the object

The 2023 *Studi Storici* forum `Il ritorno di Martin Guerre di Nathalie Zemon Davis: quarant'anni dopo`, edited by Pasquale Palmieri, is a strong marker that the twenty-first-century object is no longer just the sixteenth-century affair. The forum explicitly treats the methodological insights of Davis's 1983 book as still productive across humanities and social sciences and divides the reassessment into four problems: Lisa Roscioni on historical writing and reticent sources; Fernanda Alfieri on the reasons and feelings of Bertrande de Rols; Fabio Dei on Martin Guerre and the peasant world; Ottavia Niccoli on Davis, history, and cinema.

The structure of the forum is itself evidence for an epistemic recanonization. The historical event has become inseparable from the history of the procedures by which it was made into history: archival silence, narrative inference, popular culture, social identity, affect, and cinema are no longer peripheral reception topics but constitutive methodological problems. `Martin Guerre` now names at least three stacked objects:

1. the sixteenth-century legal affair;
2. Davis's reconstruction and its proof burdens;
3. the later history of disciplines and media that use that reconstruction to debate what historical knowledge can be.

This stacking matters for source control. A twenty-first-century statement about `Martin Guerre` may actually describe Davis, the 1982 film, a musical, a theory of microhistory, or the historiographical controversy rather than the sixteenth-century actors. The same proper name therefore carries multiple objects, and any argument has to specify which one owns the claim.

## 6. 2024: Bertrande's knowledge becomes the adaptive variable

Sally Barnden's `Women's knowledge and musical form: adapting historical identities in Martin Guerre` (*Adaptation* 17.1, 2024) offers the most explicit twenty-first-century bridge between adaptation history and history of knowledge. Barnden treats the 1996 West End musical not simply as a retelling but as an intervention shaped by then-current historiographical arguments about contingent early modern identity. Her central analytic variable is **Bertrande's knowledge and consent**.

The crucial point is formal. The musical can allocate private knowledge through devices unavailable to a court record: soliloquy, duet, reprise, the `I want` song, and other conventions can tell an audience what a character knows, wants, or recognizes. The 1996 version made Bertrande's awareness of Arnaud's imposture explicit and therefore relocated uncertainty away from `does she know?` toward the consequences of what she knows. Later versions, especially the 2007 revival, retreated toward ambiguity and recovered some of the film's uncertainty, which also reopened uncertainty around consent.

For a history of knowledge this is a precise media effect, not just a thematic one. **Adaptation changes the distribution of epistemic access.** The archive preserves testimony and reported speech under legal conditions; the historian can infer interiority only under evidentiary constraint; dramatic form can manufacture privileged audience access to a character's private state. Once that happens, agency and consent are reorganized because they depend on what the adapted Bertrande is made to know.

Barnden therefore helps separate three propositions that are often collapsed:

- `K_historical`: what the historical Bertrande knew;
- `K_historiographical`: what a historian can warrant about her knowledge;
- `K_dramatic`: what an adaptation explicitly assigns to the character and lets an audience know.

These are not rival answers at the same evidentiary level. They are different knowledge regimes. The adaptation can make `K_dramatic` certain while `K_historical` remains unresolved.

## 7. 2026: proof, common knowledge, and a reflexive theatrical return

Denis Crouzet's `Listening to Natalie Zemon Davis` (*Representations* 173.1, February 2026) makes the epistemological significance of Martin Guerre unusually explicit through Davis's retrospective reflections. Crouzet reports Davis's description of the book as an exploration of truth and doubt that linked a sixteenth-century community's search for truth about identity with a historian's search for truth about the past. He also records her insistence that historians retain practices and an ideal of proof even when unanswered questions remain. In this retrospective register, Martin Guerre becomes an analogy for historiography itself: **identifying the absent person and identifying the absent past are parallel problems of warranted reconstruction under incomplete evidence**.

The Old Vic's October 2026 `Martin Guerre` adds a new performative layer. The theatre describes the production as an `all-new version`, with new music, new lyrics, new folk-inflected orchestrations, and an in-the-round staging; its own marketing line says the musical is `Back, but with a whole new identity.` A separate Old Vic guide calls it a `re-thought take on love, law and deception` and stresses the intimacy of the semi-staged form.

The following is an **analytical inference, not a claim about authorial intention**: the 2026 paratext makes the afterlife reflexive. A work about disputed personal identity now advertises itself through disputed **work identity**. If music, lyrics, orchestration, staging, and performance form are substantially rewritten, on what grounds is this still the same *Martin Guerre*? Title, authorship lineage, retained musical material, plot, institutional branding, and audience recognition become the work-level equivalents of name, kinship, memory, bodily signs, and witness recognition in the original affair. The production's slogan makes the analogy unusually visible:

`the same person?` ↔ `the same work?`

The in-the-round format also has an epistemic affordance worth tracking once reviews and production documentation exist. The historical lawsuit depended on distributed witnesses whose recognition had to be aggregated into judgement. An in-the-round audience is likewise spatially distributed around claimants and performances. This does **not** yet prove that the production deliberately stages communal identification; it establishes a precise question for later performance analysis: whether spatialized witnessing becomes part of how the 2026 version makes identity knowable.

## 9. The deeper knowledge-history structure

The strongest synthesis is a change in **where the warrant sits**.

### A. Recognition is redistributed

In the affair, knowledge of identity is socially distributed. No single sign is decisive: memory, kinship, bodily marks, accent, intimacy, property relations, and testimony have to be combined. Twenty-first-century identification history recognizes this as a historically specific knowledge infrastructure rather than a primitive absence of modern ID.

### B. Verification is materialized

Groebner, Eliav-Feldon, Denis, and related identification studies move the problem into media: papers, seals, signatures, bodies, marks, records. Identity becomes a result of technologies that stabilize a claim sufficiently for institutions to act on it.

### C. Historical reconstruction becomes reflexive

Davis's case is repeatedly used to ask what historians themselves do when evidence is incomplete. The relevant distinction is not fact versus fiction in the abstract but **degrees and types of warrant**: archival statement, contextual inference, conjectural reconstruction, cinematic concretization, dramatic assignment of interior knowledge.

### D. Knowledge is gendered and unevenly authorized

Bertrande is an epistemic actor whose knowledge is also a problem for every later narrator. Whether she recognized Arnaud, when she recognized him, whether recognition entailed consent, and how publicly she could authorize what she knew determine the moral and causal shape of the story. The twenty-first-century adaptation literature makes this explicit by treating the assignment of her knowledge as a formal decision with consequences for agency.

### E. Media do not merely transmit a case; they change its epistemic geometry

Court record, monograph, film, musical, photograph, digital scan, OCR text, seminar, and in-the-round performance do not give equivalent access to the same information. Each medium redistributes visibility, interiority, sequence, comparison, and authority. Martin Guerre survives because the case is structurally simple enough to migrate while evidentially difficult enough to reveal those differences.

### F. The case becomes a model organism for knowledge studies

`Model organism` is used here analogically. Martin Guerre is compact, repeatable, exceptionally legible, and difficult at exactly the right points. It permits repeated interventions on the same basic problem while researchers change one variable: documents, bodies, photographs, cognition, narrative, gendered knowledge, performance, or digital infrastructure. Its scholarly longevity therefore reflects not only narrative attractiveness but **experimental usefulness**.

## 10. Comparative control for the Lovejoy project

This sidecar can calibrate the Lovejoy project's vocabulary without implying any genealogy or historical relation.

The useful comparative proposition is:

> A concept or case can remain historically productive not because its object remains stable, but because its **relational problem is portable** and can be re-instantiated under new evidentiary carriers.

Martin Guerre supplies a clean external control for distinguishing:

- stable object from stable problem;
- actor vocabulary from analyst vocabulary;
- recognition from verification;
- evidence carrier from evidentiary warrant;
- public representation from epistemic operation;
- same name/title from same historical object;
- analogy from genealogy.

This is structurally comparable to the active Lovejoy work on relational decomposition and reaggregation only at the level of **method control**. It is not evidence that Lovejoy, early anthropology, or `primitive` shared a genealogy with Martin Guerre scholarship. The comparative gain is that it shows how a relation can persist while its carriers, disciplinary homes, and authorized units change.

## 11. Claims, confidence, and what would falsify them

### High confidence

1. **Twenty-first-century Martin Guerre scholarship has shifted strongly toward identification practices, proof, media, and the conditions under which identity claims are warranted.** Supported by Groebner, Eliav-Feldon, Denis, Bullot, Saltzman, Barnden, and the 2023 forum.
2. **Davis's Martin Guerre remains methodologically canonical rather than merely remembered as a successful microhistory.** The 2023 forty-year forum and 2026 Crouzet retrospective directly support this.
3. **Bertrande's knowledge is a central adaptive variable in recent scholarship.** Barnden makes this the organizing problem and separates versions according to how they allocate knowledge and consent.
4. **Digital remediation materially changes access to Coras without eliminating edition/source criticism.** Directly observable from Google Books/Tolosana interfaces and metadata.
5. **The Old Vic 2026 production explicitly markets itself through identity language while substantially revising the work.** Directly supported by Old Vic's `all-new version` and `whole new identity` wording.

### Medium confidence / analytical synthesis

6. **The case is best understood as a portable epistemic object or model case across disciplines.** This is an analyst's synthesis from multiple independent reuses, not actors' terminology.
7. **The 2026 Old Vic version creates a reflexive work-identity problem.** Strongly suggested by the paratext and revision record; interpretation should be tested against programme notes, rehearsal documentation, reviews, and the performance itself.
8. **In-the-round staging may convert distributed witnessing into part of the audience's epistemic experience.** Plausible affordance only; requires performance-level evidence after opening.

### Findings that would force revision

- evidence that the apparent identification-history uses only cite Davis ornamentally and do not operationalize the case;
- production documents showing that 2026 revisions preserve almost all prior identity/knowledge architecture despite marketing language;
- archival/version evidence showing that `all-new` is commercially inflated and work-level changes are minor;
- systematic reception evidence demonstrating that twenty-first-century readers primarily reuse Martin Guerre for another problem not represented here;
- source-level evidence that a supposedly digital `edition` conflates distinct witnesses in ways that materially alter the accessibility argument.

## Compressed formulation

**Martin Guerre's twenty-first-century return is not one continuous story of reception. It is a sequence of reassignments of the same identification problem to new knowledge systems. The court asked whether witnesses, bodies, memories, and relations could establish a person's identity; identification history asks how institutions materialize such claims; cognitive theory asks how trackers distinguish recognition from explanation; visual theory asks whether images guarantee identity; microhistory asks how incomplete traces can warrant narrative; adaptation asks whose private knowledge can be made public and with what consequences for consent; digitization changes who can inspect the source and at what cost; the 2026 theatrical revival finally turns the case back on its own carrier, returning a work whose makers explicitly advertise it as the same title with a `whole new identity`. The durable object is therefore not Martin Guerre as a stable meaning but a portable relation between claimant, trace, recognition, warrant, and authorized judgement.**
