## Core result

Direct OCR recovery of Söderblom 1901 pp.33–47 from the Gallica scan mirror (`bpt6k5765696v`) substantially sharpens Batches 203–206 and forces a further attribution correction.

The earlier residual claim that Lovejoy’s distinctive contribution in 005 may lie in creating an explicit **serial determinant taxonomy** now has to be narrowed again. Söderblom, another participant in Marillier’s 1898–99 seminar environment, not only says that he follows Marillier with `d’autres divisions` and from a somewhat different point of view. He then lays out an explicit serial analysis of why the dead receive different destinies:

1. cases where there is **no connection** between differentiated survivance and present life;
2. cases where post-mortem difference continues **different terrestrial conditions**;
3. cases where post-mortem difference continues **different kinds of death**.

He then subdivides terrestrial condition into tribe, wealth/social position and natural gifts/powers; and subdivides death-related conditions into manner of death, funeral provision, and rites performed by survivors. He explicitly says that in these classes the differences in the next life remain continuation rather than consequences of the deceased person’s piety.

This is already a working **selector sequence**, not merely a morphology of one-place/two-place afterlives.

The strongest current Lovejoy delta therefore becomes even narrower:

> **Lovejoy does not invent the determinant sequence. His notebook intensifies and redistributes a shared workshop taxonomy through repeated borderline-case testing, local English compression (`half-moral`, `desert`), and later migration of the same selector/mechanism procedure into sacrifice, anthropophagy and efficacy.**

Even `half-moral` now loses most of its conceptual originality: Söderblom explicitly attributes to Marillier a classification of different epochs by their **relation to morality**, speaks of indigenous **moral or quasi-moral principles**, and himself identifies a historical threshold where `good` as moral/religious separates from `good` as capacity or fitness.

## 1. Direct p.33: Söderblom explicitly redivides Marillier’s study

Printed p.33 is now directly recovered.

Söderblom first states that differentiated post-mortem conditions in the material under examination do not constitute divine punishment/reward and are independent of the deceased person’s piety. He then names Marillier’s `La survivance de l’âme et l’idée de la justice chez les peuples non civilisés` as the prior analysis of why the dead receive different conditions.

His own methodological declaration is explicit:

> `En me rattachant à cette remarquable étude, je me propose, avec d’autres divisions et à un point de vue un peu différent, de rechercher les réponses typiques à cette question : Pourquoi les morts, selon les croyances primitives, ne subissent-ils pas tous le même sort dans l’autre monde?`

He immediately gives **three successive solutions**:

1. no connection between differentiated survivance and present life;
2. different future destinies because the dead had different conditions/sorts in life;
3. different future destinies because they did not have the same kind of death.

For the second and third classes, Söderblom explicitly says the inequalities of future life are continuations of present inequalities or kinds of death **without the least question of greater or lesser piety**.

This is a direct primary control against the old attribution:

> `Marillier binary → Lovejoy serial determinant taxonomy`.

A serial determinant structure is already visible in a same-workshop student publication explicitly derived from Marillier’s study and conferences.

## 3. pp.35–36: wealth, status, natural gifts and `mana` are explicit non-moral selectors

Söderblom’s terrestrial-condition block distinguishes:

- wealth;
- social position/rank;
- fortune;
- natural gifts/personal capacities.

He explicitly says wealth and rank can continue after death; in Egyptian examples, only the wealthy can afford the funerary provision required for a better post-mortem condition. He then remarks:

> **science obliges us to distinguish what in reality is intimately united.**

This sentence is methodologically important. Terrestrial status and funerary mechanism can be causally entangled in a historical case, yet analytical work separates them for diagnosis.

The next subsection asks what allows a dead person to overcome dangers on the journey to the land of the dead. Söderblom’s answer is:

- personal vigor;
- intellectual and bodily strength;
- power to perform remarkable actions;
- in Marillier’s Melanesian vocabulary, `le mana de l’homme`.

He then explicitly marks a boundary:

> here one reaches the limit of this form of survivance and enters the form where the person’s condition is determined by piety during life.

Thus `natural gifts/power` occupy an intermediate analytical location immediately adjacent to, but not identical with, religious/moral qualification.

This strongly reduces the originality value of Lovejoy’s p.20 `half-moral` as a conceptual middle zone.

---

## 4. pp.37–40: kind of death and funeral conditions remain non-moral

At p.38 Söderblom states the rule directly:

> when post-mortem fate is treated as a consequence of the way a person dies, the kind of burial as well as the kind of death can cause the difference, **but not the piety shown during life**.

He then treats warriors killed in battle, women dying in childbirth, suicides, drowned people and other special deaths.

A particularly revealing move occurs in the Nicaragua case. A report initially speaks of `bons` and `mauvais`; Söderblom checks the underlying testimony and finds that the distinction is actually between those killed in combat and those dying a natural death. The observer’s moral vocabulary therefore loses diagnostic force when the source is examined more closely.

This is almost exactly the inference syntax later reconstructed in Lovejoy 005:

`apparently good/bad destination`  
`≠ moral reward/punishment`  
`→ inspect the operative selector`.

The operation is therefore workshop-shared, not Lovejoy-specific.

## 5. pp.40–42: funeral provision, ritual observance, and Marillier’s epochal classification

Söderblom next treats funeral provision:

- more magnificent tomb;
- greater wealth buried with the dead;
- more instruments and food;
- slaves, women or animals sacrificed;
- proper burial versus wandering without rest.

He cites Marillier for further examples.

Then comes a crucial explicit disagreement in **division**, not principle.

Söderblom separates from the funeral-provision group cases which Marillier had classified together with them: cases where observance of a ritual, taboo, tattooing, sacrifice, offering or other religious rule gives the dead a better condition.

The reason is that **the analytical point of view has changed**. Söderblom says directly:

> `M. Marillier détermine les diverses époques des croyances relatives à la mort d’après leur rapport avec la morale.`

This is decisive for attribution. Contemporary same-workshop testimony explicitly represents Marillier as possessing an **epochal classification organized by relation to morality**.

Söderblom explains the difference between their cuts:

- Marillier groups a rite according to its significance in the moral/religious development of future-life belief, regardless of whether the rite is performed by the dead person or survivors;
- Söderblom’s own exposition isolates the earlier form in which the deceased person’s future condition has not yet become a religious motive governing his own conduct in life.

The transition is then stated in developmental terms. A genuinely new mode of thought appears when belief in future life causes a person to regulate present conduct. Söderblom says the historically important step is reached when **religious and moral actions have an effect on one’s future condition**.

Therefore the field did not merely possess a binary `continuation/retribution`. It possessed competing **epochal cuts of transitional material**.

## 6. pp.44–45: `moral or quasi-moral` is explicitly upstream of Lovejoy’s `half-moral`

The strongest correction concerns transitional moral vocabulary.

In his discussion of Steinmetz, Söderblom says that Marillier attempted to judge indigenous conceptions of the afterlife according to the **`principes moraux ou quasi-moraux des peuples étudiés`** rather than according to the analyst’s familiar standards.

This directly occupies the conceptual territory previously left open for Lovejoy’s `half-moral character`.

Söderblom then criticizes Steinmetz for treating poverty, strength, dexterity and socially important faculties as moral qualifications merely because they determine afterlife outcomes.

His own positive definition is striking. Scientifically, he argues, `morale` should denote something that develops in a certain opposition to undisciplined impulse: habits and obligations toward a deity or another person that have some independence from the mere natural development/capacity of the human being.

He then historicizes the semantic boundary:

- at one stage `bon` moral/religious and `bon` meaning capable/fit are not distinguished;
- `virtus` originally means capacity before moral virtue;
- Swedish `dygd` similarly shifts from capability to virtue;
- **when that distinction appears and is applied to future life, a new epoch begins in the history of the belief**.

This is much closer than our previous controls to the conceptual work encoded in Lovejoy’s `half-moral` and `desert` notes.

Revised status:

> `half-moral` may still be Lovejoy’s local English wording, but the idea of an intermediate / quasi-moral stage and an epochal threshold between fitness and moral qualification is directly present upstream in the Marillier workshop field.

## 7. pp.45–47: Söderblom explicitly rejects forced moralization of the same borderline cases as Lovejoy

Söderblom’s critique of Steinmetz supplies near-exact controls for Lovejoy’s case logic.

He says strength, dexterity and wealth can determine advancement in this life and the next while the afterlife conception has **not yet entered the religious and moral sphere**.

He then rejects a moral reinterpretation of:

- women dying in childbirth;
- suicides;
- warriors killed in battle.

Steinmetz interprets such privileged future destinies as reward because these people are supposedly `the best`. Söderblom calls this a forced introduction of the theory of retribution into the intellectual world of the peoples studied merely because their arrangement seems incomprehensible to the analyst.

He likewise rejects the attempt to interpret a poor funeral as punishment of the dead for the avarice of their relatives. The simpler historical fact is that the dead person’s condition depends on what the survivors put into the tomb.

This is a very close primary parallel to Lovejoy 005’s treatment of drowning, childbirth, suicide, ritual qualification and clan/social consequences.

The Lovejoy delta cannot therefore lie in discovering these cases as diagnostic counterexamples to retribution.

## 8. A second major result: Söderblom’s `successive solutions` are not a universal mechanical civilization ladder

This is important both for 005 and for the larger `primitive` problem.

At first glance Söderblom’s language of `solutions successives`, `époque`, and transition toward retribution could look like a standard unilineal evolutionist chronology.

But his own introduction explicitly blocks that reading.

He says his five-chapter division is **not an a priori system**; it arose gradually from prolonged analysis of beliefs and doctrines. He explicitly distinguishes **three separate and parallel evolutions**, which can coexist within the same religion.

Later, after criticizing Steinmetz, he says directly that retribution beyond the grave does **not** appear among all peoples at one and the same degree of civilization. Israel is his central counterexample to an evolution that would always proceed equally and, as he puts it, `pour ainsi dire mécanique`.

Therefore Söderblom’s classificatory stages are not simply a universal ranking of peoples on one chronological ladder. The object can be layered:

- different relation-types can represent distinct developments;
- several developments can coexist in one religion;
- a `later` conceptual relation need not correspond to one fixed global civilizational level.

This provides an important immediate-period control for interpreting Lovejoy 005’s own mixture of `primitive`, intermediate and moral categories. We should test whether Lovejoy likewise uses these labels as **relation-specific layers** rather than as one globally synchronized civilizational chronology.

This does not prove Lovejoy adopted Söderblom’s model; it demonstrates that such a non-unilineal evolutionary grammar was available inside the same Marillier research environment.

## 9. Revised Lovejoy attribution after direct Söderblom control

### No longer defensible as Lovejoy-specific conceptual inventions

- continuation/status versus retribution;
- serial classification by terrestrial condition, natural capacity, manner of death, funeral condition and ritual;
- distinction between apparent good/bad destiny and genuine moral piety;
- transitional or quasi-moral territory;
- historical epochal shift when moral/religious conduct begins to determine future destiny;
- refusal to moralize strength, skill, wealth, childbirth, suicide, warrior death or funeral provision;
- separation of analyst morality from actor moral/quasi-moral standards;
- non-mechanical relation between conceptual stage and global degree of civilization.

### Remaining plausible Lovejoy deltas

1. **Local lexical compression**
   - `half-moral`;
   - repeated English use of `desert` as a coding threshold.

2. **Specific redivision**
   - Lovejoy’s exact adjacency/order and grouping of naturalistic, ritual, social/clan, intermediate and strict moral cases is not identical to Söderblom’s sequence.

3. **Dense repeated reclassification**
   - Lovejoy uses borderline cases as active tests, frequently changing the proposed explanatory class within the notebook.

4. **Cross-domain portability inside 005**
   - the same selector/mechanism logic migrates from future-life belief into sacrifice, cannibalism, blood, charm/fetish and efficacy.

5. **Later causal abstraction**
   - by 1906 one recurrent mechanism family becomes an explicit theoretical category of impersonal/quasi-mechanical/transferable efficacy.

Even points 2–5 are systematization/application claims, not priority claims.

## 12. Strongest current paper formulation

> **Direct comparison with Nathan Söderblom’s 1901 book removes the last plausible version of a simple `Marillier binary → Lovejoy taxonomy` story. Söderblom, drawing explicitly on Marillier’s study and 1898–99 conferences, already arranged differentiated afterlives into a serial set of selectors: chance, terrestrial condition, wealth and rank, natural powers, manner of death, funerary provision and ritual observance. He explicitly distinguished these from the dead person’s piety, attributed to Marillier an epochal classification by relation to morality, and defended Marillier’s use of indigenous moral or quasi-moral standards against Steinmetz’s attempt to redescribe strength, skill, wealth, suicide, childbirth, warrior death and burial provision as moral desert. Lovejoy’s Paris notebook therefore matters less as the birthplace of these categories than as a particularly dense working surface on which inherited distinctions were redivided, compressed and repeatedly applied to ambiguous cases, before the same operation migrated into the notebook’s treatment of sacrifice and causal efficacy.**

A second, potentially larger implication is:

> **Within the Marillier seminar field, developmental language did not necessarily entail a single synchronized ladder of peoples. Söderblom explicitly described several parallel evolutions that could coexist inside one religion and rejected a mechanically uniform relation between retribution and degree of civilization. This makes `primitive`, intermediate and moral layers candidates for relation-specific historical strata rather than automatically global civilizational ranks.**

The second formulation is a contextual control and should not yet be attributed directly to Lovejoy without a dedicated 005 vocabulary/chronology pass.
