## Core result

The best 004 delta is now narrower and more defensible than `Lovejoy distinguished resemblance from borrowing`.

Oldenberg had already stated that rule clearly by 1890. In _Buddha_² p.100 n.1, he accepts real `Anklänge` between Buddhist and Sāṁkhya doctrines but says they need not exceed the ordinary **family resemblance** among Indian philosophical systems. At p.226 n.2 he likewise admits a striking fourfold Sāṁkhya/Yoga–Buddhist parallel while leaving the direction of borrowing undecidable.

Rhys Davids, in the exact source copied into notebook 004, performs a related source-critical move: the Buddhist eternalist passage is poor evidence for a developed Sāṁkhya system because the technical term `Prakṛti` is absent and the described eternal `world` is said to produce nothing, contradicting the generative role of primordial matter in Sāṁkhya. Lovejoy copies this argument, including Rhys Davids's first-person sentence `I am at a loss to understand...`.

Lovejoy's own intervention starts only at the manuscript marker:

> `To R.D.'s remark it should be added...`

What he adds is not another general warning about resemblance. He asks **which feature has enough discriminatory value to identify a genuinely Sāṁkhyan doctrine**. He argues that mere eternity of Matter and Self is not the central diagnostic property; the doctrine of release rests more specifically on the **separateness of Self and World**, Sāṁkhya's `thorough-going dualism`. He therefore proposes a different Buddhist textual witness — the Majjhima-Nikāya proposition that `the soul is one thing & the body is another` — as a more likely reference to genuinely Sāṁkhyan doctrine.

The operation can therefore be stated:

> **generic resemblance control → identify a system-defining feature → search for a textual witness carrying that feature.**

This is more specific than `resemblance ≠ influence`. It is a problem of **diagnostic specificity / discriminatory power**.

## 1. Oldenberg 1890 supplies the generic family-resemblance rule

Oldenberg's second-edition p.100 n.1 is now primary-controlled from the uploaded OCR.

His position is not that Buddhism and Sāṁkhya show no resemblance. He explicitly concedes real `Anklänge` but limits their evidentiary reach: in his judgment they go no farther than the `Familienähnlichkeit` characteristic of Indian philosophical systems generally.

This formulation matters because it identifies the inferential problem as one of **specificity**:

- if a trait is widely shared across a family of systems, it has low power to establish a particular genealogical relation;
- a stronger genealogy requires a more discriminating correspondence or a historical transmission route.

Oldenberg does not formulate this in modern classificatory vocabulary, but the evidentiary logic is already present.

## 2. Rhys Davids supplies the immediate source-critical test copied by Lovejoy

Notebook 004 pp.12–17 follows Rhys Davids, _Buddhism: Its History and Literature_ (1896), Lecture I, especially pp.24–43.

The copied argument tests whether one Buddhist eternalist doctrine can serve as evidence for pre-Buddhist Sāṁkhya.

Rhys Davids's negative controls are concrete:

1. the passage lacks the technical term `Prakṛti`;
2. taking its `world` as primordial matter creates a functional contradiction, because this eternal world is said to `give birth to nothing`, whereas Sāṁkhya primordial matter is generative;
3. the passage therefore does not establish the existence of the later Sāṁkhya system as a whole.

The notebook sentence `I am at a loss to understand...` belongs to Rhys Davids, not Lovejoy. That attribution remains essential.

## 3. Lovejoy changes the question from similarity to diagnosticity

Immediately after the copied Rhys-Davids sentence, Lovejoy writes:

> `To R.D.'s remark it should be added...`

He then argues that the **eternity of Matter and Self**, though certainly part of Sāṁkhya, is not the doctrine's most decisive characteristic for the present identification problem.

The more central feature is the **separateness of Self and World**, because Sāṁkhya's doctrine of release depends upon this `thorough-going dualism`.

That produces a different textual search strategy. Instead of asking:

> Does this early Buddhist passage contain something resembling later Sāṁkhya?

Lovejoy asks:

> Does this passage contain the feature whose presence would be comparatively diagnostic of Sāṁkhya rather than of a broader Indian philosophical family?

He therefore nominates the Majjhima-Nikāya soul/body distinction (`the soul is one thing & the body is another`) as a more plausible genuinely Sāṁkhyan analogue.

Whether that historical identification is correct is a separate question. The archival significance is the **operator**.

---

## 4. A three-level control now emerges

### Level 1 — resemblance

Two systems share a proposition or formal structure.

This is low-warrant evidence because Indian philosophical systems may display broad family resemblance. Oldenberg 1890 already makes this explicit.

### Level 2 — functional contradiction / source-critical exclusion

A proposed identification must survive tests of vocabulary and conceptual function. Rhys Davids rejects the eternalist passage because `Prakṛti` is absent and the `world` performs the wrong causal role.

### Level 3 — diagnostic specificity

Among features that survive broad comparison, ask which one is sufficiently central/distinctive to discriminate the target system from neighboring systems. Lovejoy chooses Sāṁkhya dualistic separateness rather than generic eternity.

This is the clearest notebook-level increment now visible.

## 5. Relation to the later `upādāna / upādisesa` work

The same operator may help explain why notebook 004 does not remain a loose comparison of Sanskrit/Pāli look-alikes.

In the later lexical section Lovejoy repeatedly asks whether a proposed equation has **discriminating power** for the actual semantic problem:

- competing etymologies for `upādi/upadhi` can both be possible, so etymology alone cannot discriminate;
- apparent duplication of terms in the nidāna chain does not settle whether two categories perform the same causal role;
- textual distribution and doctrinal contrast are used to determine function when genealogy underdetermines meaning.

The common operation is therefore not merely skepticism:

> **when an evidentiary feature is shared by too many hypotheses, replace it with a more discriminating test.**

In the early Sāṁkhya section the test is a system-defining doctrinal property; in the later lexical section it is patterned usage / causal role.

This is a potentially stronger bridge from notebook practice to Lovejoy's later category-history than `he disliked influence claims`.

## 7. Paper-level formulation

A defensible formulation is:

> **By the time Lovejoy was working through Sāṁkhya and Buddhist materials, the insufficiency of resemblance as evidence for direct derivation was already a live Indological rule. Oldenberg had called the parallels no more than the family resemblance of Indian philosophical systems, while Rhys Davids tested proposed parallels against terminology and causal function. Lovejoy's notebook shows a further classificatory move. After copying Rhys Davids's objection, he asks what feature would actually discriminate a Sāṁkhyan doctrine from generic eternalism: not the eternity of matter and self, he argues, but the dualistic separateness on which Sāṁkhya liberation depends. The manuscript thus makes visible a shift from noticing likenesses to selecting diagnostically discriminating properties.**

This should remain a manuscript-method claim, not a statement that Lovejoy was uniquely first to formulate such a rule.
