# Lovejoy / AAUP 1913–1915 — multi-case stress tests and source allocation for the Practical Proposals

Date: 2026-09-03  
Status: **MULTI-CASE SOURCE-ALLOCATION MATRIX / CASE EXPERIENCE != SINGLE-CASE GENEALOGY**

## Core result

The four 1915 Practical Proposals should not be explained by a single `Utah -> policy` line. Their documentary prehistory is distributed across:

- unresolved questions already formulated by the Joint Committee of Nine;
- Lovejoy's pre-existing 1914 governance programme;
- failed or incomplete investigation in the Fisher/Wesleyan case;
- Utah as the first intensive AAUP stress test;
- Lovejoy's public post-Utah governance intervention at the Association of American Universities on 27 Aug 1915;
- the Brewster/Colorado case as a negative/adjudicative control;
- the Nearing/Pennsylvania case as a strong professional-jurisdiction and reappointment control;
- Montana as an ongoing late-1915 governance/tenure case whose final report was not yet available during drafting;
- other first-year investigations considered collectively by Committee A.

The best model is:

`PRE-EXISTING PROBLEM`
-> `CASE-SPECIFIC STRESS TESTS`
-> `VARIABLES ACQUIRE DIFFERENT EVIDENCE WEIGHT`
-> `LOVEJOY HANDWRITTEN PRACTICAL PROPOSALS`
-> `COLLECTIVE DRAFT REVISION / FINAL REPORT`.

Hard firewall:

`MULTIPLE CASES SUPPORT SAME VARIABLE != ONE CASE CAUSED THE RULE`.

## I. Baseline before the 1915 AAUP cases

### Fisher / Wesleyan, 1913–14 — stated reason, actual reason, pretext, and failed investigation

Willard Fisher's dismissal already exposed a recurring evidentiary problem before the AAUP existed.

The institutional president supplied written reasons for dismissal, then privately insisted that those stated reasons were **not the actual reasons**. Fisher developed a list of alternative motives and suspected that the public speech controversy was pretextual.

The Joint Committee sought help from the Carnegie Foundation because an outside body with institutional standing might obtain evidence that disciplinary associations could not.

But Pritchett did not perform the expected inquiry. Seligman had understood that the Foundation would conduct a `careful sifting of the facts` through additional correspondence and oral examination; instead it supplied what Seligman regarded as **ex parte statements from institutional authorities**.

This is a negative precursor for later AAUP inquiry procedure:

`STATED GROUND`
!=
`ACTUAL GROUND`

and

`ONE-SIDE INSTITUTIONAL STATEMENT`
!=
`ADEQUATE INVESTIGATION`.

It also helps explain why the preliminary report did not prematurely resolve every disputed question: some case evidence was genuinely inadequate.

Authority: Hans-Joerg Tiede, *University Reform*, Fisher/Committee-of-Nine discussion.

### Joint Committee preliminary report, Dec 1914 — questions already open

Before Utah, the preliminary report already:

- justified security of tenure;
- recognized dismissal as the most consequential disciplinary mechanism;
- left open whether faculty should receive a hearing before dismissal;
- left open whether a faculty committee should conduct that hearing.

Thus `TENURE / HEARING / FACULTY JURISDICTION` were already problem variables.

### Lovejoy 1914 — governance programme already exists

Lovejoy's `The Profession of the Professorate` already placed personnel authority inside a broader redistribution of university governance, with elected faculty representation and a `self-governing republic of scholars` as a programme.

Thus faculty participation in appointments/dismissals also predates Utah.

## II. Utah, Apr–Jul 1915 — highest-density first-year stress test

Utah is the strongest single case correspondence with the later Practical Proposals because it makes all four variables concrete:

1. **reappointment / personnel authority** — president and Regents controlled personnel; faculty–Regent access was weak; a local Administrative Council reform proposed elected faculty participation;
2. **tenure definition** — annual legal appointments coexisted with an informal expectation of continued reappointment; Regents admitted no distinct/definite rule;
3. **dismissal grounds** — no permanent statutory/regulatory grounds; grounds were assigned case by case;
4. **hearing** — hearing could be granted in practice but was not recognized as a right.

Utah also supplies the most developed 1915 inquiry architecture directly associated with Lovejoy:

- ex parte public accounts described as incomplete/conflicting;
- opposing statements fail to `join issue`;
- 22 questions to Regents and 17 to the president;
- oral and written evidence from multiple institutional positions;
- topical rather than merely narrative report structure;
- explicit withholding of motive attribution when evidence is circumstantial or insufficient.

Thus Utah gives the four Practical Proposal variables a concrete **failure-mode morphology**, but does not create them ex nihilo.

Authority: direct 1915 AAUP Utah report plus `research_notes/Lovejoy_Utah_1915_inquiry_warrant_architecture_2026-09-03.md` and `research_notes/Lovejoy_1914_1915_preexisting_questions_Utah_stress_test_claim_type_warrant_2026-09-03.md`.

## III. 27 Aug 1915 — Lovejoy publicly converts post-Utah experience into a responsibility/jurisdiction argument

About a month after publication of the Utah report, John Dewey's `Faculty Share in University Control` was presented at the **Association of American Universities, Seventeenth Annual Conference, 27 Aug 1915**. Dewey's paper was delivered by Cassius J. Keyser; Lovejoy was present and participated in the recorded discussion.

Lovejoy identified:

- security of tenure;
- judicial trials in cases of dismissal

as among the most important US higher-education problems for the next decade.

He then addressed an objection made by presidents/trustees: faculty allegedly lacked the `sense of responsibility` and `capacity for severity` needed to judge dismissals.

Lovejoy's answer was developmental rather than merely declarative: faculty would not adequately acquire those capacities **except through use — through being given responsibility to exercise them**.

This is a crucial intermediate state between Utah and the year-end proposals:

`FACULTY MAY LACK PRACTICED ADJUDICATIVE CAPACITY`

is not treated as a reason to withhold jurisdiction;

instead:

`JURISDICTION / RESPONSIBILITY`
-> `PRACTICE OF JUDGMENT`
-> `CAPACITY / RESPONSIBILITY DEVELOPED`.

This is Lovejoy's own public position, not a later reconstruction of the Practical Proposals.

Primary bibliographic locus:

Arthur O. Lovejoy, `Discussion of Faculty Share in University Control`, in *Journal of Proceedings and Addresses of the Seventeenth Annual Conference, Association of American Universities* (1915), discussion pp.32–39, Lovejoy loci pp.37–38.

The meeting/date is independently fixed by AAUP's governance history: Dewey paper delivered on 27 Aug 1915.

Current text access remains through Tiede/Rabban exact transcription and citation; direct proceedings page-image recovery remains desirable.

## IV. Brewster / Colorado — negative control: temporary status + conflicting testimony + administration can prevail

The Brewster case is essential because it prevents a one-direction reading of AAUP investigations.

Facts established in the committee's work included:

- Brewster held the title `acting professor`;
- he was informed early in the appointment (though not before taking it) that the term was one year;
- there was clear evidence that President Farrand had rebuked attempted gubernatorial interference;
- the remaining dispute turned on conflicting recollections by Brewster and Farrand about an invitation to testify.

Lovejoy told Brewster he regarded the case as weak and that the committee would likely be unable to adjudicate the conflicting recollections.

The published report ultimately **exonerated the administration**, faulting only late notice.

Fetter drew a rule-level distinction: temporary appointments must be distinguished from regular professorships.

Brewster therefore reinforces two separate variables:

### Tenure/status classification

`TEMPORARY / ACTING APPOINTMENT`
!=
`REGULAR PROFESSORIAL EXPECTATION`.

### Evidence ceiling

`CONFLICTING RECOLLECTION`
without discriminating evidence
-> `NO FORCED ADJUDICATION`.

It also demonstrates that an AAUP inquiry was not defined as advocacy for complainants. That is a procedural control on the legitimacy of the new association's investigative role.

## V. Nearing / Pennsylvania — strong independent reinforcement of reappointment + faculty professional jurisdiction

The Nearing case strongly reinforces two of the most important Practical Proposal relations independently of Utah.

The committee's report turned primarily on:

1. Nearing held **professorial rank with an expectation of reappointment**;
2. his faculty, chair and dean had recommended him for reappointment.

Lovejoy and other committee members did not need to approve Nearing's politics, advocacy or even scholarly performance in order to reach their institutional conclusion.

Lovejoy explicitly stated that because the local faculty had already rendered a professional judgment, the committee was relieved of the need to conduct its own inquiry into Nearing's professional qualifications and work.

This yields a jurisdiction rule:

`PROFESSIONAL-COMPETENCE QUESTION`
-> `LOCAL PROFESSIONAL JUDGMENT HAS PRIMARY EVIDENTIARY/JURISDICTIONAL WEIGHT`.

The trustees' summary override was therefore not only adverse action against Nearing. Lovejoy framed it as **lay intervention in a professional question**.

His preliminary draft was even stronger: overriding the carefully considered professional judgment of the faculty was itself described as an infringement of academic freedom; the final committee report removed/softened this claim to satisfy more conservative members.

This is actual version evidence:

`LOVEJOY PRELIMINARY DRAFT`
-> `COLLECTIVE COMMITTEE SOFTENING`.

Nearing also reinforced tenure/reappointment procedure because the trustees subsequently adopted rules providing:

- three-year assistant-professor terms, then longer reappointments;
- presumption of permanence for full professors;
- faculty-committee conference for dismissals/nonreappointments;
- one-year notice.

The report explicitly compared those reforms to the General Report.

Thus Nearing supplies high independent weight for:

- defined status / reappointment expectation;
- faculty participation in nonreappointment/dismissal;
- professional competence as a jurisdiction-specific question;
- the distinction between individual grievance and a profession-wide governance problem.

## VI. Montana — live late-1915 control, but lower input weight for the December General Report

Montana entered Committee A's docket in June 1915 and Lovejoy traveled there during the summer.

The case exposed governance disorder:

- competing state/local governing bodies;
- personal and political conflict among board actors;
- dismissal of the president;
- dismissal of three faculty critics for `harmony`;
- uncertainty over responsibility for reinstatement and final disposition.

In Oct 1915, newly appointed chancellor Edward Elliott made acceptance conditional on movement toward **`more definite tenure`**, to be codified by explicit procedures. The three faculty members were formally reinstated, though later events showed the reinstatement was unstable and contested.

This means Montana supplied another contemporaneous signal that:

`UNDEFINED / POLITICIZED GOVERNANCE`
+
`DISMISSAL FOR HARMONY / FACTIONAL MANAGEMENT`
->
`NEED FOR DEFINITE TENURE / PROCEDURAL ORDER`.

But the evidentiary weight must remain lower for the December 1915 General Report:

- the investigation was still unresolved in late 1915;
- internal committee disagreement continued;
- the canonical Montana report and later proof-standard disputes extend into 1916–17.

Use Montana as **live case experience during drafting**, not as a completed report that necessarily supplied specific final clauses.

## VIII. What Utah changes, after the matrix

Once the other cases are restored, Utah's special role becomes more precise.

Utah is **not** unique because it first raised tenure, hearings or faculty government.

It is special because it was the earliest 1915 case in which Lovejoy personally built a large, explicit, multi-source inquiry and the four later policy variables appeared **together in one institutional anatomy**:

`TENURE AMBIGUITY`
+
`UNDEFINED DISMISSAL GROUNDS`
+
`NO RIGHT TO HEARING`
+
`FACULTY–BOARD JURISDICTION FAILURE`.

Thus its distinctiveness is **diagnostic density**, not sole genetic priority.

Nearing then gives unusually strong independent confirmation of the reappointment/professional-jurisdiction problem; Brewster supplies the negative case needed to define appointment-status distinctions and adjudicative restraint.

## IX. A three-way claim/jurisdiction distinction now appears in the 1915 evidence

The combined cases support a more precise reconstruction than a generic claim that Lovejoy was `evidence-based`.

### 1. Factual / causal institutional claim

Examples: Was a governor's pressure causally responsible? Was religious influence the motive? What actually caused a dismissal?

Lovejoy/committee practice:

`CLAIM`
-> `MULTI-SOURCE EVIDENCE`
-> `DISCRIMINATING WARRANT`
-> `JUDGE OR WITHHOLD JUDGMENT`.

Utah and Brewster are strongest controls.

### 2. Professional-competence claim

Example: Was Nearing professionally qualified for reappointment?

Lovejoy's move is not to rerun the entire evidentiary inquiry himself. Because the competent local professional body had already rendered judgment, the issue becomes **who has jurisdiction to override it**.

`PROFESSIONAL CLAIM`
-> `IDENTIFY COMPETENT PROFESSIONAL JUDGMENT`
-> `TEST WHETHER LAY BODY HAS WARRANT/JURISDICTION TO SUBSTITUTE ITS JUDGMENT`.

Nearing is the strongest control.

### 3. Normative rule-design claim

Example: What should general tenure/dismissal rules be?

Lovejoy's 21 Aug 1915 objection to Ford is explicit: when existing practices are themselves unregularized, frequency of current practice cannot generate the desired norm.

`UNREGULARIZED PRACTICE`
-> `FORMULATE PRINCIPLE`
-> `SURVEY PRACTICE AFTERWARD`.

This is Lovejoy's direct actor-level distinction and he admits it reverses his usual order for most topics.

Therefore the 1915 materials support a narrow but strong proposition:

> **Lovejoy changed both the evidentiary order and the relevant decision-maker according to the type of claim.**

Do not promote this into a universal transhistorical `Lovejoy method` without additional domains.

## X. Strongest safe synthesis

> **The 1915 Practical Proposals are best understood as prescriptive closure across a distributed problem history. Tenure and hearing were already open questions before Utah, and faculty governance was already part of Lovejoy's 1914 programme. Utah supplied the densest first-year stress test by making tenure ambiguity, undefined grounds, missing hearing rights and faculty–board jurisdiction failure visible together. Lovejoy then publicly argued on 27 Aug 1915 that faculty could acquire the responsibility and severity required for dismissal judgments only by being entrusted with that responsibility. Brewster supplied a negative control separating temporary from regular appointments and showing that irreconcilable evidence could defeat a complainant's case. Nearing independently strengthened reappointment and professional-jurisdiction claims: Lovejoy refused to retry a competence judgment already made by the faculty and treated trustee override as lay intervention in a professional question. Montana added a live late-1915 demand for definite tenure amid governance disorder. The year-end proposals therefore emerge from pre-existing questions plus multiple stress tests, not from one case or one linear doctrine genealogy.**
