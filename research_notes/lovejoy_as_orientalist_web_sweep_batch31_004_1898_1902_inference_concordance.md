## Core finding

The strongest continuity from notebook 004 through Lovejoy’s 1898 JAOS paper to **“Religion and the Time-Process” (1902)** is not a stable list of doctrines and not a proto-`unit-idea` terminology. It is a sequence of increasingly explicit controls on historical and philosophical inference.

The line can be reduced to four operations:

1. **diagnostic resemblance:** before a doctrinal resemblance can bear genealogical weight, ask whether it concerns a feature central enough to distinguish the system;
2. **question-frame control:** do not treat a refusal to answer as absence of theory when the system rejects the presuppositions built into the question;
3. **functional non-equivalence:** formal repetition or common provenance does not prove conceptual identity; ask what causal/semantic work a component performs in the arrangement;
4. **genealogy versus configuration:** historically borrowed or recurrent material can acquire a distinctive function in a new configuration, while conceptual resemblance alone does not prove historical borrowing.

The 1898 paper securely publishes operations 3–4. The 1902 paper generalizes operations 1 and 4 beyond Buddhist philology into comparative history of philosophical/religious **categories, presuppositions and logical motives**. This is the safest bridge toward later history-of-ideas practice.

## 1. Diagnostic resemblance: from Sāṁkhya comparison to anti-diffusion control

### 004

The notebook first receives the Sāṁkhya/Buddhism priority controversy through T. W. Rhys Davids’s digest of Garbe. That source uptake itself is not Lovejoy’s conclusion.

The authorial hinge appears on PDF p.17:

> `To R.D.'s remark it should be added ...`

Lovejoy argues that eternity of Matter and Self is not Sāṁkhya’s most distinctive feature. The feature on which the doctrine of release especially depends is the **separateness of Self and World**, its `thorough-going dualism`. He therefore proposes a different Buddhist passage as a more genuinely Sāṁkhyan parallel.

The inferential operation is specific:

> **not every resemblance is equally probative; identify the feature that is structurally diagnostic before using resemblance in a historical comparison.**

This does not yet settle the genealogy. It controls what kind of similarity would even count as relevant evidence.

### 1898

The published article is not principally about Sāṁkhya priority, so this operation recedes. Its analogue appears in the insistence that apparently repeated terms be semantically distinguished before repetition is taken as proof of accidental compilation.

### 1902

The same control becomes explicit at a much larger scale. Lovejoy notes the affinity between Aristotle’s changeless/self-sufficient deity and Vedānta, then rejects the suggestion that resemblance itself warrants an Eastern source after Alexander’s Asiatic campaigns. The Greek conception can be reconstructed from an internal Platonic/Eleatic/Socratic development, so historical diffusion is unnecessary to explain the similarity.

This is a much stronger published formulation of the rule:

> **structural affinity and historical transmission are different propositions and require different evidence.**

The connection to p.17 is operational, not verbal. In both cases Lovejoy asks what resemblance can legitimately support.

## 2. Question-frame control: what a system refuses is not what it lacks

### 004

On PDF p.18 the notebook treats the Buddhist refusals concerning infinity, eternity, soul/body relation and post-mortem existence. Warren 1896 already supplied two explanations: such questions take for granted what Buddha denies, and Buddha also has a `purely practical aim` hostile to metaphysical speculation.

Lovejoy reweights these alternatives. The refusals should not be read merely as the philosophical agnosticism of a practical teacher; they rest on an objection to the **form of the questions put**, while Buddhism remains a doctrine of salvation based on insight.

The next pages make the positive consequence explicit. PDF p.20 defines the `present effort` as reconstructing Buddhism’s general philosophical position and its technical working-out in dependent origination.

The rule is therefore:

> **before coding silence as lack of theory, identify whether the question itself imposes categories the system refuses. Then reconstruct the system’s own problem-form.**

### 1898

The JAOS paper applies the same discipline to a different framing problem. Lovejoy resists readings that convert Buddhist `avijjā` and dependent origination into an ontological/metaphysical problem alien to the psychological and soteriological work the formula performs. The theoretical content required for salvation is to be reconstructed through Buddhist psychology rather than an imported ultimate-metaphysical schema.

### 1902

`Religion and the Time-Process` no longer stages the rule around unanswered Buddhist questions, but it repeatedly reconstructs doctrines from the **problem or logical motive** that generates them rather than from a surface formula alone. Historically different systems can express related `perfectionist` presuppositions while answering different problems.

Thus the question-frame operation becomes less local and more comparative: the historian asks what conceptual problem makes a proposition intelligible before classifying it by verbal resemblance.

## 3. Functional non-equivalence: repetition is not identity

### Source environment

Warren 1896 already calls the twelve-link dependent-origination formula a `piece of patchwork` because the sentient being appears to be generated twice. Senart 1896 uses the alleged recurrence of the skandha complex through `upādāna` as evidence against the formula’s coherence.

Lovejoy therefore does not discover compositeness or repetition.

### 004

The notebook tests the apparent duplication by mapping the khandhas onto the nidānas and by tracing terms through multiple textual contexts. It increasingly asks whether categories that recur actually perform the same work in the causal structure.

### 1898

This becomes secure published argument.

- The five skandhas are not identical with `upādāna`; they are the objects/conditions subject to attachment, while `upādāna` is the attachment itself.
- `karma` and `upādāna` both bear on rebirth but are causally non-equivalent: karma explains the **particular condition** of rebirth, while upādāna explains the **fact of rebirth as such**.

The rule is clear:

> **formal recurrence, shared object, or common historical source does not establish conceptual identity; identity must be tested by semantic and causal function.**

This is probably the single most precise inferential operation recoverable from the 004→1898 transformation.

### 1902

The same logic operates on larger conceptual units. Lovejoy follows a `perfectionist` presupposition across Plato, Aristotle, Christianity and later idealism without treating those historically different systems as identical. Near the end he decomposes Royce into **two incompatible logical motives** or `warring categories` coexisting in one doctrine.

Thus the scale changes:

- 1898: distinguish functions of technical Buddhist terms inside one formula;
- 1902: distinguish logical motives/categories inside and across historically different philosophical systems.

The continuity is not that `upādāna` became a `unit-idea`; it is the prior analytical rule that **components must be discriminated by function rather than by container or recurrence**.

## 4. Genealogy versus configuration: borrowing does not settle meaning

### 004

The notebook repeatedly encounters historically inherited categories: Sāṁkhya/Nyāya logical material, pre-Buddhist khandha formulas, Indic current ideas and Senart’s claim of composite nidānas. PDF p.20 explicitly states that the technical working-out of Buddhism’s general position must be understood in connection with current Indic ideas while also analysing the twelve notions in their interrelation.

This is already a two-level problem:

- where components come from;
- what they do in this configuration.

### 1898

Lovejoy states the distinction in unmistakably authorial prose. The terms, and `perhaps even the ideas`, may be largely borrowed, while their **arrangement and application are original and characteristic**.

That sentence is the hinge of the whole 004 line. It denies two inverse shortcuts:

1. **historical derivation does not exhaust conceptual explanation**;
2. **systemic intelligibility does not require pristine or unitary historical origin**.

The historian must reconstruct both genealogy and configuration.

### 1902

The same dual control becomes comparative history. Lovejoy follows recurring categories/presuppositions across traditions but refuses to convert recurrence into diffusion where an internal genealogy suffices. Conversely, a recurrent `perfectionist` motive can be analytically tracked even when its historical combinations differ.

This is the point at which the 1898 rule becomes recognizably adjacent to later history-of-ideas practice:

> a concept can recur, migrate or be recombined without its historical identity being inferred simply from surface similarity; its logical consequences and combinations must also be reconstructed.

Again, mature `unit-idea` vocabulary should not be projected backward. The continuity is a **division of explanatory labour between genealogy and logical configuration**.

## 5. Scale change from 004 to 1902

The progression can now be described without teleology.

### 004: local research workshop

Object: one disputed Buddhist/Sāṁkhya source field.

Operations:
- excerpt authorities;
- distinguish copied source from supplementation;
- redefine diagnostic doctrinal features;
- test question presuppositions;
- diagram causal/category relations;
- reconstruct a technical system from heterogeneous components.

### 1898: narrow publishable intervention

Object: two Buddhist technical terms and their place in dependent origination/Nirvāṇa discourse.

Operations retained:
- semantic discrimination;
- causal-role discrimination;
- compositeness without unintelligibility;
- borrowed material versus distinctive arrangement/application.

Much of the broader chronology and psychology is pruned.

### 1902: comparative category-history

Object: the religious/philosophical valuation of time/process across Indian, Greek, Christian and modern systems.

Operations generalized:
- isolate categories/presuppositions/logical motives;
- distinguish logical recurrence from historical diffusion;
- trace recombination and survival across named systems;
- decompose a doctrine into conflicting inherited motives;
- use the 1898 Buddhist analysis as one substantive case inside the wider history.

The movement is therefore not `Buddhist notebook → unit-ideas`. It is:

> **local source criticism and system reconstruction → published functional discrimination → comparative tracking of categories and logical motives.**

## 7. Historiographical payoff

Wilson already recognized the early religion/history-of-philosophy papers as part of the prehistory of Lovejoy’s history of ideas. The new contribution is therefore not the existence of an early bridge.

The notebook adds a different evidentiary level:

> **we can reconstruct how selected inferential operations were made from named reading materials before they appeared in print, and then observe which of those operations Lovejoy generalized by 1902.**

This is particularly useful because source control also shows what cannot be credited to Lovejoy:

- decomposition/recombination is already in Rhys Davids;
- the false-presupposition versus practical-aim distinction is already juxtaposed in Warren;
- historical patchwork is already proposed by Warren and Senart;
- recovery of greater coherence is also being attempted by Oldenberg.

Lovejoy’s identifiable intervention therefore lies in **reweighting, functional discrimination, and reconstruction of configuration**, not in inventing the whole comparative apparatus.
