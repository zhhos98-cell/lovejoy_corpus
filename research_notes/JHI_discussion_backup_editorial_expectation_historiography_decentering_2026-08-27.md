## 3. Notebook/published-text comparison should be distributed, not isolated

A separate `manuscript versus publication` section would interrupt the notebook-through-line. The stronger structure is to place one to three notebook/print comparisons at the exact notebook loci where the issue becomes visible.

### A. Warren/Senart → 1898

Notebook 004 preserves a live research environment: copied authorities, corrections, broader Sāṃkhya/Buddhism questions, and disputes over the twelve nidānas.

The 1898 article narrows this field. It retains the `upādāna` controversy and the distinction between borrowing and `arrangement and application`, while much wider chronology, taxonomy and system-level speculation recede.

Writing consequence: publication is not a transparent copy of the notebook; it selects which claims Lovejoy is willing to carry into print.

### B. manuscript p.123 → 1898

On 004 manuscript p.123 / PDF p.42, `viññāṇa` is handled under classificatory, temporal and logical questions at once.

The printed article compresses such open mapping into a more ordered public argument about dependent origination and rebirth.

Writing consequence: do not call this `methodological maturation`; simply show that print turns a page containing several concurrent questions into a narrower argument.

### C. `upādisesa` notebook apparatus → 1898 close

The notebook preserves etymologies, competing passages, lexical problems and stronger possibilities concerning Nirvāṇa.

The published article explicitly limits what etymology can prove, turns toward documented usage, retains contrary evidence and withholds complete settlement.

Writing consequence: this is a particularly clean example of Lovejoy narrowing a claim between working paper and publication.

Overall first-part conclusion can therefore be very plain:

> the published article is much narrower than the notebook from which much of its material emerged.

## 4. Why the historiography question now matters

The novelty cannot be `Lovejoy studied Buddhism`.

Daniel J. Wilson already knew and documented much of the formation. The more precise question is how later historiographies **classified** that known material.

The emerging pattern is not one of simple forgetting. It is one of differently organized disciplinary memories.

### Wilson

Wilson preserves:

- comparative religion at Harvard;
- Lanman, Sanskrit and Buddhist texts;
- Paris;
- Lévi;
- the 1898 article;
- the 1901 Buddhism syllabus.

Yet his biographical and bibliographical architecture gives denser retrospective methodological routes to other early materials: religion/temporalism, Bruno/Spinoza, evolution and the later *Great Chain*.

The Buddhist/Indic strand remains visible but does not become an equally dense genealogy of Lovejoy's historical practice.

Canonical control:

- `research_notes/Wilson_1982_Indic_reception_topology_consolidated.md`.

### Simon Schaffer, 2010

Schaffer is a stronger later control because he is not merely writing biography. `Lovejoy's Series` directly reconstructs Lovejoy's historiography through seriality, evolution, hierarchy, intellectual authority and *The Great Chain of Being*.

Schaffer nevertheless includes the biographical statement that Lovejoy went to Paris and studied Pāli and Sanskrit. He does not develop that fact into a discussion of Buddhism, Lanman, Lévi, the 1898 paper or the technical work behind it.

This gives a strong safe statement:

> Lovejoy's Asian-language formation remained biographically visible even inside later methodological historiography, while the scholarly work performed within it remained analytically thin.

Direct extraction/control:

- `research_notes/Schaffer_2010_Pali_Sanskrit_historiographical_control_2026-08-27.md`.

### Indological/Buddhist memory

The complementary possibility is that specialist Indological/Buddhist literature preserves Lovejoy as the author of the 1898 technical intervention while having little reason to preserve the later founder of the history of ideas.

The currently discussed Karttunen case is useful as a candidate witness because it appears to preserve the 1898 article while treating Buddhist study as a youthful detour. Before publication use, verify the exact text and bibliographic status directly.

### Later Lovejoy reassessment

Recent Lovejoy scholarship has already complicated a simple Skinnerian caricature and has reconstructed actual practice from other texts, including European and Chinese cases. Therefore the article should not claim novelty merely by saying:

- Lovejoy cared about context;
- Skinner was wrong;
- unit-ideas were more flexible than caricature suggests;
- Lovejoy sometimes worked outside Europe.

The missing connection is narrower: **known Asian formation + technical notebook practice + later methodological genealogy have rarely been treated as one historical problem.**

- `research_notes/JHI_blog_historiography_novelty_control_consolidated.md`.

## 5. The historiographical map itself performs a kind of decentering

The useful move is not to replace a Western center with an Indic/Buddhist center.

The stronger historical result is that different later histories made different parts of Lovejoy central or peripheral:

- biography preserves one configuration;
- Buddhist/Indological specialist memory preserves another;
- history-of-ideas historiography preserves another;
- mature Lovejoy reorganizes the geography and function of earlier materials again.

This means there is no single later `Lovejoy` from which the significance of the early notebooks can simply be read backward.

Useful formulation for internal drafting:

> The problem is not that one missing geography must be restored to the centre of Lovejoy's story, but that different later histories made different parts of his early work central and peripheral.

This also protects against a coherence myth. The article need not make the young Lovejoy into the embryo of the mature historian of ideas in order to explain why the early notebooks matter.

## 7. Why the editor's *Great Chain* question is productive rather than automatically teleological

A first reaction to `Lovejoy + Buddhism` is naturally `what happens in *The Great Chain of Being*?` That question risks reading the mature Lovejoy as the automatic endpoint of early materials.

The article should use the question rather than reject it.

The mature comparison is necessary because later works do redistribute the role of Asian materials. But it should be asked after the notebook has been reconstructed on its own terms.

Thus:

- notebook first;
- publication comparison during the notebook discussion;
- later texts as later placements, not as the hidden truth of the notebook;
- *Great Chain* as a closing comparison rather than a developmental telos.

This allows the article to expose, rather than reproduce, the tendency of canonical historiography to make mature works explain early ones.

## 8. Informal Lovejoy/Skinner mirror: retain only as a private thinking device

The conversation noted an amusing stereotype-level mirror:

- canonical caricature of Lovejoy: persistent ideas across contexts;
- canonical caricature of Skinner: usage, utterance and context;
- young Lovejoy's Buddhist work frequently insists on usage, specific textual context and limits on what resemblance/etymology can prove;
- later reassessments of Skinner often make his practice appear less opposed to long-range conceptual history than the old binary suggests.

This is useful for detecting how disciplinary caricatures simplify both figures.

It should not become a first-order claim such as:

- `young Lovejoy was proto-Skinner`;
- `late Skinner became Lovejoy`;
- `the two methods are secretly identical`.

If used at all, the public version should be very restrained: close reconstruction of early Lovejoy makes the later Lovejoy/contextualism opposition less tidy than its canonical shorthand.
