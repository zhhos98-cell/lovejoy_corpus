# JHI -> Skinner -> JHI critique -> Skinner reply -> Parekh/Berki rejoinder, 1961–1976

Date: 2026-08-31
Status: **JHI 1973 DIRECT ISSUE CONTROL / SKINNER 1974 DIRECT RESPONSE TEXT + OFFICIAL SAGE METADATA / PAREKH-BERKI 1976 OFFICIAL METADATA / 1973 FULL ARTICLE FACSIMILE STILL DESIRABLE**

## Core result

The methodological relation between the *Journal of the History of Ideas* ecology and Quentin Skinner can no longer be represented as a one-way succession:

`LOVEJOY / JHI`
-> `SKINNER 1969 CRITIQUE`
-> `CAMBRIDGE CONTEXTUALISM`.

The documentary sequence now supports a genuine argumentative round trip:

`WIENER 1961, JHI — source discrimination is a condition of influence`
->
`SKINNER 1969 — formalizes influence tests and explicitly credits Wiener on condition (ii)`
->
`PAREKH + BERKI 1973, JHI — full article criticizing Skinner's methodology`
->
`SKINNER 1974, POLITICAL THEORY — extended reply, repeatedly naming Parekh/Berki and restating/revising his position`
->
`BERKI + PAREKH 1976, POLITICAL THEORY — published communication replying to Skinner's 1974 article`.

The strongest historiographic conclusion is not that JHI possessed a unified counter-method to Skinner. It is:

> **The history-of-ideas field was an active site in the formation, criticism, and revision of contextualist methodology. A proof-burden problem articulated inside JHI fed into Skinner's 1969 critique; a later JHI article then contested Skinner; Skinner answered those objections at length; Parekh and Berki answered again. The canonical 'rupture' was historically a debate with traffic in both directions.**

Critical evidence rule:

`ARTICLE IN JHI`
!=
`JHI OFFICIAL / CORPORATE POSITION`.

No editorial evidence has yet been recovered showing that the Parekh/Berki article was commissioned as an institutional rebuttal or represented Wiener/the editors collectively.

## II. Skinner 1974 directly names the two older assumptions he was attacking

Skinner's `Some Problems in the Analysis of Political Thought and Action`, *Political Theory* 2.3 (August 1974), 277–303, is an extended methodological response to critics.

Official SAGE metadata confirms article, issue, date and pagination. A searchable full-text carrier preserves the article text.

Skinner retrospectively says his 1966/1969 methodological essays were polemical attacks on two prevailing assumptions in the study of classic political texts:

1. the belief — which he says Parekh and Berki `unrepentantly` return to — that in some cases a text's intelligibility lies inside the text itself and context is unnecessary;
2. the belief that a satisfactory history can be constructed from the `unit ideas` contained in classic texts, or by linking texts into chains of alleged influences.

This is crucial for the Lovejoy/JHI project because Skinner himself joins:

`UNIT-IDEA HISTORY`
+
`CHAIN-OF-INFLUENCE HISTORY`

as methodological targets, while his own 1969 influence criterion had already explicitly credited Wiener 1961 for one source-discrimination condition.

Thus the relation is already internally non-binary:

`SKINNER CRITIQUES A HISTORY-OF-IDEAS PRACTICE`
while
`SKINNER ACKNOWLEDGES HISTORY-OF-IDEAS PRIOR ART ON ONE OF THE PROOF PROBLEMS`.

## III. Parekh/Berki objection 1: complex works and definite intention

Skinner says Parekh and Berki criticize him for assuming that the author of a complex work can be described as having a definite intention in performing a single action to bring about a definite result, citing their p.169.

Skinner's reply is categorical: he says he **never made that assumption** and had explicitly rejected it in earlier work.

This makes the first dispute partly a disagreement over what Skinner's intentionalism commits him to.

Relation to the larger repo problem:

`RECOVERY OF INTENTION`
!=
`ONE WORK = ONE DEFINITE INTENTION`.

This is another relation-typing problem: the existence of intentional action does not license collapsing a complex work into one unitary intention.

Before publication-level analysis of Parekh/Berki, their p.169 should still be read directly to determine whether Skinner represents the objection fairly and how they formulate their alternative.

---

## IV. Parekh/Berki objection 2: is intention sufficient for understanding?

Skinner says Parekh and Berki also criticize him for treating the recovery and statement of an author's intentions as sufficient in themselves for proper understanding, citing their p.170.

Again Skinner says this is not his claim. He restates his distinction:

- recovery of authorial intentions is **among** the interpreter's tasks;
- it does not constitute the whole of interpretation.

This yields a useful logical control:

`NECESSARY TASK`
!=
`SUFFICIENT ACCOUNT`.

This distinction matters because simplified retrospective accounts of the Cambridge School often collapse `intention is necessary to historical meaning` into `intention exhausts meaning`.

The 1973–74 exchange shows that this ambiguity was already being fought over almost immediately.

---

## V. The deepest dispute: can some classic texts make sense without specific context?

The most consequential difference is less about whether context ever matters than about **whether historical context is always a necessary condition of historical understanding**.

Skinner says even Parekh and Berki concede that context is sometimes necessary, but they insist that in other cases `the context is negligible and the audience irreducibly general and transhistorical` (he cites their p.174).

He further reports their view that Hobbes's *Leviathan* is perhaps the clearest classic text which can make eminent sense on its own and lacks a specific context or limited identifiable audience, citing pp.170 and 173.

Skinner answers by making *Leviathan* his strongest counter-case. His historical articles on Hobbes, he says, aim to show that even such a seemingly transhistorical classic cannot be understood precisely without reconstructing what Hobbes was doing relative to prevailing conventions of political argument.

The real fault line can therefore be typed as:

`CONTEXT SOMETIMES EXPLANATORILY RELEVANT`
vs
`HISTORICAL CONTEXT NECESSARY FOR RECOVERING HISTORICAL MEANING`.

That is much more precise than `textualists vs contextualists`.

---

## VI. Skinner turns the methodological dispute into an empirical wager

A striking feature of Skinner's response is that he does not leave the dispute at abstract hermeneutics.

He says Parekh and Berki reject his claims while failing to discuss the new historical information he had offered about Hobbes's context; he therefore treats the methodological controversy as partly testable by historical casework.

He then invokes Locke as another example: the absence of appeal to the prescriptive force of the ancient English constitution becomes meaningful only against contemporary conventions of political obligation, allowing the historian to ask what Locke was doing by omitting an otherwise prestigious argumentative resource.

This is highly relevant to the Lovejoy project because it has the same broad architecture as several earlier proof problems:

`ABSTRACT METHODOLOGICAL CLAIM`
-> must be tested against
`PARTICULAR HISTORICAL / TEXTUAL CASES`.

Lovejoy's 1944 language of `working hypotheses` and Skinner's 1974 insistence on historical corroboration should not be called the same method, but both resist methodology becoming self-validating doctrine.

---

## VII. Parekh/Berki's positive alternative: paradigm / tradition line — provisional until direct full text

High-grade later citations characterize the 1973 article as making a case for considering the history of political ideas in terms of **paradigms**. Skinner's response also says Parekh/Berki favor attempts such as W. H. Greenleaf's to write histories of major traditions of political analysis.

This suggests that their critique was not a return to atomized unit-ideas. Their positive direction may instead involve:

`PARADIGM / TRADITION`
+
`LANGUAGE AS DYNAMIC WHOLE`
+
`VARIABLE DEGREE OF CONTEXT-DEPENDENCE`.

Secondary quotation control preserves their argument that language is dynamic and undergoes stretching, ambiguity, new nuances and transformations rather than merely imposing a fixed prior set of conventions.

But this branch remains `DIRECT ARTICLE READ PENDING`. Do not reconstruct Parekh/Berki's complete methodology from Skinner's reply or later summaries.

---

## VIII. Skinner 1974 is itself a methodological revision / clarification, not a static restatement

Skinner explicitly describes his earliest methodological essays as polemical and says he now mildly regrets their tone. He presents the 1974 essay as a chance to make qualifying remarks and remove misunderstandings of his **present views**.

This adds to the prior 1966 -> 1969 revision already documented in the companion dossier.

The better Skinner chronology is:

`1966 — strong skepticism about historical influence explanations`
->
`1969 — influence retained under strict tests; Wiener credited on source discrimination`
->
`1970–72 — speech-act / convention / intention refinements`
->
`1973 — Parekh/Berki JHI critique`
->
`1974 — Skinner clarifies limits of intentionalism, necessity vs sufficiency, and empirical contextual casework`.

Contextualism therefore has its own internal history of revision.

`JHI 1973 CRITIQUE`

`POLITICAL THEORY 1974 SYMPOSIUM / RESPONSE`.

This is a migration of methodological controversy across journals and disciplinary spaces, exactly the kind of cross-jurisdiction movement the wider Lovejoy/JHI project is reconstructing.

## XI. Revised longue-durée methodological map

The strongest current sequence across the repo is now:

### 1898 — Lovejoy Buddhist/Pali casework

`classification`
!= `chronology`
!= `causation`
!= `derivation`.

### 1906–14 — primitive religion

`category/type`
!= `chronological priority`
!= `nominal definition`.

### 1944 — Spitzer/Lovejoy dispute

`idea/content presence`
!= `continuity`
!= `causal explanation`;
methodological propositions as historically testable working hypotheses.

### 1948 — Mandelbaum

`recurrence/similarity`
!= `historical continuity/transmission`;
diffusion vs independent invention.

### 1948 — Pearce

`analytic decomposition`
-> needs `cultural-process synthesis`.

### 1961 — Wiener

`pupil/proximity`
!= `discipleship/influence`;
alternative-source discrimination required.

### 1969 — Skinner

three influence tests + broader attack on decontextualized doctrine/history; Wiener explicitly credited on condition (ii).

### 1973 — Parekh/Berki in JHI

challenge Skinner on intention, sufficiency, universal necessity of context, and apparently propose paradigm/tradition alternatives.

### 1974 — Skinner response

clarifies `necessary != sufficient`, `intention != single unitary intention`, insists contextual claims require empirical historical corroboration.

### 1976 — Parekh/Berki rejoinder

exchange continues.

The safe historical thesis is not `one method gradually perfected itself`. It is:

> **A recurrent jurisdictional problem — what kind of evidence licenses what kind of historical relation — repeatedly generated new methodological disputes across changing objects, institutions and journals.**

## XII. Historiographical payoff: replace rupture with argumentative ecology

The canonical memory of intellectual history often turns Skinner 1969 into a boundary event separating an old `history of ideas` from a new contextualism.

The recovered sequence suggests a different research question:

> **How did a heterogeneous history-of-ideas ecology help generate the very methodological problems by which it was later declared superseded, and how did its journals and adjacent venues continue to contest those solutions after the supposed rupture?**

This is stronger than trying to rehabilitate Lovejoy against Skinner. It historicizes the making of the methodological canon itself.

Possible article architecture:

`EARLY OBJECT-LEVEL PROOF PROBLEMS`
-> `INTERWAR FIELD / JHI INFRASTRUCTURE`
-> `POSTWAR INTERNAL METHOD ARGUMENTS`
-> `WIENER 1961 INFLUENCE CRITERION`
-> `SKINNER 1969 SYSTEMATIZATION`
-> `PAREKH/BERKI 1973 COUNTERCRITIQUE`
-> `SKINNER 1974 CLARIFICATION`
-> `LATER MEMORY OF A CLEAN BREAK`.

The final section could ask why this reciprocal argument was later remembered as a one-directional methodological revolution.
