# Lovejoy / AAUP 1913–1915 — programme -> open questions -> case stress test -> prescriptive closure

Date: 2026-09-03

## Core correction

The striking overlap between the July 1915 Utah inquiry and Lovejoy's late-1915 handwritten Practical Proposals is real, but it must not be described as though the Utah case invented the four proposals.

Several relevant objects already existed before Utah:

- Lovejoy's 1913 Hopkins Call already listed tenure, dismissal, professional ethics, and a representative judicial committee for academic-freedom/professional-injury cases as possible national-association activities;
- the Dec 1914 Preliminary Report of the Joint Committee of Nine already joined academic freedom to tenure and explicitly posed the unresolved questions whether a faculty member should have a hearing before dismissal and whether such a hearing should be before a faculty committee;
- Lovejoy's 1914 `Profession of the Professorate` already proposed substantial faculty jurisdiction over university personnel/governance.

What Utah supplies is different:

> **a high-pressure empirical stress test in which the pre-existing programmatic questions appear as specific institutional failures and can be analyzed proposition by proposition.**

The 1915 General Report then supplies a prescriptive closure, with its Practical Proposals based entirely on Lovejoy's handwritten notes but revised/softened by the committee.

The safest model is:

`PROGRAMMATIC POSSIBILITY`
-> `OPEN QUESTION`
-> `CASE STRESS TEST`
-> `NORMATIVE / PROCEDURAL SPECIFICATION`
-> `COMMITTEE REVISION / FINAL RULE`.

## I. 1913 Hopkins Call — the menu already contains tenure, dismissal and judicial investigation

Tiede reconstructs the Hopkins Call as coordinated by Lovejoy; in May 1913 Lovejoy had sent Cattell a first draft of the call, and the Hopkins signatories launched the organization campaign from it.

The Call conceptualizes professors as members of:

- disciplinary communities;
- local faculties;
- a broader professoriate.

It proposes a national association as:

- forum for educational questions;
- organ for professional pronouncements;
- vehicle for collective action.

Among possible topics/activities, it already identifies:

- university governance;
- principles of **tenure**;
- principles of **dismissal**;
- professional ethics;
- creation of a **representative judicial committee** to investigate violations of academic freedom or serious/unwarranted injury to a professor's professional standing/opportunities.

Thus:

`TENURE`
+
`DISMISSAL`
+
`JUDICIAL PROFESSIONAL INVESTIGATION`

are **pre-Utah programmatic objects**.

Do not call them products of the Utah investigation.

At the same time, the Call expressly presents such topics as examples rather than a predetermined action programme.

## II. Dec 1914 Preliminary Report — programme becomes unanswered institutional questions

The Joint Committee of Nine's Preliminary Report is deliberately tentative.

Tiede describes it as consisting largely of questions a later final report would need to answer.

It already:

- links academic freedom and security of tenure;
- treats dismissal as the most severe disciplinary measure;
- justifies tenure through disinterested scholarship, common good and attractiveness of the profession;
- refuses to prescribe the exact mechanism by which tenure should be secured.

At the procedural boundary it asks:

`SHOULD FACULTY HAVE A HEARING BEFORE POSSIBLE DISMISSAL?`

and, if so,

`SHOULD THAT HEARING BE BEFORE A FACULTY COMMITTEE?`

The committee does **not yet answer these questions**.

Thus:

`1913 MENU`
-> `1914 OPEN DESIGN QUESTIONS`.

This is crucial: due-process questions precede Utah, but remain unresolved.

## III. Utah Apr–Jul 1915 — the open questions become failure modes

Lovejoy's Utah investigation directly tests precisely the institutional variables that the earlier programme/report had left open.

### A. Tenure / reappointment

One of the first formal questions to the Regents asks whether professors acquire a definite presumption of continuous reappointment after satisfactory service.

The answer reveals:

- no distinct/definite rule;
- an informal presumption based on practice;
- one-year formal appointments;
- statutory termination at will.

Diagnostic state:

`INFORMAL EXPECTATION`
!=
`DEFINED TENURE RULE`.

### B. Grounds for dismissal

The report states that Utah has no permanent statute/regulation defining pertinent and sufficient grounds for dismissal.

Grounds are determined case by case by the sitting president/regents.

Diagnostic state:

`DISMISSAL GROUNDS`
= `AD HOC OFFICIAL JUDGMENT`

rather than

`PREDECLARED INSTITUTIONAL RULE`.

### C. Hearing / judicial procedure

The report directly asks whether Regents claim power to refuse reappointment:

- without specific charges;
- without a hearing.

Majority answer: yes in principle.

The inquiry then analyzes whether the accused faculty in fact received a **fair trial** and whether truth of charges was adequately ascertained.

Diagnostic state:

`OPPORTUNITY TO SPEAK`
!=
`JUDICIAL HEARING / FAIR TRIAL`.

### D. Faculty role in personnel/governance

The report documents:

- faculty request for hearings ignored/not acted upon;
- earlier faculty petition on tenure and faculty-regent relations effectively tabled;
- faculty complaint that Regents were inaccessible;
- president possessing de facto control of personnel recommendations without obligation to seek faculty advice;
- board typically adopting recommendations without independent inquiry.

Diagnostic state:

`FACULTY PROFESSIONAL RESPONSIBILITY`
without
`FORMAL PERSONNEL / GOVERNANCE JURISDICTION`.

This is especially consonant with Lovejoy's 1914 governance programme, but Utah turns the abstract jurisdiction issue into a concrete institutional pathology.

## IV. The Utah report is not just data collection; it is a procedural stress test

The report's own design is important:

- sides fail to `join issue` on key points;
- Secretary formulates fact questions;
- Regents answer 22 questions;
- President answers 17;
- evidence includes resigners/nonresigners, dismissed faculty, students, affidavits, public documents, governor, etc.;
- report is explicitly analysis rather than narrative;
- direct/indirect evidence is separated;
- suspicious facts are not allowed to prove motives without warrant;
- absent direct testimony can cause evidence to be disregarded for final conclusions;
- unresolved residue remains unresolved.

Thus Utah shows not only **what institutional variables matter** but also **how a profession-level judicial body might investigate them**.

## V. July -> late 1915 chronology licenses transformation, not single-case causation

Chronology:

- Mar/Apr 1915: Utah case taken up; Lovejoy travels/investigates;
- July 1915: Utah report published;
- Aug 1915: Seligman circulates report-planning material; Ford proposes survey-first method;
- 21 Aug: Lovejoy objects that existing practice is too unregularized; principles must be formulated first, then practice surveyed;
- Lovejoy handwritten `Suggestions for General Report` are undated but precede Seligman's late-November prefinal draft;
- late Nov/Thanksgiving: prefinal and committee/subcommittee revision;
- Dec 1915: final General Report/Declaration.

Therefore the Utah diagnostic state clearly precedes Lovejoy's preserved Practical-Proposal substrate.

But:

`TEMPORAL PRIORITY + STRUCTURAL MATCH`
!=
`UTAH ALONE CAUSED PROPOSALS`.

The Committee A policy report drew on a year containing **five investigations** plus the earlier Joint Committee report and governance debate.

Tiede explicitly says the General Report was based on the preliminary report **and on the experience gained through investigative work**.

AAUP's later first-year account likewise says the policy report reflected Committee A's experiences during the first year.

So the correct relation is:

`PRE-EXISTING PROGRAM / QUESTIONS`
+
`MULTIPLE 1915 INVESTIGATIONS, INCLUDING UTAH`
-> `PRESCRIPTIVE CLOSURE`.

Utah is the strongest directly controlled Lovejoy-drafted stress test, not the exclusive source.

## VII. The strongest pair is `grounds` + `hearing`, not all four equally

Source criticism changes the weighting.

### Faculty participation

Lovejoy already had this programme in 1914.

Utah is evidence of application/relevance, not origin.

### Tenure

Security of tenure was already a broad field/preliminary-report concern.

Utah supplies a particularly clear mismatch between legal one-year form and customary expectation.

### Grounds for dismissal

This is unusually strong because Utah's report explicitly diagnoses the **absence of predefined grounds**, then Lovejoy's own late-1915 notes treat establishment of grounds as **essential** protection against academic-freedom violations.

### Judicial hearing

The question predates Utah, but Utah performs an elaborate substitute hearing and demonstrates why a mere opportunity to appear before a board is not adequate process.

This is later crystallized into specific written charges, fair trial, a faculty judicial body, evidence rights, and specialist evaluation for professional incompetence.

Thus the best same-year transformation is:

`UNDEFINED GROUNDS + FAILED TRUTH-ASCERTAINMENT`
-> `FORMULATE GROUNDS + INSTITUTIONALIZE JUDICIAL HEARING`.

This is much stronger than a generic `Utah led to tenure` statement.

## VIII. The final report itself separates investigation facts from general principles

The General Report says:

- special investigative committees alone are responsible for findings of fact;
- Committee A examines those reports and approves conclusions;
- Committee A separately considers academic freedom `as a whole` and submits a general report;
- principles need arrangements/regulations to prevent infringement and false charges.

Thus even the institutional authors explicitly distinguish:

`CASE FACT-FINDING`
from
`GENERAL PRINCIPLE / PROCEDURE DESIGN`.

The historical relation is transformation across document types, not direct incorporation of Utah findings into the Declaration as one text.

> **PRE-EXISTING PROBLEM != PRE-EXISTING SOLUTION.**
