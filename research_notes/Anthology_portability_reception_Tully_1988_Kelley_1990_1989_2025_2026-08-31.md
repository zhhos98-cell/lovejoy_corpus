# Anthology portability and reception: Tully 1988 and Kelley 1990 as usable methodological canons

Date: 2026-08-31
Status: **CONTEMPORARY REVIEW + LATER RECEPTION + PEDAGOGICAL CONTROL / NOT A QUANTITATIVE CITATION STUDY**

## Core result

The claim that Tully 1988 and Kelley 1990 functioned as **portable canon infrastructures** now has external reception evidence rather than resting only on their tables of contents.

The strongest controls are asymmetrical but complementary:

1. a 1989 review of Tully explicitly says Skinner's methodological writings had not previously been conveniently available under one cover and praises the anthology for remedying that deficiency;
2. later teaching materials cite Skinner's 1969 essay specifically through Tully 1988, showing the anthology became a practical carrier rather than merely a bibliographic curiosity;
3. Quentin Skinner's current institutional profile itself describes his methodological essays as collected in Tully 1988 and later *Regarding Method*, evidence of the anthology's durable role in organizing his methodological corpus;
4. Mark Bevir 2004 explicitly criticizes Kelley 1990 as constructing a canon of history of ideas focused on Lovejoy and predominantly American scholars working in his wake, as distinct from the broader discipline of intellectual history;
5. Kelley 1990 likewise appears as an assigned/readable methodological corpus in later history-of-ideas teaching and was reissued by University of Rochester Press.

Thus the concept `portable canon` can now be treated as an historically observable publication effect.

---

# I. Tully 1988: contemporaneous reviewer explicitly identifies the portability function

Michael L. Morgan's 1989 *Review of Metaphysics* notice gives an unusually clean formulation.

He says Skinner had made major contributions as historian and method theorist, but his methodological articles had **not been conveniently available under one cover**. Morgan says Tully's volume remedies that deficiency by bringing together:

- five important Skinner writings on interpretation;
- Tully's introduction;
- seven critical essays;
- Skinner's long reply.

This is direct reception evidence for the mechanism hypothesized in the competing-canons dossier:

`SCATTERED JOURNAL ARTICLES`
-> `ONE COVER`
-> `CONVENIENT ACCESS`.

The canon effect is therefore not only interpretive. It is logistical.

A methodological corpus becomes easier to assign, cite, purchase, consult and inherit.

---

# II. Tully becomes a durable citation carrier for Skinner's 1969 essay

Later scholarship and syllabi frequently cite `Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas` not through the original *History and Theory* issue but through its reprint in Tully.

A direct example is an Angus Burgin US intellectual-history syllabus published by the Society for U.S. Intellectual History. It assigns:

Quentin Skinner, `Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas`, **in James Tully, ed., *Meaning and Context***, pp.29–67.

This is a small but concrete pedagogical control:

`ORIGINAL ARTICLE`
-> `ANTHOLOGY REPRINT`
-> `TEACHING COPY / COURSE CANON`.

The mechanism should not be generalized statistically from one syllabus, but it demonstrates the pathway the portability hypothesis predicts.

---

# III. Skinner's own current institutional profile still identifies Tully as a collector of the methodological corpus

Quentin Skinner's current Queen Mary University of London profile says that he has written extensively on historical method/explanation and that many of these essays were collected in:

- James Tully, *Meaning and Context: Quentin Skinner and His Critics* (1988);
- Skinner's later *Regarding Method* (2001).

This gives the anthology a durable place in Skinner's own public bibliographic self-presentation.

The important relation is not authorial endorsement of every editorial selection. It is corpus organization:

`TULLY 1988`
functions alongside
`REGARDING METHOD 2001`

as a recognized container for Skinner's methodological writings.

This helps explain why texts inside Tully's package acquire durable portability.

---

# IV. Kelley's canon construction was explicitly recognized and criticized by a later methodological theorist

Mark Bevir's 2004 `Ideas in History` gives direct reception evidence of a different kind.

In a footnote he characterizes Kelley's earlier *The History of Ideas: Canon and Variations* as a construction of a canon focused on:

- Lovejoy;
- predominantly American-based scholars working in Lovejoy's wake;

rather than on a broader discipline of intellectual history.

This is extremely valuable because it independently identifies what the table-of-contents analysis had suggested:

`KELLEY 1990`
= a **bounded canon construction**,
not a neutral anthology of the entire field.

Bevir's objection also shows that Kelley's selection boundary remained historiographically visible enough to become a point of criticism fourteen years later.

Therefore `canon` in Kelley's title was not empty rhetoric. Later scholars could treat the volume's construction of lineage as an argument requiring challenge.

---

# V. Kelley 1990 also becomes a pedagogical corpus

Later course materials assign individual essays through Kelley's anthology rather than only through their original JHI issues.

One cultural-studies/history-of-ideas module assigns Joseph Mazzeo's `Some Interpretations of the History of Ideas` specifically from Kelley, *Canon and Variations*, and describes history of ideas as a `canon with variations` bound to the history of philosophy while undergoing change.

Another historical-method bibliography lists Kelley's volume among major works in historical theory/method.

CiNii library data also show broad institutional holdings, and Google Books records a 1994 Rochester reissue/reprint/revised edition.

These are not enough for quantitative claims about readership, but they establish multiple portability channels:

- library acquisition;
- reissue;
- course assignment;
- citation through the collected volume.

---

# VI. Competing portability produces competing methodological memory

The two anthology packages now have different observed afterlives.

## Tully

Makes Skinner's methodological corpus conveniently available under one cover.

Later users can cite/teach Skinner through the anthology itself.

The package is explicitly organized around:

`SKINNER + CRITICS + REPLY`.

## Kelley

Makes a selected JHI lineage available as a founders/followers/questions history.

Later users can cite/teach JHI methodological pieces through the anthology itself.

A later critic explicitly identifies it as a Lovejoy-centered, predominantly American canon construction.

Thus:

`DIFFERENT PORTABLE BOOKS`
-> `DIFFERENT DEFAULT PATHS INTO THE FIELD`.

A student entering through Tully receives a Skinner-centered controversy.

A student entering through Kelley receives a Lovejoy/JHI-centered genealogy with internal variation.

The clean rupture story can emerge when these routes are subsequently compared as if each were a transparent representation of one field rather than a deliberately bounded publication object.

---

# VII. A useful distinction: availability vs authority

The evidence suggests two analytically separate effects of an anthology.

### `AVAILABILITY EFFECT`

Scattered texts become conveniently obtainable together.

Morgan 1989 directly documents this for Tully.

### `AUTHORITY / CANON EFFECT`

Selection and organization tell readers which texts constitute the meaningful controversy or lineage.

Bevir 2004 directly treats Kelley as constructing a bounded canon.

These effects can reinforce one another but should not be conflated.

A text can have high authority but low availability.

An anthology can increase availability without guaranteeing canonical authority.

The strongest canon infrastructure combines both.

---

# VIII. Parekh/Berki and Wiener 1961 become sharper negative controls

The portability model makes the status of omitted texts easier to define.

Parekh/Berki 1973 remains available in JHI and survives through Skinner's reply, but it is absent from both Tully's selected critic package and Kelley's JHI canon volume.

Wiener 1961 remains available in JHI and is explicitly credited by Skinner 1969, but Kelley selects a different Wiener methodological text for the JHI lineage package.

Therefore their later status can be described as:

`ARCHIVALLY AVAILABLE`
+
`HISTORIOGRAPHICALLY RELEVANT`
+
`LOWER ANTHOLOGY PORTABILITY`.

This is much more precise than `forgotten`.

2. Do the same for Lovejoy/Spitzer/Pearce/Wiener using original JHI vs Kelley 1990 vs other anthologies.
3. Track book reviews of Tully and Kelley from 1988–95 for explicit claims about convenience, comprehensiveness, selection and field representation.
4. Compare citation forms in later methodological surveys: original article vs anthology reprint.
5. Trace whether Parekh/Berki 1973 is more likely to appear where scholars reconstruct `critics of Skinner` independently rather than inherit Tully's critic roster.
6. Trace reissues/editions/translation histories of both anthologies.

A modest sample would be enough to test the mechanism without pretending to reconstruct total readership.

## Evidence firewall

- A handful of syllabi != quantitative pedagogical dominance.
- Library holdings != actual reading.
- Reissue != proven high sales.
- Skinner's current profile identifying Tully as a collection != endorsement of Tully's critic selection.
- Bevir's description is a critical interpretation of Kelley's canon, not neutral fact about the entire field.
- `LOWER ANTHOLOGY PORTABILITY` != `FORGOTTEN`.
