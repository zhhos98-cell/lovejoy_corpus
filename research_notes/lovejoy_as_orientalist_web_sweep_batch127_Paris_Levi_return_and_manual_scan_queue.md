# Batch 127 — Paris 1898–99: Lévi's return-to-teaching chronology and bounded manual scan queue

Date: 2026-08-19  
Status: synced  
Scope: continue the digital-first Paris closure after Batches 125–126. This pass lowers the Lévi chronology from general biographical reconstruction to year-on-year primary EPHE teaching reports, then isolates the few dynamic viewers where a human click is currently more efficient than further generic web searching.

## Core result

The EPHE historical-and-philological annual reports now give a clean primary institutional before/after control around Sylvain Lévi's Asian mission:

- **1897–98:** the report states that Lévi, being on mission in India, **could not direct any conference during the academic year 1897–98**. Louis Finot gave three conferences per week instead.
- **1898–99:** the next report places Lévi back in active teaching and specifies **two conferences per week**, Thursday afternoon and Friday morning. The first was an explanation of the **Bhagavad-Gītā**, with Joseph Trumbull Stickney taking a constant and very active part; the second was a continuous survey of Indian literature for students lacking an elementary synthesis.

This is the strongest direct institutional calibration yet for Wilson's reconstruction from Lovejoy's 1898 letters. It does not independently prove that Lovejoy requested a Pāli-Buddhism course, but it does establish that the regular Lévi teaching actually documented for 1898–99 was Bhagavad-Gītā plus Indian literature, not a Pāli-Buddhism course. The reported private sacred-text reading therefore fits a demonstrable gap between the teaching Lovejoy reportedly wanted and Lévi's documented regular programme.

Primary sources:

- EPHE IVe Section, `XV. Langue sanscrite`, report on 1897–98, Annuaire 1899, pp.61–62: https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0001_1899_num_1_1_2200
- EPHE IVe Section, `XV. Langue sanscrite`, report on 1898–99, Annuaire 1900, pp.65–67: https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0001_1900_num_1_1_2251

## 1. The 1897–98 absence is explicit, not inferred from travel dates

The 1897–98 Sanskrit report says, in substance and explicitly, that S. Lévi was charged with a mission in India and could not direct any conference during that academic year. This matters because earlier reconstruction relied partly on mission chronology and later institutional histories to explain why teaching was unavailable.

We now have a primary institutional statement of non-teaching for the entire 1897–98 academic year.

This should replace any formulation that merely infers his absence from the mission itinerary.

## 2. By the 1898–99 teaching report Lévi is demonstrably back and teaching twice weekly

The 1898–99 report names Lévi as director of studies and records two weekly conferences:

1. Thursday afternoon: explanation of the Bhagavad-Gītā. Stickney is singled out for sustained active participation.
2. Friday morning: a continuous survey of Indian literature, organized as a corrective to the lack of elementary handbooks and built around characteristic works, selected passages, principal scholarship, and the current state of questions.

This is an unusually useful control because it gives the actual regular teaching menu in the same academic year in which Lovejoy is documented elsewhere in the EPHE religious-sciences environment.

Evidence limit: the report does **not** list Lovejoy as an attendee in Lévi's Sanskrit conferences. It establishes availability and content, not Lovejoy's attendance.

## 3. The Vth Section confirms a parallel Indian-philosophy programme, again without a Pāli course

The Vth Section `Religions de l'Inde` report for 1898–99 separately records Alfred Foucher's conference on Indian logical doctrines according to Nyāya, including definition, causation, knowledge, perception, inference, analogy/induction and testimony. This remains a different conference and should not be conflated with Lévi's IVth Section teaching.

Primary source:
https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0002_1898_num_12_8_19504

The resulting institutional matrix is now especially clear:

- IVe Section, Lévi: Bhagavad-Gītā + Indian literature;
- Ve Section, Foucher: Nyāya and related Indian-religions/philosophy material;
- no recovered official 1898–99 EPHE programme in these two tracks advertises the Pāli-Buddhism course Wilson says Lovejoy wanted.

This is a bounded programme-level negative, not proof that no Pāli teaching existed anywhere in Paris.

## 4. Lévi's contemporaneous private residence is now fixed, but the Lovejoy visit address remains an inference

AGORHA's current Lévi authority record gives his private address from **1894 to 1935** as:

**9, rue Guy-de-la-Brosse, Paris 5e.**

A modern social history of French Indian studies independently describes the Lévi family moving there after his 1894 Collège de France election and identifies the apartment as a scholarly/social gathering place around the turn of the century.

Sources:

- AGORHA Lévi record: https://agorha.inha.fr/ark:/54721/85da9f6d-dbb5-4fc9-b665-200ba9d9285f
- Roland Lardinois, *L'invention de l'Inde*, chapter on scholarly practices: https://books.openedition.org/editionscnrs/60557

This gives a concrete geographical control for Wilson's statement that Lovejoy sometimes went to Lévi's home to read sacred texts. However:

> Do **not** yet write that Lovejoy read with Lévi at 9 rue Guy-de-la-Brosse as a documentary fact.

The address is securely Lévi's contemporaneous home; the surviving public evidence checked here does not identify the address in Lovejoy's own 1898 letters. The link remains a high-confidence geographic inference awaiting MS-0873 or another copy of the letters.

## 8. Lévi archive-history caution: do not assume a rich reciprocal correspondence survives

A useful negative control emerged while checking whether another online Lévi archive might make the Lovejoy-side letter unnecessary. Published research on Lévi's papers states that his personal papers were looted from the rue Guy-de-la-Brosse apartment in 1941, taken to Germany, then moved to Moscow. Researchers who consulted that Moscow archive reported **almost no professional correspondence**, only a small number of incoming cards among heterogeneous papers.

A Russian archival guide separately lists `Fond 231 — Levy Silvain` among fonds transferred to France in 2000. At present, the public sources checked here do not establish a one-to-one equivalence between that returned fond and the present Collège de France `41 CDF` archive. Do not merge those shelfmark histories without a provenance document.

Controls:

- Bongard-Levin / Lardinois / Vigasin, `Deux indianistes dans la cité`, Persée: https://www.persee.fr/doc/crai_0065-0536_2002_num_146_2_22467
- Russian archival guide, transferred fonds: https://guides.rusarchives.ru/terms/22/7866/fondy-peredannye-v-strany-ih-proishozhdeniya

Practical consequence: a direct `Lovejoy` hit in the surviving Lévi papers would be excellent, but the archive's transmission history makes it unsafe to treat failure to find one as historically meaningful.

## 9. Revised Paris chronology after this pass

The narrow sequence now supported is:

`1896–97: Lévi teaching Sanskrit in Paris`

→ `1897–98: Lévi on India mission; EPHE report explicitly says no Lévi conferences`

→ `Sep 1898: mission-return window in AGORHA chronology`

→ `1898–99: Lévi demonstrably resumes two weekly EPHE Sanskrit conferences`

→ `regular programme = Bhagavad-Gītā + Indian literature`

→ `Lovejoy's reported desired Pāli-Buddhism course is not among those documented regular offerings`

→ `Wilson reports occasional private sacred-text reading with Lévi`

The strongest safe interpretation is therefore:

> Lovejoy encountered Lévi immediately after a year-long interruption in Lévi's Paris teaching. The recovered 1898–99 institutional record shows Lévi back in regular Sanskrit instruction, but with a Bhagavad-Gītā and Indian-literature programme rather than the Pāli-Buddhism course Lovejoy reportedly sought. Private reading, if Wilson's letter-based reconstruction is confirmed verbatim, should be understood against this precise institutional configuration.

This is materially stronger than saying simply that Lévi had been travelling and therefore may not have offered the desired course.
