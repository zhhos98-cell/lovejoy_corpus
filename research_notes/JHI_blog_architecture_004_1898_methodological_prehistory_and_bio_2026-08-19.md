# JHI Blog architecture: 004 + 1898 article + methodological prehistory

Date: 2026-08-19
Status: active drafting architecture; supplements the existing JHI reframing note

## Core editorial decision

The blog should now be built around a deliberately fast movement through five layers rather than around a source-microhistory:

1. **Skinner + the archival oddity** — begin with the familiar later critique of Lovejoy, then introduce the strange object that disrupts the retrospective picture: notebook `MS38_004_001_061_004`.
2. **004 + Lovejoy's 1898 JAOS article** — establish, with one or two compact manuscript-to-print controls, that Lovejoy was not merely reading Buddhism as background but participating in a live specialist controversy.
3. **One actual Sāṃkhya/Buddhism controversy** — stage the dispute briefly as a methodological conflict over what comparison could prove, then leave technical Buddhist discussion quickly.
4. **Oriental scholarship + classical scholarship** — widen from the controversy to the nineteenth-century disciplinary field, using Max Müller's retrospective `classical and Oriental scholarship` contrast as a framing device rather than an objective disciplinary map.
5. **Methodological genealogy of the history of ideas** — argue that the evidentiary problems later associated with intellectual history were being worked through in a transregional field built around Sanskrit, Pāli, Indian philosophy and Orientalist scholarship. The point is not a simple East/West reversal but a genealogy in which non-Western texts and South Asian scholarly interventions were constitutive rather than peripheral.

The detailed Quellenforschung, page-by-page source uptake, notebook stratigraphy, and full 005/Marillier material remain reserved for a later journal article.

## The opening: Skinner + `the strange thing I found`

The opening should exploit a productive mismatch.

Start from the later disciplinary memory: Lovejoy as the canonical target of contextualist criticism, especially the familiar shorthand in which `unit-ideas` appear detachable from linguistic, argumentative and historical context.

Then pivot immediately to the archival object:

> I went looking through Lovejoy's early papers and found something that did not look much like this retrospective Lovejoy at all.

The `strange thing` is 004: a dense Sanskrit/Pāli/Indological notebook occupied by Sāṃkhya, Buddhism, the nidānas, technical terminology, textual witnesses, historical borrowing, competing chronologies and named contemporary Orientalists.

The point of the hook is not `surprise: Lovejoy knew Buddhism`. It is:

> **the canonical founder of the history of ideas appears, at the beginning of his career, inside another discipline's argument about how historical inference should work.**

This permits Skinner to function as the reader's entry point without turning the essay into a `Skinner was wrong` polemic.

## 004 + 1898 article as the evidentiary pair

The blog needs both objects.

### 004

The notebook demonstrates active participation at working-paper level. It contains direct engagement with Garbe, Rhys Davids, Warren, Senart, Oldenberg and the wider Sāṃkhya/Buddhism/nidāna problem field. Crucially, it preserves Lovejoy's own analytical interventions alongside copied or digested authorities.

### 1898 JAOS article

`The Buddhistic Technical Terms upādāna and upādisesa` supplies the public endpoint and prevents the notebook from being dismissed as miscellaneous reading notes. It shows Lovejoy converting the controversy into a narrow specialist intervention.

The blog should use only one or two controls:

- notebook evidence that an identified Sāṃkhyan doctrine/element does not by itself show that the later Sāṃkhya **system existed as a whole**;
- the 1898 article's separation of **etymology from use**, and of borrowed terms/materials from the originality of their **arrangement and application**.

This is enough to show active methodological participation. The full source genealogy remains for the article.

## One actual controversy, then exit Buddhist technicalities quickly

The Sāṃkhya/Buddhism controversy should occupy perhaps 300–450 words, not the whole essay.

The controversy is useful because its participants occupied different evidentiary positions:

- **Garbe:** sufficiently diagnostic formal similarities can identify borrowed material and potentially its direction.
- **Rhys Davids:** chronology and the lateness of surviving evidence impose a documentary brake.
- **Senart:** duplication and heterogeneity in the nidāna formula can support a compositional/stratificational history.
- **Jacobi:** systematic analogy can sustain a stronger Sāṃkhya-Yoga genealogy.
- **Oldenberg:** internal usage, causal function and source criticism limit what resemblance is entitled to prove historically.
- **Lovejoy:** accepts that borrowing and compositeness may be real while restricting the scale of inference from element/doctrine to whole system and keeping semantic/function questions separate from provenance.

Do not present these as sealed methodological schools. They are distinguishable evidentiary regimes within a shared, entangled philological field.

The blog-level abstraction should be reduced to three questions:

1. **What resembles what?**
2. **What exactly has that resemblance proved — a term, a doctrine, an element, an arrangement, or a whole system?**
3. **When may that resemblance become a historical genealogy?**

Once these questions are established, leave the Buddhist technicalities behind. The essay is about the history of historical reasoning, not primarily about dependent origination.

## Oriental scholarship and classical scholarship

The Max Müller / Mommsen node can widen the frame after the 004/1898 demonstration.

Müller's 1901 autobiography retrospectively staged his 1869 Académie election as a contest `between classical and Oriental scholarship`, claiming that `for once Oriental scholarship carried the day`.

Use this as a self-conscious disciplinary contrast, immediately qualified by the more mixed institutional reality already established in the repo.

The value of the anecdote is that it names a boundary that the essay can then unsettle. The historical procedures at issue — comparison, chronology, textual stratification, source criticism, genealogy, reconstruction — were not neatly contained by a sealed `Oriental scholarship` opposed to a sealed `Classics`.

Lovejoy's early notebook belongs precisely to this unstable field.

The transition can be:

> **Müller remembered the late nineteenth century as a contest between classical and Oriental scholarship. Lovejoy's notebook suggests a different question: what happened to historical reasoning as methods, texts and proof problems moved across that boundary?**

## Non-Western / transregional genealogy of history-of-ideas method

The strongest formulation must be more precise than `the history of ideas has a non-Western origin`.

The evidence does **not** establish a direct Bankim/Mitra → Lovejoy transmission chain. Nor were Sanskrit/Pāli studies institutionally outside European and American scholarship.

But the repo now securely shows that the proof problem was transregional rather than a one-way Europe → India export of method.

- **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay (1871)** distinguishes similarity from identity and then separates the existence of resemblance from the direction of borrowing.
- **Rajendralal Mitra (1883)** distinguishes pre-existing philosophical materials from a complete system and rejects genealogical conjecture based on phonetic resemblance without adequate proof.

These are not merely `Indian data` supplied to European theorists. They are South Asian scholarly interventions into the logic of historical inference itself.

Therefore the blog's larger claim should be:

> **The methodological prehistory of the history of ideas was not exclusively intra-Western. It developed, in part, through transregional arguments about Indian texts in which South Asian as well as European and American scholars contested what resemblance, borrowing, chronology and textual evidence could legitimately prove.**

Or, slightly more provocative but still safe:

> **Part of the non-Western prehistory of the history of ideas lies not in a simple transfer of Asian doctrines into Europe, but in the methodological problems generated by—and debated within—the nineteenth-century study of Indian intellectual traditions.**

This gives `non-Western origin` a precise meaning: not a singular geographic birthplace, but a constituent genealogy of problems, texts, scholarly actors and evidentiary practices that cannot be reduced to the Western philosophical canon.

## Skinner return

Skinner should return only after the historical reconstruction.

The payoff is not:

`Lovejoy was already a contextualist.`

It is:

> **Before contextualism became a critique of the history of ideas, Lovejoy had already been formed in a controversy about the limits of historical inference.**

The irony is useful but must remain historical rather than triumphalist.

A stronger final move:

> The contextualist critique asked intellectual historians to recover the conditions under which arguments acquired meaning. Applying that demand to the history of ideas itself leads somewhere unexpected: back to Sanskrit and Pāli scholarship, to controversies over Indian philosophy, and to a scholarly field in which the future founder of the history of ideas was learning what counted as historical proof.

Possible final sentence:

> **To contextualize the history of ideas is therefore also to recover the non-Western texts and transregional scholarly arguments through which one of its founders first learned to make history.**

## Author positioning / deliberately unexpected byline

The author's short bio can reinforce the essay's argument by making the route into the archive itself slightly unexpected.

The useful juxtaposition is:

- **early modern historian / manuscript and intellectual-history work**;
- **formal Sanskrit training across SOAS and RYI**.

This mirrors the essay's own crossing of disciplinary boundaries without turning the bio into a joke or a credential list.

Possible compact form, to be verified and polished against the exact preferred institutional wording before submission:

> **Haohao Zhang is an early modern historian working on manuscripts, intellectual history, and the history of scholarly practices. He has also trained in Sanskrit at SOAS and RYI, an unlikely combination that led him into Arthur Lovejoy's Buddhist notebooks.**

More restrained:

> **Haohao Zhang is an early modern historian working on manuscripts and intellectual history, with additional training in Sanskrit at SOAS and RYI. His current research follows the movement of historical practices across disciplinary and textual traditions.**

More deliberately `strange`, if the venue permits a slightly personal bio:

> **Haohao Zhang is an early modern historian with Sanskrit training at SOAS and RYI. The combination is largely responsible for why he was reading Arthur Lovejoy's Pāli notes in the first place.**

Do not finalize the institutional names or affiliation syntax until submission; use the exact official names the author prefers at that point.

## Current thesis after this revision

> **Arthur Lovejoy's early Buddhist notebook and his 1898 specialist article place the future founder of the history of ideas inside a transregional nineteenth-century controversy over the logic of historical inference. The dispute over Sāṃkhya and Buddhism was also a dispute over method: what counted as a significant resemblance, what scale of historical entity that resemblance could establish, and when comparison could become genealogy. Read against the unstable nineteenth-century boundary between `classical` and `Oriental` scholarship—and against South Asian interventions by figures such as Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay and Rajendralal Mitra—Lovejoy's early archive suggests that the methodological prehistory of the history of ideas was never exclusively intra-Western. To historicize Lovejoy is therefore to recover the Sanskrit, Pāli and transregional scholarly worlds in which he first learned to make historical claims.**
