The Marillier genealogy behind notebook 005 can now be grounded in a much stronger primary-text control. A searchable full-text reproduction of *Revue de l’histoire des religions*, tome XXXVI (1897), derived from the BnF/Gallica scan, makes it possible to collate the first two installments of Léon Marillier’s:

> **“La place du totémisme dans l’évolution religieuse, à propos d’un livre récent (F. B. Jevons: *An Introduction to the History of Religion*)”**

Publication sequence:

- *RHR* 36 (1897), pp.208–253;
- *RHR* 36 (1897), pp.321–369;
- continuation in *RHR* 37 (1898), pp.204–233 and 345–404.

This batch collates the 1897 installments. The result is unusually important for 005 because Marillier states, in direct primary prose, the same analytical rule that Lovejoy later repeatedly executes in the notebook:

> **a partial explanation may be true without being the only explanation; outward resemblance does not establish a common causal class; several distinct but analogous belief/practice complexes may exist in parallel; one must resist deriving all ritual forms from a single origin.**

The historical claim is **programmatic/methodological continuity**, not proof that Lovejoy read this printed article. The direct institutional bridge remains Lovejoy’s documented active participation in Marillier’s 1898–99 EPHE conferences.

## 1. Marillier explicitly attacks the reduction of sacrifice to one original sacramental type

At the opening of the second installment (printed article begins at p.321), Marillier summarizes the Jevons / Robertson Smith system. Among the doctrines he singles out is:

> `la réduction des multiples formes de sacrifice à un seul type originel, le sacrifice d’union ou sacrifice sacramentaire, d’où procéderaient tous les autres`

He immediately argues that Jevons’s very systematic rigor exposes the artificiality of the construction. The facts, Marillier says, overflow the narrow frames imposed upon them because Jevons tries to force a unity and simplicity that the current state of knowledge does not reveal.

Most important, Marillier says that refusing to admit:

> `l’existence parallèle de plusieurs classes de croyances et de pratiques distinctes, bien qu’analogues entre elles`

forces the scholar to derive one set of usages/conceptions from another by unnatural and laborious hypotheses.

### 005 concordance

This is almost exactly the methodological problem Lovejoy later works through in the sacrifice block. At PDF p.65 / MS p.131, 005 says that several motives may be mingled, that only `delicate analysis` can separate their ritual elements, and that syncretism can combine sacrifices originally distinct in character. At pp.106–111, ceremonial cannibalism, propitiation, magical transfer, funerary provision and communion are repeatedly separated even when their outward ritual forms resemble one another.

The relation is therefore not simply `both discuss sacrifice`. It is the same **anti-single-origin operator applied to sacrifice**.

## 2. `Partially exact` does not mean universally sufficient

In the first 1897 installment Marillier evaluates Frazer’s theory without forcing an all-or-nothing verdict. He writes that even if Frazer’s hypothesis is only:

> `partiellement exacte`

and requires completion by other interpretations, it may still contain a large element of truth. He then specifies that protection of the soul is not necessarily the `unique` motive for initiation/totemic alliance; another motive may operate alongside it.

The same formulation returns more sharply in the second installment when Marillier discusses funerary taboo. Jevons rejects an interpretation because it accounts for only a small part of the larger class. Marillier objects:

> because an interpretation does not suffice by itself to account for an entire group of phenomena, it does not follow that it is not **partially exact**.

Jevons then argues that fear of the dead spirit is `not the true reason` for avoiding the corpse. Marillier corrects the formulation explicitly:

> `il vaudrait mieux dire “n’est pas la seule raison”`

This sentence is probably the cleanest primary-text ancestor yet found for the operation visible throughout 005.

### 005 concordance

Lovejoy does not generally replace one interpretation with one rival interpretation. He repeatedly asks which **combination** of mechanisms can explain a case:

- future-life fate: status, manner of death, ritual, social relation, taboo, moral desert;
- ancestral sacrifice: alimentary, funerary, expiatory, transactional;
- blood rites: communion, incorporation, protection, transferable efficacy;
- cannibalism: famine, revenge, affection, acquisition of qualities, sympathetic magic, propitiation, sacramental union.

The notebook’s procedure is therefore additive-discriminatory rather than binary: an explanation can be locally valid without defining the whole class.

## 3. `External resemblance` does not create a valid comparative category

Marillier’s treatment of taboo makes the rule explicit. Crawley’s contagion theory, he says, can explain part of the evidence very precisely, but cannot by itself explain the heterogeneous set of interdictions grouped under one name merely because of:

> `ressemblances extérieures`

The proper next move is not to discard Crawley’s explanation, but to search for **other motives** where it ceases to work.

Marillier simultaneously criticizes Jevons for approaching the evidence with a `système construit d’avance`.

### 005 concordance

This is directly parallel to 005 PDF p.76, where Lovejoy stresses that a visible practice may be provision for the dead, a protective/magical act, or a later ritual interpretation, and especially pp.106–107, where publicity, repetition, solemnity and ceremonial regularity of cannibalism are explicitly declared insufficient to establish religious or sacramental origin.

Thus the methodological sequence can be rendered:

> **surface morphology → refuse premature genus → identify determinant/mechanism → permit several causal classes → reconstruct later syncretism/reinterpretation.**

## 4. Primary Marillier confirms the corpse/contact contagion mechanism

The same 1897 article now provides the primary text behind the corpse-contagion passage previously known through Van Gennep’s later quotation.

Marillier says that a corpse can transmit its properties by `simple contact`; its essential property is precisely that it is a corpse, so contact can make others die. Purification and preservation rites counter this dangerous influence, and the mortal contagion can propagate from one person to another. The isolation of buriers, mourners and those connected with the dead follows from this mechanism.

This confirms that **contact → transfer of property → propagated effect → prophylactic ritual** belonged directly to Marillier’s published analytical vocabulary before Lovejoy’s Paris year.

### 005 concordance

005 pp.17–18 separates moral fault from ritual interdiction and then moves into clan relation, exclusion, blood/contact and suicide. The same notebook later treats blood and body parts as carriers of efficacy and selected organs as transmitting qualities. The early and late sections therefore share a transmissibility problem, not merely a religious topic.

## 5. Important correction: Marillier uses `mana` directly in 1897

The 1897 primary text explicitly uses the Melanesian term `mana`.

Marillier says that taboos differ in their degree of infrangibility and that their sacred character is, in a sense, proportional to the **mana**, glossed directly as:

> `la force surnaturelle et magique`

of those who imposed them; a person with superior magical power or superior mana may violate a taboo with impunity.

Marillier then refers readers to his earlier *Sur le caractère religieux du tabou mélanésien*.

### Historiographical correction to Batch 38

Batch 38 correctly localized the first `mana` occurrence **within Marillier’s 1900 encyclopedia article `Religion`** to printed p.349, following Nicolas Meylan. It must not be read as the first occurrence of `mana` in Marillier’s career.

The direct 1897 primary evidence proves a pre-1900 use. Meylan further reports an incidental use already in Marillier’s 1892 discussion of Frazer, but that 1892 primary passage has not yet been directly collated and remains a manual-retrieval target.

The revised safe sequence is:

> Codrington 1891 → Marillier’s 1892 Codrington/Frazer engagement (primary mana occurrence still to be checked) → **Marillier 1897 direct primary `mana`** → Lovejoy’s 1898–99 active participation in Marillier’s EPHE programme → 005’s intrinsic/transferable efficacy distinctions → Marillier 1900 `Religion` synthesis → Lovejoy 1906 `Primitive Energetics`.

## 6. Marillier separates taboo/religious sanction from morality

The same 1897 passage is a strong primary control for 005’s moral/ritual distinction.

Marillier rejects Jevons’s suggestion that ritual prohibitions are progressively preserved by being transformed into moral obligations through divine sanction. He argues that this implies a religious origin of morality unsupported by the evidence. His formulation is explicit: religious sentiment, as emotion, has an origin and significance distinct from morality; later moral and social developments transform religious forms, and religion can then reinforce ethical obligations.

He also observes that taboos morally indifferent in themselves, whose original reasons had been forgotten, may survive especially long.

### 005 concordance

This sharpens the already established connection to 005 pp.16–20:

- `moral fault` is not equivalent to violation of taboo/ritual interdiction;
- social/clan exclusion must be distinguished from individual moral desert;
- a moralized ethnographic statement may represent a later historical layer;
- afterlife becomes strictly moral only when reward/punishment is assigned **because of desert**.

The connection is therefore simultaneously conceptual and historical: **ritual sanction can survive, shift rationale and acquire later moral interpretation.**

## 7. Category discipline: keep a term `limited and precise`

Marillier’s critique of Jevons’s totemism contains an especially revealing sentence. An animal may be regarded as the dwelling of a spirit, may possess supernatural power, and may even receive cult; nevertheless it does not follow that it is a totem. Marillier writes:

> `le mot a une signification limitée et précise, il y a tout intérêt à la lui maintenir.`

He then proposes the simpler hypothesis that, alongside totemic cults, other forms — cults of the dead, natural forces/objects, animals/plants, chiefs and sorcerers — have always existed and may have generated public cults through their own histories.

This is not Lovejoy’s later `unit-ideas` doctrine. But it is a very close methodological environment for 005’s resistance to category inflation:

- ritual killing ≠ sacrifice;
- cannibalism ≠ sacramental cannibalism;
- circumcision ≠ partial human sacrifice;
- sacred animal ≠ sacrificed/divine animal;
- fetish ≠ charm/amulet;
- blood use ≠ communion.

After this primary-text pass, the best 005 genealogy is no longer simply:

> Marillier taught comparative religion → Lovejoy studied similar themes.

It is:

> **Marillier’s published 1897 critique explicitly rejects single-origin reduction, distinguishes partial validity from universal sufficiency, refuses categories built from external resemblance, permits parallel but analogous classes, and keeps technical terms narrowly defined**
>

This formulation is stronger than a generic intellectual-influence claim because it specifies a reproducible scholarly operation.

## 9. What this does to the paper argument

A defensible paper-level sentence is now:

> **The Paris connection is visible less as a borrowed doctrine than as a way of handling comparative evidence. In Marillier’s 1897 critique of Jevons, an explanation may be partly right without being the sole explanation, externally similar rites need not share an origin, and several distinct but analogous religious forms may coexist. Lovejoy’s 005 notebook turns that anti-unilateral rule into a working research procedure, repeatedly decomposing broad ethnological categories according to causal mechanism and historical layer.**

This keeps the argument on named people, dated publications, documented seminar participation and manuscript operations, rather than projecting later Lovejoy backwards.
