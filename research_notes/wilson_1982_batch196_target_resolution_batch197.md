## 1. Target 51 resolved: `The Obsolescence of the Eternal` (1909)

### Bibliographic identity

51. Arthur O. Lovejoy, `The Obsolescence of the Eternal`, *Philosophical Review* 18 (1909), 479–502.

### Wilson's annotation

Wilson presents the essay as a confrontation between two major intellectual forces of the preceding half-century:

- the evolutionary conception of the world;
- post-Kantian idealism.

Lovejoy asks whether belief in an eternal ultimate reality can be reconciled with genuine evolution and argues that real qualitative evolution is incompatible with a metaphysics that preserves an eternal concrete totality. Wilson summarizes Lovejoy's conclusion as the obsolescence of the eternal under an evolutionary worldview.

### Formal cross-reference

Wilson gives:

`51 → 5, 21–22, 118`.

This matters because Batch196 already established:

`21 → 5, 22, 51, 435`

`22 → 21, 51, 435`.

Thus the 21/22/51 relation is formally reciprocal:

`21 → 51`

`22 → 51`

`51 → 21–22`.

And 51 itself points directly to *The Great Chain of Being*:

`51 → 5`.

The index independently places 51 under:

- `Eternalism, 21–22, 51, 55`;
- `Temporalism, 21, 34, 50–51, 55, ...`;
- `Evolution, 17, 26, 48, 50–51, 53–54, 57, ...`.

### Consequence

Wilson's early-history-of-ideas genealogy is better represented not as a simple line

`21/22 → Great Chain`,

but as a triangular bridge:

```text
21 Religion and the Time-Process
 ↕
22 Dialectic of Bruno and Spinoza
 ↘   ↙
  51 Obsolescence of the Eternal
   ↓
  5 Great Chain of Being
```

The bridge is conceptually specific: **eternalism ↔ temporality/evolution**. It is therefore a stronger genealogical structure than mere subject resemblance.

## 2. Target 54 resolved: `Kant and Evolution` (1910–1911)

### Bibliographic identity

54. `Kant and Evolution`, *Popular Science Monthly* 77 (1910), 538–53; 78 (1911), 36–51.

### Wilson's annotation

Wilson summarizes Lovejoy's argument that Kant has been retrospectively misclassified as a pioneer of biological evolution. The error arises from extracting expressions from their contexts and disregarding the meanings Kant ordinarily assigns to terms. Lovejoy examines relevant Kantian texts and concludes that Kant never actually professed the transformist hypothesis.

### Relation from entry 26

`26 → 54` is therefore not merely a topical edge labelled `evolution`.

It connects the 1904 history of eighteenth-century evolutionists to a later **contextualist control against false precursor construction**.

This is methodologically important for the repository because it resembles the evidence rules already isolated in Batches194–195:

`lexical resemblance ≠ doctrinal identity`

`historical availability ≠ direct transmission`

`isolated expression ≠ stable authorial doctrine`

`retrospective similarity ≠ legitimate precursor status`.

Wilson's evolution cluster is consequently a history-of-concepts / history-of-claims network, not only a biological-evolution topic list.

## 3. Target 57 resolved: `Buffon and the Problem of Species` (1911)

### Bibliographic identity

57. `Buffon and the Problem of Species`, *Popular Science Monthly* 79 (1911), 464–73, 544–67.

### Wilson's annotation

Wilson describes Lovejoy as confronting six competing interpretations of Buffon's transformism. Lovejoy uses close analysis of Buffon's language and the chronology of his utterances to determine the shifting status of the doctrine. Buffon's historical role is double: he helped make organic evolution thinkable while also impeding its development through his treatment of species.

### Formal cross-reference

Entry 57 ends:

`See 26, 48.`

Since entry 26 already gives:

`26 → 17, 48, 54, 57`,

we now have a formal reciprocal edge:

`26 ↔ 57`.

And a small triangle:

`26 → 48`

`57 → 48`

`26 ↔ 57`.

### Consequence

The evolution network is denser than Batch196's first one-way table suggests. It contains explicit backward and forward bibliographic reuse, and Wilson's annotations repeatedly foreground **language, chronology, precursor claims, and changing doctrinal status**.

That makes this cluster a serious candidate for a second early genealogy of Lovejoy's historical method alongside the 21–22–51 Platonic/temporalist cluster.

## 4. Target 82 resolved: foundational AAUP academic-freedom report (1915)

### Bibliographic identity

82. With Edwin R. A. Seligman and others, `General Report of the Committee on Academic Freedom and Academic Tenure`, *Bulletin of the American Association of University Professors* 1 (December 1915), 15–43.

### Wilson's annotation

Wilson treats the report as foundational for the AAUP. It distinguishes:

- freedom of inquiry and research;
- freedom of teaching;
- freedom of extramural utterance and action;

and addresses the social functions of universities and procedures needed to protect professional independence. Wilson notes that the report was largely written by Lovejoy.

Its own formal line points onward to later academic-freedom materials, including 128.

### Relation from entry 25

Entry 25, `Religious Instruction in Non-Sectarian Colleges and Universities` (1904), gives:

`25 → 82, 127, 128`.

Resolving 82 shows that Wilson reads the 1904 discussion of nonsectarian institutions and scholarly freedom as an antecedent to Lovejoy's later **institutional codification** of academic freedom.

## 5. Target 127 resolved: `Anti-Evolution Laws and the Principle of Religious Neutrality` (1929)

### Bibliographic identity

127. `Anti-Evolution Laws and the Principle of Religious Neutrality`, *School and Society* 29 (1929), 133–38.

### Wilson's annotation

Lovejoy examines laws restricting the teaching of evolution, especially an Arkansas statute, through the principle of religious neutrality. Wilson's summary culminates in Lovejoy's defense of full liberty of inquiry, discussion, and opinion for teachers and students as the only workable neutrality for educational institutions.

### Relation from entry 25

`25 → 127` is therefore highly specific:

`1904 nonsectarian university / religious instruction`

→

`1929 religious neutrality / evolution teaching`.

It is an **applied doctrinal-institutional continuity**, not simply a generic academic-freedom cross-reference.

## 6. Target 128 resolved: `Academic Freedom` (1930)

### Bibliographic identity

128. `Academic Freedom`, *Encyclopedia of the Social Sciences*, vol. 1, ed. Edwin R. A. Seligman (New York: Macmillan, 1930), pp. 384–88.

### Wilson's annotation

Wilson presents this as a mature conceptual statement. Lovejoy defines academic freedom as the freedom of teachers and researchers in higher education to investigate, discuss, and express conclusions without outside interference, subject to professionally competent judgment concerning competence and ethics. The article explains why the scholar's social role requires such independence.

### Relation from entry 25

The three targets of entry25 can now be functionally distinguished:

- `25 → 82` = institutional codification / AAUP foundational report;
- `25 → 127` = applied religious-neutrality controversy;
- `25 → 128` = mature conceptual definition.

Wilson's `See` apparatus is therefore doing more than grouping a topic. It retrospectively turns the 1904 piece into an origin node for a developed institutional corpus.

## 7. Target 139 re-confirmed: `Monboddo and Rousseau` (1933)

### Bibliographic identity

139. `Monboddo and Rousseau`, *Modern Philology* 30 (1933), 275–96.

### Wilson's annotation

Wilson summarizes Lovejoy's claim that Monboddo and Rousseau are poorly described as simple advocates of primitivism. Monboddo instead participates in a historical conception according to which humanity begins in animality and ascends gradually toward rational and social existence; Lovejoy stresses the need for a `new historical science` capable of reconstructing that process.

### Formal cross-reference

139 ends:

`See 17, 116.`

Since 17 ends `See 139`, the relation is explicitly reciprocal:

`17 ↔ 139`.

Thus Wilson formally preserves a nearly four-decade return to an 1895 subject.

The later node also joins the Monboddo line to Lovejoy's Rousseau/primitivism work through 116.

## 8. Target 243 confirmed: `Syllabus: The Philosophy of Buddhism` (1901)

### Bibliographic identity

243. `Syllabus: The Philosophy of Buddhism`, The Washington University Association, 1901.

### Wilson's annotation

The syllabus is for four lectures:

1. `The Place of Buddhism among the Philosophies of India`;
2–3. `Two Essential Formulas of Buddhism`;
4. `Buddhist Ethics and its Goal`.

### Relation to entry 19

Wilson's index gives:

`Buddhism, 19, 243`.

The title index gives the syllabus itself as item 243, and the 1898 Buddhist technical-term article itself remains title-indexed only as item 19.

Therefore the controlled relation remains:

`19 —[W-IDX: Buddhism]— 243`.

It is not presently recoverable as:

`19 → 243 [W-SEE]`

or

`243 → 19 [W-SEE]`.

### Consequence

Wilson preserves an actual two-item Buddhist corpus across 1898 and 1901, but he preserves the connection at the **subject-index layer**, unlike the explicit reciprocal routing used for Monboddo, evolution, temporalism, and academic freedom.

This is a sharper statement than saying entry19 is simply `isolated`:

> Entry19 is **formally isolated at the entry-level `See` layer but not bibliographically isolated at the subject-index layer**.

That distinction should be used consistently from here onward.

## 10. Historiographical result: network density is itself evidence

Batch196's phrase `selective retrospective visibility` can now be made more exact.

Wilson's bibliography does not merely remember some subjects and forget others. It assigns them different **relation architectures**:

- reciprocal `See` edges;
- forward genealogical `See` edges;
- annotation-level anticipation claims;
- introduction-level canonization;
- subject-index co-location;
- title-index reception links.

The early Buddhist material is preserved, but with a comparatively thin architecture:

`technical article + syllabus + subject heading`.

The 21–22–51 line is preserved through repeated explicit genealogy:

`formal reciprocity + Great Chain routing + introduction + later secondary reception`.

The evolution line is preserved through repeated longitudinal reuse and reciprocal cross-reference.

The academic-freedom line is preserved through an explicit 1904→1915→1929/1930 retrospective route.

This difference can be demonstrated without imputing motive.

## 11. Methodological consequence for the project

The target-resolution pass changes one substantive point in how Wilson should be used.

It would be too simple to say:

`Wilson canonizes only 21–22 as the origins of history of ideas`.

A more accurate formulation is:

> Wilson gives 21–22–51 the strongest explicit route into the canonical *Great Chain* genealogy, while simultaneously preserving a separate, unusually dense evolution/precursor-reconstruction genealogy around 17/26/48/54/57/139. The Buddhist 19/243 pair is preserved under a subject heading but not given comparable formal longitudinal routing.

This opens a better comparative question:

**Were there at least two early historical methods in Lovejoy before the mature history-of-ideas canon was retrospectively stabilized: a Platonic/temporalist line and an evolution/precursor line, with the Indic/Buddhist work supplying a third philological-semantic practice that Wilson's formal genealogy leaves comparatively underconnected?**

That is now a testable research question, not yet a conclusion.
