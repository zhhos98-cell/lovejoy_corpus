# Batch 78 — 1906 cross-regional proof is calibrated but asymmetric: grammatical controls, independent-observer convergence, extrapolation, and attribution errors

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced
Scope: continue Batch 77's citation-architecture audit across the North-American, Oceanic, and African packets in Lovejoy, `The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy` (1906), with external bibliographic controls only where authorial attribution or journal metadata require them.

## Core result

Batch 77 established that Lovejoy's source criticism operates below the level of whole documents: a witness can be reliable for an observed custom or lexical occurrence while weak for translation, semantic abstraction, or causal interpretation.

The cross-regional audit now adds an important qualification. That proposition-relative discipline is real, but the 1906 argument does **not** distribute evidentiary resolution evenly across its three major regional fields.

Lovejoy works with at least two distinct grades of evidence:

1. **low-resolution independent convergence** — many observers, including superficial missionaries and travelers, report strikingly similar applications of `wakonda`, `manitou`, `oki`, `mana`, `atua`, `mulungu`, `ngai`, etc.;
2. **high-resolution internal controls** — grammatical morphology, native-language distinctions, and indigenous causal explanations disclose whether the term denotes a person, property, transferable efficacy, or impersonal abstraction.

The second grade is richest in the North-American packet and independently appears in the Yao/Bantu packet. It is not equally available for every Oceanic and African term. Lovejoy nevertheless uses the richer North-American analysis as an analogical key for interpreting less finely described regional vocabularies.

The correct methodological reconstruction is therefore:

> **independent-observer convergence licenses a minimal common semantic core; high-resolution grammatical/native evidence licenses a stronger mechanism claim; the final universal category is produced by triangulation under unequal regional evidence.**

This is a material correction to any overly idealized reading of Batch 76's `vera causa` rule. Lovejoy seeks independent controls, but his three-region proof is **asymmetric**. At the same time, two bibliographic controls show that his otherwise granular source criticism has ordinary scholarly failure modes: a likely journal-volume miscitation for Hetherwick, and an apparent misattribution of a sentence from Sir Charles Eliot's introduction to A. C. Hollis.

The best resulting historiographic formulation is:

> **Lovejoy's method is structurally critical but locally uneven: he calibrates claims more finely than sources, yet his cross-regional synthesis can outrun the calibration he demands within a local source packet.**

## 1. W. Jones 1905 closes the pending Batch 77 item: stronger proposition, more diagnostic evidence

Batch 77 left the continuation of W. Jones, `The Algonkin Manitou`, pending after Lovejoy introduced it at p.370. The next page closes the question decisively.

Lovejoy has just said that seventeenth-century missionary evidence proves only the **concrete application** of `oki` and `manitou` to persons or things possessing unusual powers. It does not yet prove an abstract, pervasive property or force. He then introduces Jones's recent specialist study precisely at that evidentiary boundary.

At printed p.371, Jones supplies evidence of a different logical type:

- `manitou` is primarily an **impersonal substantive**;
- in Sac, Fox, and Kickapoo, grammatical gender distinguishes living from non-living entities;
- when `manitou` denotes a virtue, property, or abstraction, the form used is the **inanimate** gender;
- when the property becomes identified with natural objects, the gender distinction becomes confused;
- the property is treated as omnipresent and manifested throughout nature;
- selected ritual explanations describe its **transfer** into persons: eating the heart of a brave enemy absorbs manitou; in the sweat-lodge, cutting the skin opens passages through which manitou released from the heated stone enters the body in steam.

This is not merely a newer observer disagreeing with older missionaries. Jones brings **more diagnostic evidence for a stronger proposition**.

The proof ladder is now explicit:

`early missionary lexical occurrence`

→ licenses **concrete application / unusual efficacy**

but not yet

→ **abstract pervasive property**.

Then:

`grammatical morphology + specialist field study + native causal explanation`

→ licenses **impersonal abstraction + transfer mechanism**.

### Batch 77 correction

The W. Jones row in `Lovejoy1906_source_function_admissibility_matrix_batch77.csv` should be read as superseded only on its pending status. The semantic continuation is now directly controlled.

## 2. p.372: Lovejoy explicitly rejects a folklorist-inference basis for the North-American mechanism

The North-American section closes with an unusually important methodological sentence.

Lovejoy says that totemism, personal medicine/fetish, agriculture, women's taboos, sweat-lodge observances, and beliefs about blood and the heart can be connected to the one underlying energy conception **not merely by the inferences of comparative folklorists but through Indian language itself**.

That distinction is exactly what Batch 76's `vera causa` formulation requires.

He also corrects Dorsey's wording. Dorsey reports that buffalo-hunt rules, heart/tongue consecration, sacred-pole anointing and corn-planting customs were regarded as laws received from Wakanda. Lovejoy says Dorsey has given an **erroneous personification** to `wakonda`, while retaining the informant-based association between those customs and the `wakonda` idea.

Thus a single citation is decomposed:

- described custom: RETAIN;
- native association of custom with `wakonda`: RETAIN;
- observer's personification of `wakonda`: REJECT.

This is an especially clean instance of Batch 77's rule:

> **source → statement type → competence → proposition licensed.**

## 3. Codrington is a `best authority` and still contains adverse evidence

At the transition to Melanesia and Polynesia, Lovejoy introduces the evidence as coming from the **best authorities** and begins with R. H. Codrington, *The Melanesians* (1891), pp.118–119.

Codrington's account strongly supports several features that Lovejoy needs:

- `mana` is a power/influence;
- it is not ordinary physical power;
- it manifests as unusual force, excellence, or efficacy;
- it is not fixed in one object;
- it can be conveyed through water, stone, bone, and other media;
- spirits possess and can impart it.

But the same primary quotation also complicates Lovejoy's later abstraction. Codrington calls mana `in a way supernatural` and says that it **essentially belongs to personal beings to originate it**, even though it can act through impersonal media.

That is not a trivial detail. It means the Oceanic authority that most strongly supports transferability also preserves a relation to personal/spiritual agency that does not fit perfectly with Lovejoy's strong impersonalization.

Lovejoy does not, in this passage, directly refute Codrington's personal-origin clause. The tension should remain visible.

### Later reweighting

By p.380, in direct criticism of Marett, Lovejoy says that `wakonda`, `manitou`, and `mana` are not primarily names for the `supernormal` or astonishing but for **the efficacious, the powerful, the productive**. This effectively reweights the Codrington packet: efficacy/transferability becomes diagnostically central, while `supernatural` is denied the status of semantic essence.

The correct historical claim is therefore not `Codrington already said exactly what Lovejoy says`. It is:

> **Lovejoy extracts one causal-semantic axis from an internally mixed authoritative source and makes that axis carry the comparative abstraction.**

This is proposition-relative use of a `best authority`, not simple deference to it.

## 4. Hetherwick supplies a second independent grammatical control

The Bantu section at pp.374–375 is stronger than a generic parallel with North America.

Lovejoy says that until quite recently there had been no good evidence for a comparable Bantu conception and that such a conception can easily escape missionaries and ethnographers. He then cites Alexander Hetherwick's long experience at Blantyre concerning the Yao term `mulungu`.

The decisive evidence is grammatical:

- missionaries had adopted `mulungu` as the word for `God`;
- in native form, however, the word **does not belong to the personal noun class**;
- its morphology instead denotes a **property inhering in something**;
- the `untaught Yao` refuses to assign to it an idea of being or personality and treats it more as a quality/faculty extended across the spirit world.

This is structurally independent of Jones and yet remarkably close in evidentiary design.

### Jones

`inanimate grammatical gender`

→ supports **property / abstraction rather than personal being**.

### Hetherwick

`non-personal noun class`

→ supports **inhering property rather than personal being**.

This is a major upgrade. Lovejoy's cross-regional case is not built only from English glosses such as `power` or from superficial resemblance in ritual usage. In at least two unrelated linguistic fields, he uses **grammatical morphology** as an independent control on ontology.

The stronger formulation is:

> **Lovejoy's linguistic proof is morphological as well as lexical.**

And because the Algonkin and Yao evidence are independently structured, their convergence is substantially more probative than mere repetition of the word `power` by translators.

## 5. Hetherwick bibliography: Lovejoy's 1906 volume number appears wrong

The 1906 footnote, as extracted from the printed page, cites Hetherwick in the *Journal of the Anthropological Institute* as `Vol. 33, p.93`.

External bibliographic control securely identifies the article as:

Alexander Hetherwick, `Some Animistic Beliefs among the Yaos of British Central Africa`, *Journal of the Anthropological Institute of Great Britain and Ireland* **32** (1902), pp.89–95, DOI 10.2307/2842905.

JSTOR's volume-32 contents independently place the paper at pp.89–95.

Until a second original copy proves the extraction wrong, the safest verdict is:

> **the 1906 paper appears to contain a volume-number miscitation (`33` for `32`), while the page locus is in the correct neighborhood.**

This matters as a negative control. Lovejoy's source architecture can be conceptually granular without being bibliographically infallible.

## 6. The Masai packet: adverse evidence is retained, but authorial attribution slips

At p.375 Lovejoy first quotes Joseph Thomson, *Through Masailand* (1885), p.445, for the wide application of `ngai`: Thomson himself, his lamp, and anything strange or incomprehensible could be connected with `ngai`.

Lovejoy then turns to a more recent book and gives adverse evidence. The source says that prayers for children, rain, and victory are addressed to `En-ga'i` as a **distinct personality** who is prayed to and hears. But the same discussion adds that sun, moon, morning/evening star, mountain clouds, and in one song a medicine-man can also receive the same term. The passage concludes that this may represent a still undeveloped religious condition in which divine personality is not clearly separated from striking natural phenomena.

Lovejoy attributes that conclusion to **`Mr. Hollis`**.

Bibliographic control complicates this attribution.

A. C. Hollis's 1905 book is *The Masai: Their Language and Folklore*, explicitly published **with an introduction by Sir Charles Eliot**. The relevant p.xix passage sits in that introduction and itself speaks about `Mr. Hollis's collection` in the third person. The natural authorial reading is therefore that the interpretive conclusion belongs to **Eliot's introduction**, not to Hollis himself.

Safe correction:

> **Lovejoy's page/book locus is essentially right, but `Mr. Hollis suggests` appears to collapse the ethnographic collector and the author of the introduction.**

This is an important historiographic check. Proposition-relative source criticism should not be romanticized into perfect bibliographic source control.

## 7. p.376 makes observer-resolution itself an explicit evidentiary variable

Lovejoy explains why he has quoted his authorities so fully and often in their own words. The near-verbal agreement of **many independent observers** dealing with three wholly distinct races is, he says, significant.

But he immediately differentiates the resolution of those observers. Several missed the finer ramifications and less obvious meanings of the idea. Nevertheless, nearly all converge on a minimal set of features:

- the major religio-magical term denotes something more impersonal than personal;
- it fluctuates among a communicable property, mobile invisible substance, and transferable energy;
- it is widely diffused among persons and natural objects;
- unusual power/efficacy is treated as evidence of its presence.

Then Lovejoy marks a stronger evidentiary tier: **in the case of the American Indians the evidence shows us still more**. There, language and customs together show localization, blood, sexuality, fertility, and transferable productive efficacy in greater detail.

Thus p.376 itself establishes the two-tier architecture:

### Tier A — independent low-resolution convergence

Many observers across distinct regions agree on a minimal semantic pattern.

### Tier B — high-resolution local evidence

More discriminating linguistic and ritual evidence reveals richer causal structure in a subset of the corpus.

The universal category is therefore not made from equally fine evidence in every region.

## 8. p.377 reveals the asymmetry directly: North-American resolution becomes an analogical key

The strongest correction comes immediately before the `vera causa` passage.

Lovejoy notes that superficial observers use `manitou` and `wakonda` in the same peculiar way that other superficial observers report for `mana`, `atua`, `ngai`, and related terms. From this he says it is **not improbable** that the richer meanings and associations revealed by more accurate North-American observation also belong to the corresponding Melanesian, Polynesian, and Bantu words.

This is methodologically consequential.

The inference is:

`surface usage parallel across regions`

+

`deep semantic/mechanism analysis in North America`

→ **probabilistic extension of the deep analysis to less richly documented regional analogues**.

Only after this move does Lovejoy state that he will be dealing with a `vera causa` and that linguistic and custom evidence reciprocally corroborate one another.

Therefore Batch 76's `independent evidence` claim needs an explicit regional qualification:

> **Lovejoy independently establishes that relevant native categories exist, but the richer semantic content assigned to the cross-cultural category is not established at identical resolution in every region.**

The North-American packet carries disproportionate inferential weight.

This is best called **triangulation under asymmetric evidence**.

## 9. The asymmetry does not erase the Yao grammatical control

The correction above should not be overstated in the opposite direction.

Lovejoy is not simply projecting North-American semantics onto Africa. Hetherwick independently supplies a strong Bantu grammatical argument for a non-personal property. Likewise Codrington provides genuine Oceanic evidence for transferability and distributed efficacy.

The asymmetry is about **resolution and completeness**, not absence of independent regional evidence.

A better model is:

- North America: dense packet — old attestations + specialist semantics + grammatical morphology + native causal explanations + ritual applications;
- Oceania: strong authoritative lexical/semantic packet, but with unresolved personal/supernatural tensions;
- Bantu/Yao: strong grammatical property evidence from Hetherwick;
- Masai: mixed evidence, including explicit personality claims and broad impersonal/natural applications;
- cross-regional synthesis: independent low-resolution convergence plus uneven high-resolution controls.

This is a more historically faithful account of the 1906 proof than either `three independent demonstrations` or `mere analogy`.

## 10. Relation back to notebook 005

The new result sharpens the 005→1906 developmental reconstruction.

005 repeatedly asks:

- who reported a belief;
- after what contact history;
- whether an outward rite actually belongs to the proposed causal class;
- whether chronology independently warrants the proposed development;
- whether apparent analogy reflects borrowing or local mechanism.

1906 preserves these concerns but makes the evidence architecture more explicit:

- source statements are decomposed by proposition;
- native grammar is used to check missionary translation;
- specialist field evidence is recruited where old observations reach a proof ceiling;
- cross-regional agreement is graded by observer resolution;
- a richer local mechanism is probabilistically extended across analogous vocabularies.

Thus the development is not simply `005 pluralism → 1906 one theory`.

It is:

> **005 decomposes ambiguous morphology; 1906 reconstructs a cross-cutting category through a hierarchy of evidence whose local strengths and weaknesses remain visible.**

## 11. Historiographic consequence: structural criticism without methodological idealization

The strongest current paper-level sentence is:

> **Lovejoy's 1906 comparative method was structurally source-critical without being uniformly strict. He distinguished external observation from translation, lexical occurrence from semantic abstraction, and superficial cross-regional resemblance from higher-resolution grammatical or indigenous explanation. Jones's Algonkin gender morphology and Hetherwick's Yao noun classes supplied independent controls on impersonality. Yet the final universalization was asymmetrical: Lovejoy explicitly used the richer North-American semantics as a probabilistic key to less richly resolved Oceanic and African terms, and his apparatus contains ordinary bibliographic slips, including a likely miscitation of Hetherwick's journal volume and an apparent attribution to Hollis of prose belonging to Sir Charles Eliot's introduction.**

The broader interpretive payoff is important:

> **Lovejoy calibrates local claims more carefully than his final comparative synthesis.**

This gives the article a productive tension rather than a progress narrative.

## 12. Evidence status

### Direct primary

- Lovejoy p.370 explicitly limits early `oki/manitou` evidence to concrete applications and introduces Jones at that proof ceiling.
- p.371 gives Jones's grammatical-gender evidence and native causal explanations of transfer.
- p.372 corrects Dorsey's personification while retaining custom/informant evidence.
- pp.372–373 quote Codrington as major/best Oceanic authority and preserve both transferability and the personal-origin/supernatural clauses.
- pp.374–375 quote Hetherwick's Yao noun-class/property evidence.
- p.375 prints the Masai/En-ga'i discussion.
- p.376 explicitly contrasts independent-observer agreement with missed finer ramifications, and says American evidence shows `still more`.
- p.377 explicitly infers that richer North-American meanings probably belong to corresponding regional terms, then gives the `vera causa` rule.
- p.380 explicitly prioritizes `efficacious / powerful / productive` over `supernormal` as the semantic core.

### External bibliographic control

- Hetherwick's paper is in *JAI* 32 (1902), pp.89–95, not volume 33.
- Hollis's *The Masai* (1905) explicitly has an introduction by Sir Charles Eliot; the p.xix interpretive passage occurs inside that introduction and refers to Hollis in the third person.

### Strong reconstruction

- `triangulation under asymmetric evidence` is our analytic label for the p.376–377 proof structure.
- `grammatical control` is a direct description of the evidence type, but the cross-regional methodological significance is our reconstruction.
- `structurally critical but locally uneven` is our historiographic synthesis, not Lovejoy's terminology.

### Do not claim

- that Lovejoy had equally independent semantic proof in all three regions;
- that Codrington straightforwardly anticipated Lovejoy's impersonal theory;
- that all African terms are merely extrapolated from North America;
- that the Hollis/Eliot error invalidates the underlying Masai evidence;
- that Lovejoy's `vera causa` rule meets modern ethnographic standards simply because it seeks independent evidence.
