# Batch 70 — AOS exit window, 1899–1903: institutional affiliation fades while Buddhism teaching continues

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced
Scope: push the AOS institutional chronology beyond 1899, test what disappearance from the corporate-member roll can and cannot mean, and compare that institutional trajectory with Lovejoy’s documented Buddhist/Indic practice after the 1898 JAOS article.

## Core result

This sweep yields a hard terminus but, more importantly, a methodological correction.

The revised January 1903 membership list of the American Oriental Society no longer contains Arthur O. Lovejoy. The corporate list is alphabetical; in the relevant L-section it passes from Jacob W. Loch to Percival Lowell without Lovejoy. Since the December 1899 member roll still listed `ARTHUR ONCKEN LOVEJOY, 1689 Cambridge St., Cambridge, Mass. 1897`, the currently defensible institutional exit window is:

> **after the 1899 roll and before the revised January 1903 roll.**

The exact year of removal is not yet closed because the 1900 and 1901 membership rolls have not yet been independently recovered in searchable form.

At the same time, Daniel J. Wilson’s primary-source bibliography records a 1901 Washington University Association syllabus, `The Philosophy of Buddhism`, organized as four lectures. Thus the AOS membership chronology and the Buddhist-practice chronology demonstrably diverge: Lovejoy’s AOS affiliation was fading or had ended by the opening years of the twentieth century while he was still teaching Buddhism in a dedicated format.

The Society’s own constitutional language makes this separation historiographically mandatory. Its membership notice explicitly states that one need not be a `professed Orientalist` to become a member. Corporate membership therefore records institutional affiliation and support, not a stable professional identity. Conversely, disappearance from the roll can establish loss of affiliation but cannot by itself establish loss of Orientalist/Indic competence.

The revised model is therefore:

> **AOS affiliation is bounded and probably contracts around 1900–02; Buddhist/Indic practice continues across teaching, comparative religion, and philosophy. Institutional exit and intellectual exit are not the same chronology.**

## 1. Hard primary terminus: Lovejoy is absent from the January 1903 AOS roll

Primary object:

*Journal of the American Oriental Society* 23, second half. The volume contains the Society’s membership list headed:

`LIST OF MEMBERS.`

`REVISED, JANUARY, 1903.`

The explanatory note specifies that the number after an address is the year of election. The corporate-member list then proceeds alphabetically.

In the relevant L-section the sequence includes:

- Charles Rockwell Lanman
- Berthold Laufer
- Henry C. Lea
- C. S. Leavenworth
- Caspar Levias
- Robert Lilley
- Thomas B. Lindsay
- Charles E. Little
- Enno Littmann
- Jacob W. Loch
- **Percival Lowell**
- Benjamin Smith Lyman
- David Gordon Lyon

There is no Lovejoy between Loch and Lowell, where an alphabetically ordered entry would necessarily fall. A full-volume search for `Lovejoy` also returns no match.

This is substantially stronger than an isolated OCR failure. The alphabetic neighborhood itself supplies the negative control.

Safe claim:

> **By the revised January 1903 AOS membership list, Lovejoy was no longer listed as a corporate member.**

The last secure positive member-list evidence currently remains December 1899. Therefore the exact exit interval is:

> **1899 < AOS corporate-membership exit < January 1903.**

Do not convert this into a precise resignation date without the 1900/1901 rolls or a Directors/Treasurer record.

---

## 2. The Society’s bylaws provide a possible administrative mechanism, not a cause

The same volume prints the Society’s constitutional/bylaw material. Corporate members owed an annual assessment of five dollars. It further provides that if a corporate member failed for two years to pay the assessment, the Directors could drop the person’s name from the membership list.

This is useful because it shows that disappearance from the roll need not signal an intellectual, disciplinary, or ideological break. Membership could terminate through routine administration.

But there is presently no Lovejoy-specific evidence that this is what happened.

Searches of the available 1903 volume text for resignation/withdrawal language have not produced a notice naming Lovejoy, and no Treasurer/Directors entry has yet been found saying that he was dropped for arrears.

Therefore:

**Established:** the Society had an administrative mechanism by which two years’ non-payment could lead to removal.

**Not established:** Lovejoy was removed for non-payment.

**Not established:** Lovejoy formally resigned.

The cause of disappearance remains open.

---

## 3. AOS membership is not a proxy for professional Orientalist identity — by the Society’s own definition

The same AOS material contains a section `CONCERNING MEMBERSHIP` which is unusually valuable for the historiography of this project. It states explicitly:

> `It is not necessary for any one to be a professed Orientalist in order to become a member of the Society.`

The notice invites persons sympathetic to the Society’s objects and willing to aid its work, whether through assessments, gifts, or scientific contributions. It also points to the Society’s section for the Historical Study of Religions.

This gives a primary-source methodological control on how the membership evidence should be used.

### What membership can prove

- formal affiliation with the American Oriental Society;
- election into its corporate body;
- eligibility for the institutional life and publications of the Society;
- in Lovejoy’s 1897 case, conjunction with an actual Buddhist paper on the annual-meeting program.

### What membership alone cannot prove

- that the member was professionally employed as an Orientalist;
- that `Orientalist` was a stable self-identity;
- sustained annual-meeting activity;
- continuous specialist publication.

### What disappearance from membership cannot prove

- intellectual abandonment of Asian/Indic subjects;
- disappearance of philological or philosophical competence;
- a disciplinary conversion from `Orientalism` to `philosophy` at a single date.

This primary statement from the AOS is one of the strongest reasons to keep the project’s preferred wording at the level of **practice, competence, genre, and institutional location**, rather than identity.

## 4. 1901 Buddhism teaching: a decisive asynchronous marker

Daniel J. Wilson’s *Arthur O. Lovejoy: An Annotated Bibliography* records as item 243:

`“Syllabus: The Philosophy of Buddhism.” The Washington University Association, 1901.`

Wilson describes a four-lecture structure:

1. `The Place of Buddhism among the Philosophies of India`
2. `Two Essential Formulas of Buddhism`
3. `Two Essential Formulas of Buddhism`
4. `Buddhist Ethics and its Goal`

This is important for chronology because it falls directly inside the AOS exit window.

The evidentiary status should remain exact:

- **bibliographically secure through Wilson’s primary-source bibliography**;
- **the underlying 1901 syllabus artifact has not yet been independently recovered in this web sweep**.

So the current claim should be:

> **Wilson records Lovejoy teaching a dedicated four-lecture course on the philosophy of Buddhism in 1901.**

Do not yet quote the syllabus beyond Wilson’s bibliographic description as though the primary artifact had been inspected.

Even at that controlled level, the chronological consequence is substantial:

> **AOS institutional contraction and substantive Buddhist activity overlap.**

If the precise membership exit turns out to be 1900, 1901, or 1902, the point remains: institutional membership is not a reliable proxy for intellectual continuation.

## 5. The trajectories now decouple

| date | AOS institutional status | Buddhist / Indic practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1897 | elected corporate member; Buddhist paper no. 30 on program, read by title | specialist Buddhist technical-term paper in meeting form |
| 1898 | corporate member | eleven-page technical Buddhological article in *JAOS* |
| 1899 | corporate member; no recorded Cambridge meeting attendance/paper | no new Oriental specialist publication located in this AOS volume |
| 1900–02 | exact exit date unresolved | institutional status must not be inferred from subject practice |
| 1901 | somewhere inside current AOS exit window | Wilson records four-lecture `The Philosophy of Buddhism` syllabus |
| Jan. 1903 | absent from revised corporate roll | absence from AOS is only institutional evidence |
| 1907 | no AOS affiliation presently evidenced | Deussen review shows current Indic-philosophical field judgment and a program for treating Indian systems as technical philosophy |

Adding the already established 1902 `Religion and the Time-Process`, which explicitly reuses the result of Lovejoy’s 1898 Buddhist analysis, makes the asynchronous pattern still clearer.

The transition is not well described as `Orientalism ended, philosophy began`.

A better chronology is:

> **specialist institutional location contracts first; Buddhist/Indic materials and competencies continue to travel into teaching, comparative religion, and philosophical criticism.**

## 6. 1901 AOS field ecology remains active, even as Lovejoy’s own institutional status becomes uncertain

*JAOS* 22 (1901) shows that the AOS field itself remained intensely engaged with Indic and Buddhist materials. The volume contains, among other work:

- Edward Washburn Hopkins, `Yoga-Technique in the Great Epic`, p. 333;
- Edward Washburn Hopkins, `Notes on the Çvetāçvatara, the Buddhacarita, etc.`, p. 380;
- the New York AOS proceedings of April 11–13, 1901, beginning p. 391.

The volume’s searchable vocabulary also makes visible the continuing Sāṃkhya/Sāṃkhya-Yoga/Vedānta/Śaṅkara/Deussen problem-field.

This should be used only as **field ecology**. It does not establish that Lovejoy attended the 1901 meeting, read Hopkins’s papers, or remained a corporate member in that year.

Its value is comparative: while the AOS continued to host the specialist field in which Lovejoy had entered in 1897–98, his own documented Buddhist work had already begun to appear in another institutional form, the Washington University Association syllabus.

---

## 7. The 1898 Buddhist paper acquires a later specialist afterlife, but early reception remains open

A quick reception sweep adds a different kind of continuity. Later bibliographic and specialist literature continues to classify/cite Lovejoy’s 1898 `The Buddhistic technical terms upādāna and upādisesa` inside technical Buddhist debates.

Examples located in this sweep include:

- a *Bibliography of Indian Philosophies* sequence that classifies the paper under an Abhidharma/Theravāda-related section, placing Lovejoy among specialist items between nineteenth- and twentieth-century Buddhist scholarship;
- later Buddhist scholarship that explicitly revisits Lovejoy’s interpretation of `upādi` and treats his argument as substantive;
- modern bibliographies that continue to list the 1898 paper alongside later scholarship on the same technical problem.

This establishes **long specialist afterlife / bibliographic persistence**.

It does **not** yet establish contemporary reception in the years immediately after 1898. The targeted c.1898–1915 citation graph remains an open task.

This distinction matters because later disciplinary memory can preserve a paper even if its author left the institutional field that produced it.

---

## 8. Karttunen’s `Apparently never returned to Buddhist studies` can now be decomposed

Klaus Karttunen’s biographical notice says that Lovejoy `Apparently never returned to Buddhist studies.`

That sentence can still be retained under a very narrow interpretation:

> no second dedicated specialist Buddhist philological article comparable to the 1898 *JAOS* paper has yet been located.

It becomes misleading if expanded into a broader proposition that Lovejoy ceased working with Buddhism or Indian philosophy after 1898. The evidence now includes:

- Wilson’s 1901 dedicated Buddhism syllabus;
- the 1902 explicit reuse of the 1898 Buddhist analysis;
- the 1907 Vedānta review’s current and discriminating specialist judgment.

So the historiographical correction should be about **genre and mode**, not a simple contradiction of Karttunen:

> **Lovejoy did not return to the same genre of specialist Buddhological publication, but Buddhist/Indic practice continued in other genres.**

---

## 9. Revised historiographical wording after Batch 70

The project’s preferred sentence remains strong:

> **not continued professional Buddhology, but continued Orientalist practice across changing genres.**

Batch 70 allows a second sentence that is more institutionally exact:

> **The membership chronology and the practice chronology diverge: Lovejoy’s formal AOS affiliation ended sometime after the 1899 roll and before January 1903, while Buddhist and Indic competence continued to be redeployed in teaching, comparative religion, and philosophy.**

A more compressed conceptual version is:

> **AOS affiliation fades before Indic competence does.**

And the key methodological safeguard can now be grounded in the AOS’s own self-description:

> **Membership is evidence of affiliation, not identity; non-membership is evidence of institutional non-affiliation, not intellectual cessation.**

This is preferable to `Lovejoy remained an Orientalist`, because it permits identity, institutional location, and publication venue to change while tracking concrete scholarly operations.

## Bottom line

> **The most important result is not simply that Lovejoy disappears from the AOS roll by January 1903. It is that the corporate-member chronology and the Buddhist/Indic practice chronology can no longer be made to stand in for one another. The AOS itself denied that membership required one to be a `professed Orientalist`; Wilson records Lovejoy teaching Buddhism in 1901 inside the current institutional exit window; and later evidence shows Indic-philosophical judgment surviving well beyond it. The transition is therefore best reconstructed as institutional and generic relocation, not a single moment of intellectual abandonment.**
