The bibliography appended to Arthur O. Lovejoy’s *Essays in the History of Ideas* gives him an unlikely beginning. Its first entry is an 1898 article on two Buddhist technical terms—not a precursor to *The Great Chain of Being*, not a statement of the unit-idea program, but a piece of specialist Buddhist scholarship. The impression is false: Lovejoy had already published an essay on Lord Monboddo in 1895. Yet the false beginning is useful. Before we have learned much about the young Lovejoy, a later bibliography has already arranged his career for us.

Nor was this Buddhist episode simply forgotten. Daniel J. Wilson documented Lovejoy’s comparative-religion training, his Sanskrit and Buddhist studies, and his later work in Paris; Simon Schaffer could still note in 2010 that Lovejoy studied Pāli and Sanskrit there. The biographical fact survived. What survived less clearly was the work inside the fact. Among Lovejoy’s papers at Johns Hopkins is a Harvard Cooperative Society notebook labeled in his hand “Sankhya + Buddhism.” It does not preserve a lost version of his mature method, still less a Buddhist origin for the history of ideas. What it does preserve is more concrete: repeated disputes over what particular evidence could establish about a term, a sequence, a cause, a borrowing, or a historical connection.

One of the clearest begins with Henry Clarke Warren. Warren had warned that the Buddhist formula of dependent origination should not be treated as a simple “chain of causation”: its links expressed different kinds of dependence, and each had to be examined separately. Yet when he reached the relation between *taṇhā* (desire) and *upādāna* (attachment), he treated it as one of identity. Lovejoy copied the judgment and then interrupted it: “But not so if we are to follow the present text” (“Sankhya + Buddhism,” MS p. 75). He turned to Buddhist classifications of the two terms, comparing four kinds of attachment with six forms of desire. There is no invention here of a general principle; Warren had already supplied the problem. Lovejoy’s disagreement is narrower. He refuses one answer and asks whether the texts in front of him can really bear it.

With Émile Senart, the scale expands from two terms to the historical composition of a Buddhist formula. Senart had argued that the twelve *nidānas* contained heterogeneous categories assembled from different sources; repetition and mixture could therefore be evidence of a secondary construction. Lovejoy’s notebook concedes much of the premise. The terms need not all be originally Buddhist, the series is not a homogeneous temporal sequence, and materials may have been borrowed. But he refuses the further inference that the resulting arrangement has thereby been shown to be accidental or unintelligible. Hermann Oldenberg had already challenged important parts of Senart’s semantic case, so Lovejoy’s significance is not priority. The narrower point is that evidence about where the parts came from did not, by itself, settle what the parts did once combined.

That distinction survives in Lovejoy’s 1898 article, “The Buddhistic Technical Terms Upadana and Upadisesa.” There he names Senart, tests the textual witnesses Senart had cited, and says that the terms of the formula, “perhaps even the ideas,” may be largely borrowed while their “arrangement and application” remain original and characteristic (127, 132). But reading article and notebook together also shows how much was left out. The notebook ranges across Sāṃkhya-Buddhist chronology, psychological classifications, textual criticism, etymology, causation, and competing genealogies. The article is much narrower. Publication did not simply polish the notebook; it reduced the size of some of its claims.

Across the manuscript, the same materials are repeatedly reopened under different questions. Lovejoy asks whether two terms are identical in use, what causal work a term performs, what comes before what in time, what belongs inside what as a classification, where a doctrine or term came from, and what a borrowed element does once placed in a larger arrangement. An answer to one of these questions does not automatically answer the others. One page makes this especially visible. On manuscript p. 123, Lovejoy places *viññāṇa* within the *nāma* side of a classification while writing beside it that “Viññāṇa is temporally (?) an antecedent of nāmarūpa, and logically a subdivision of it.” Forced onto one axis, these statements can look contradictory. On the page they answer different questions. The same term can occupy one position in a classification and another in a sequence. The force of the page lies partly in its modesty: this is not a methodological manifesto but a working sheet on which one relation is not allowed to answer for another.

The treatment of *upādisesa* shows most clearly what happened when such working problems entered print. In the notebook Lovejoy moves among competing etymologies, textual passages, and stronger possibilities about the term’s relation to death and Nirvāṇa. The published article explicitly limits what etymology can establish: the meaning of *upādi*, he writes, must be settled “rather by an examination of its use than by etymological arguments” (134). He retains contrary passages, calls some evidence “relevant but not necessarily conclusive,” and ends by saying that the matter “cannot be regarded as finally settled” (136). The published paper is not simply a cleaned version of the notes. It is an argument with a smaller evidentiary burden.

Only at this point does Harvard help explain why one student was moving so easily between close Buddhist textual work and much broader comparison. The 1896–97 course announcements placed Charles Rockwell Lanman’s Sanskrit and Pāli teaching, including advanced work on the *Dhammapada*, *Sutta Nipāta*, and *Majjhima Nikāya*, beside Crawford H. Toy’s comparative study of Hebrew and other Semitic religions and Charles Carroll Everett’s comparative study of religions, including Buddhism and Hindu philosophies. Wilson’s archival reconstruction establishes Lovejoy’s study with these figures, although the catalogue alone cannot prove enrollment in a particular Pāli course. Harvard did not transmit one unified “method.” It placed close textual study, comparison among related traditions, and broad comparative religion within the same graduate environment.

A second Lovejoy notebook changes the evidence. In notebook 005, with a strong Paris 1898–99 core, the objects are no longer primarily Pāli terms but death, ritual, afterlife, sacrifice, and social practice. A differentiated fate after death cannot simply be called moral reward or punishment because it looks like one: the notebook tests other possible explanations, including manner of death, ritual provision, social status, kinship or group relation, taboo, and moral conduct. Léon Marillier’s comparative-religion work and Nathan Söderblom’s research in the same seminar environment show that many of these distinctions were already available in the field. Again, there is no clean originality story. What the notebook lets us see is the problem of inference in another material register: what does a visible difference actually warrant us to say caused it?

Lovejoy himself carried a related distinction beyond the Buddhist case. In “Religion and the Time-Process” (1902), he sent readers back to his earlier Buddhist article while discussing wider comparisons. The affinity between Aristotle’s theology and Vedānta, he wrote, was “obvious”; the theory that Aristotle therefore came under eastern influence was nevertheless “entirely gratuitous,” because the internal development of the Platonic dialectic supplied an account of the Greek position (446–47). Resemblance did not have to become genealogy. This is not evidence that a Buddhist “method” had migrated intact. It is evidence that Lovejoy could still distinguish comparison from a claim of historical transmission.

In 1906 the object changes more sharply. Writing on “Democracy in the Twentieth Century,” Lovejoy described the conjunction of social-evolutionary stages and racial or national difference as having split Enlightenment “generic Man” “in two directions—longitudinally and cross-wise” (94). The two axes were not equivalent. Institutions fitted to one stage of culture were, he said, certainly unfitted to an earlier one; racial or national difference might make institutions inappropriate elsewhere, but he explicitly refused to say that they “must” (94–95). He also warned that race had become an overworked category. Yet these distinctions had political consequences. In the essay’s closing pages they entered his discussion of colonial policy and political franchise; on p. 102 he wrote of regretting the previous generation’s “hasty” enfranchisement of Black Americans and of sympathizing with attempts to disfranchise illiterate Black voters, while withholding a final judgment on those specific cases (101–2). Historical differentiation here was not a politically neutral preference for finer distinctions. A more differentiated description of humanity could become part of an argument about who was fitted for what institutions and under what conditions.

The rest of 1906 blocks any comfortable developmental story. In an essay on Kant, Lovejoy tested a broad inherited contrast between dogmatism and criticism against local propositions and rejected the larger classification when it failed. Reviewing Paul Elmer More, he returned to the *Sutta Nipāta*—the same Buddhist text prominent in his 1898 argument—to resist an over-positive account of Nirvāṇa. Yet in “The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy” he moved in the opposite direction, gathering heterogeneous local reports into a broad comparative claim about “primitive philosophy.” In the same year Lovejoy could break down an over-broad historical category and construct broad categories of his own. Decomposition and generalization were contemporaneous possibilities, not successive stages in a tidy intellectual maturation.

By the time Lovejoy published *The Great Chain of Being*, Asian materials had not disappeared, but they occupied a different place. The preface explicitly defines the book’s principal historical object through “Occidental philosophy” and persistent presuppositions of “Western thought.” Buddhism and Vedānta remain visible in its account of otherworldliness (30), and “primitive Buddhism” recurs as a comparative form (97). At p. 35 Lovejoy calls Plato the principal source of the “indigenous” Occidental strain of otherworldliness while distinguishing it from “imported Oriental varieties.” The narrative center is therefore Western without the Asian material simply vanishing. In the notebooks of the 1890s, Buddhist and Indian materials had themselves been close textual and historical problems; in *Great Chain*, they more often serve as comparisons within a predominantly Occidental genealogy. That change can be described without turning the mature book into either a betrayal of the notebooks or their hidden endpoint.

Returning from the mature book to the notebooks therefore does not reveal a forgotten Buddhist origin of the history of ideas. Wilson had already preserved the early training, the 1898 article, and Paris; Schaffer could preserve the Pāli and Sanskrit fact while telling a different story about Lovejoy through evolution, hierarchy, seriality, and *Great Chain*. The formation was known but differently classified. What the notebooks alter is its evidentiary status: they let us reconstruct the scholarly work performed inside a biographical episode that later histories could mention without making it analytically central.

The odd first item in the *Essays* bibliography thus looks different at the end. A scholarly career is arranged more than once. Notes arrange materials into working problems; publication narrows some of those problems into claims an author is prepared to defend; mature books rearrange earlier materials around new narrative centers; later bibliographies and disciplines decide which of those arrangements become easiest to see. The question raised by Lovejoy’s Buddhist notebooks is therefore not simply whether he once studied Buddhism. It is what kind of historical work he did when he did so—and what later histories of a discipline made of that work once other versions of Lovejoy became canonical.
