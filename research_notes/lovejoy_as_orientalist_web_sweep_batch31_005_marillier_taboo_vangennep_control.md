## 1. Van Gennep 1904 is explicitly a Marillier-lineage document

Arnold van Gennep, *Tabou et totémisme à Madagascar* (Paris: Ernest Leroux, 1904; Bibliothèque de l’École des Hautes Études, Sciences religieuses, vol. 17), is dedicated:

> `A LA MÉMOIRE DE LÉON MARILLIER`

In the preface Van Gennep says he is using recent results in `sociologie religieuse`, testing theories against Malagasy facts, and stresses that basic work of **collecting and studying documents** is still required before Malagasy taboo can be properly situated in its institutional setting. He thanks Marcel Mauss for references and for checking proofs.

## 3. A direct Marillier quotation: a corpse transmits its property by simple contact

The most important new precursor occurs at Van Gennep printed pp.76–77. Discussing funerary taboo, Van Gennep says Jevons’s contagion theory was accepted `avec quelques restrictions` by Marillier and then quotes Marillier directly.

Secure conceptual content of the quotation:

- the corpse can **transmit its properties by simple contact** like other objects;
- its defining property is precisely being a corpse, hence it can make those who touch it become dead / cause their death;
- magical purification and preservation rites counter the dangerous influence;
- the lethal contagion can propagate from one person to another;
- this accounts for isolation of buriers and, by extension, mourners/relatives and those in contact with the dead.

Van Gennep’s citation: `Revue de l’Histoire des Religions`, t. XXXVI (1897), pp.337–358.

This is much stronger than a generic statement that Marillier was interested in taboo. It gives a concrete **contact → transfer of property → propagated effect → prophylactic rite** mechanism already in Marillier’s published field before Lovejoy’s 1906 paper.

## 4. Van Gennep 1904 develops the same mechanism with `hasina`

At printed pp.16–18 Van Gennep distinguishes two linked notions in Malagasy taboo:

1. `tohina`: contact / contagion / impurity acquired by contact;
2. `hasina`: an invisible power inherent in things and beings.

His dictionary-based definition is particularly useful for comparison with 005:

> `vertu intrinsèque ou surnaturelle qui rend une chose bonne et efficace`

and `masina` is glossed as holy / sanctified / powerful / **efficient**.

Van Gennep explicitly compares this property with Melanesian `mana`, calls the chief’s `hasina` contagious, and explains prohibitions as mechanisms protecting people from a quantity of power they cannot assimilate while also conserving the chief’s power intact.

This is a post-005 control, not evidence of borrowing. But it shows how closely the vocabulary of **intrinsic efficacy, transfer/contact, contagion, and quasi-physical conservation** belonged to the Marillier-associated research field that 005 records.

## 5. Van Gennep rejects unilateral explanations in exactly the form useful for the 005 paper

At printed p.76 Van Gennep explicitly refuses to apply contagion to every funeral rite. He says unilateral explanations are worthless in sociology because human groups carry long evolutions whose elements overlap; among the Malagasy one finds **not one religious system but several systems intermingled**.

This is an unusually good independent control for the core operation reconstructed from 005:

- do not classify a rite by surface form alone;
- do not infer one universal origin;
- decompose the same ritual complex into mechanisms with different histories;
- allow animist, contagious, social, juridical, and moral readings to coexist at different layers.

Again, direction of influence is not asserted. The point is methodological family resemblance inside the same immediate French comparative-religion environment.

## 6. Custom and taboo sanctions: religious crime can simultaneously be social

Van Gennep’s chapter on `Édiction et sanction du tabou malgache`, printed pp.28–31, is highly relevant to 005’s earlier future-life / justice notes.

Using Sakalava `lilin-draza`, he describes inherited unwritten custom as regulating familial, social, national, and religious acts. Violation can expose not only the violator but relatives to consequences. He then makes the analytical point that custom is religious in principle and therefore its sanction is religious; yet because the offender’s natural or adoptive kin are also liable, the religious crime is **at the same time social**, and the sanction is social as well.

This supplies an excellent external control for the 005 distinction between:

- individual moral desert;
- ritual/taboo violation;
- collective or clan liability;
- religious exclusion / social consequences;
- causal contamination through contact.

It also helps explain why Lovejoy’s notebook does not move directly from `bad act` to `moral punishment`: he is repeatedly asking which **norm or mechanism** actually produces the consequence.

## 10. Paper-level payoff

This batch reinforces two claims already emerging from 005.

### A. `Mechanism before morality`

The notebook’s method is not to ask only whether a society rewards good and punishes bad. It asks **what process produces the consequence**: physical/natural fact, ritual taboo, contagious property, kin status, social exclusion, religious sanction, or strictly moral desert.

### B. `Classification is itself historical criticism`

The same visible act can have several inherited rationales. Sorting mechanisms is therefore also sorting historical layers. This gives 005 a stronger connection to the Marillier / Van Gennep refusal of unilateral origin stories.

This is useful for the paper because it keeps the argument at the level of concrete scholarly operations rather than projecting Lovejoy’s later `unit-ideas` doctrine backward.
