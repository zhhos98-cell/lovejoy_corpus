# Batch 21 — Elizabeth Laetitia Moon Conard: an American future-life evidence node around Marillier, 1897–1901

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced

## Research move

Marillier’s official 1898–99 report says that the Monday survivance conference added **new facts drawn especially from the two Americas** while refuting Steinmetz. A contemporary American research project now deserves much closer attention: Elizabeth Laetitia Moon (later Conard), who studied in Paris in 1897–98, completed a University of Chicago doctorate in comparative religion in 1899 on Algonquian ideas of future life, and had that work translated into French by **Léon Marillier**.

The evidence does not yet establish that Moon attended Marillier’s conference or that her dissertation supplied the `new facts` used in Lovejoy’s 1898–99 seminar. But the chronological, institutional and thematic fit is too close to treat as incidental. Her work is best regarded at present as a **parallel American evidence node in Marillier’s immediate research/editorial orbit** and a high-priority test for reconstructing the North-American source flow behind notebook 005.

## 1. Secure chronology and institutional location

A contemporary biographical source, *Woman’s Who’s Who of America* (1914–15), records Moon/Conard’s education as:

- Smith College A.B. 1894;
- A.M. 1897;
- University of Chicago Ph.D. 1899;
- **École des Hautes Études / Sorbonne, Paris, 1897–98**.

Grinnell College’s institutional history independently says that immediately after her Smith master’s degree she went to Paris and during that year began work on the University of Chicago comparative-religion dissertation examining ideas of God and future life among Algonquian peoples.

This puts her in Paris immediately before Lovejoy’s documented 1898–99 Marillier year and while Marillier’s survivance programme was already active.

## 2. Marillier’s role in the dissertation/publication is direct

BnF and Persée authority records identify the 1899 dissertation:

> Elizabeth Laetitia Moon, *Ideas of the future life held by Algonkin Indians*, University of Chicago dissertation, 1899.

The bibliographic record explicitly states **`Traduit par L. Marillier`**. Persée gives an Angers, A. Burdin, 1899 printing; the expanded/public French publication appears as:

> E. Laetitia Moon Conard, *Les Idées des Indiens algonquins relatives à la vie d’outre-tombe*, translated by Léon Marillier, Paris: Ernest Leroux, 1901, 99 pp.

The work was also published in two instalments in *Revue de l’histoire des religions* 42 (1900): pp.9–49 and pp.220–274. JSTOR indexes both pieces under **E. Lætitia Moon Conard and L. Marillier**.

Thus Marillier’s connection is not a later bibliographic association. He personally translated and helped bring the work into the French comparative-religion publication field.

## 3. The subject sits exactly inside the 1898–99 seminar problem

Marillier’s Monday conference asked whether differentiated post-mortem conditions among non-European peoples were originally moral/retributive or instead continuations of earthly life shaped by status, rite, manner of death and other non-moral determinants. It explicitly added new evidence from the Americas.

Moon/Conard’s study is an extended documentary analysis of **Algonquian future-life beliefs**, using missionary relations, travel accounts, ethnographic works and linguistic evidence. The Google Books witness exposes a source architecture rich in:

- Jesuit/missionary sources;
- Schoolcraft and other nineteenth-century ethnography;
- Cheyenne and other North-American peoples;
- funeral practices and offerings;
- bodily/corporeal conceptions of soul;
- hunting and ordinary activity continued after death;
- differentiated access to the land of the dead;
- explicit concern with Christian elements and contact.

This is almost exactly the North-American evidentiary terrain that 005 pp.16–30 enters while testing moral versus non-moral eschatology.

## 4. Concrete thematic/source parallels with 005

### A. Earthly life continued after death

Conard’s early pages interpret grave food/tools and related practices as arising from the spontaneous assumption that the dead remain like the living, rather than from an elaborate theory of post-mortem justice. Later passages describe souls hunting animal souls and using the `souls` of snowshoes and other objects as the living use material things.

Lovejoy 005 repeatedly treats future life as intensified or continued terrestrial existence and uses continued occupations, bodily needs, status and property as evidence against reading the afterlife as moral recompense.

### B. Corporeal soul

Conard reproduces early missionary material in which the human soul is imagined as a dark image/shadow possessing feet, hands, mouth, head and the other bodily parts.

005 pp.20–30 likewise contains sustained notes on physical/corporeal continuity of the soul and on the limited/secondary character of more abstract soul concepts.

### C. `Paradise` titles based on prowess/status rather than moral virtue

A particularly striking passage, citing Charlevoix, says that being a **good hunter, brave in war, successful in undertakings, and having killed many enemies** are the titles that give access to paradise.

This is precisely the conceptual test Lovejoy performs in 005: superior future status generated by prowess, rank or worldly qualification cannot automatically be redescribed as moral reward. The similarity is conceptual and evidentiary; exact shared source dependence remains to be tested page by page.

### D. Christian/contact criticism

The Google Books indexing of Conard’s work shows repeated concern with `éléments chrétiens`, missions and contact, while the study is built heavily from early missionary relations whose evidentiary status must be evaluated.

005 pp.16–20 makes missionary contamination an explicit methodological problem, and Marillier’s own 1899–1900 report later names **Christian interpolations in American eschatological myths** as a conference subject.

This creates a tight methodological triangle: Conard’s American source corpus, Marillier’s seminar/refutation programme, and Lovejoy’s source-critical notebook.

## 5. The publication ecology is itself revealing

The 1900 *Revue de l’histoire des religions* volume places Conard/Marillier’s two-part Algonquian study inside the same editorial environment in which Marillier was reviewing new ethnographies and American folklore collections. In the second half of the volume, Marillier reviews `Navaho Legends` and Franz Boas/James Teit material on Thompson River Indians, alongside other recent ethnography.

This matters because `new facts from the two Americas` were entering Marillier’s programme through a live editorial/review pipeline, not only through old canonical books. Moon/Conard belongs to that pipeline as both an American researcher and a translated RHR contributor.

## 6. What this does — and does not — add to the 005 argument

### Secure

- Moon/Conard studied in Paris at the École des Hautes Études/Sorbonne in 1897–98.
- Her Chicago Ph.D. was on Algonquian future-life beliefs.
- Marillier translated the dissertation.
- The French study appeared in RHR 42 (1900) and as a 1901 Leroux book.
- Its subject/source field strongly overlaps Marillier’s American survivance problem and 005’s North-American evidence.

### High-value hypothesis

- Moon/Conard’s research may be one component of the American source flow behind Marillier’s statement that the 1898–99 seminar added new facts from the two Americas.
- Her work can function as a **third documentary control** on the same problem-field even if she turns out not to have been a formal Marillier student.

### Still unproved

- formal enrolment in Marillier’s conference;
- direct personal research supervision by Marillier in 1897–98;
- transfer of her dissertation notes into Marillier’s 1898–99 seminar before publication;
- direct use of Conard by Lovejoy.

Accordingly she should not yet be called a `student of Marillier` or a demonstrated source for 005.

## 7. Research payoff if the link tightens

If correspondence, enrollment records or source-order comparison establishes a direct Marillier–Moon research exchange before the 1898–99 seminar, the context becomes unusually rich:

> **American doctoral research on Algonquian future life → Marillier translation/editorial processing → EPHE controversy over American evidence → Lovejoy’s active seminar notebook**

That would allow us to reconstruct comparative religion as a **circulation of dossiers, translations and cases across Chicago, Paris and individual student notebooks**, rather than as transmission of a stable doctrine from European professor to American student.

Even without that final link, Conard already proves that Marillier’s immediate circle was actively processing exactly the kind of American future-life evidence that his official report says was newly mobilized in 1898–99.
