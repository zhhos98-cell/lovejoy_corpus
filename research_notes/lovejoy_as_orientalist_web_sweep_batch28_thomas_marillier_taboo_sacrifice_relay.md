# Batch 28 — N. W. Thomas as a Marillier control: taboo, sacrifice, and the afterlife of the French seminar

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced

## Research move

This batch continues the unfinished **French / 005 context** while keeping the main object **Lovejoy as Orientalist**. The new move is to use Northcote Whitridge Thomas as an unusually strong independent control. Thomas is the same former Marillier student who described the EPHE seminar mechanics in 1896; fifteen years later he authored the *Encyclopædia Britannica* entries on **Taboo**, **Sacrifice**, **Magic**, **Cannibalism**, **Animism**, and **Fetishism**. His 1911 syntheses explicitly cite Marillier and preserve mechanism-sensitive distinctions that map closely onto Lovejoy notebook 005.

This is not evidence that Lovejoy read Thomas's 1911 articles. It is evidence that two different students/participants emerging from the Marillier environment carried forward a distinctive way of separating ritual, magical, social and moral mechanisms.

## 1. N. W. Thomas's 1896 eyewitness description of Marillier's seminar

Thomas's primary article, `The École Pratique des hautes Études`, *Folk-Lore* 7 (1896), pp.200–201, describes the EPHE principle as **work by pupils directed by the lecturer**. In Marillier's course a pupil might write an essay on a defined topic, read it in class, receive Marillier's comments, and then have the point discussed by other students.

Thomas also identifies Marillier's then-current themes as flood legends and marriage rites. This remains direct evidence that Marillier's teaching worked through research assignments, documents, criticism and discussion rather than lecture dictation alone.

## 2. The Britannica author initials `N. W. T.` are Northcote Whitridge Thomas

Wikisource's author page and contribution lists identify **N. W. Thomas** as Northcote Whitridge Thomas and attribute to him the 1911 Britannica articles:

- `Magic`;
- `Sacrifice`;
- `Taboo`;
- `Cannibalism`;
- `Animism`;
- `Fetishism`;
- and related anthropological entries.

This matters because the same eyewitness who described Marillier's research-seminar practice later synthesized precisely the conceptual domains that dominate 005.

## 3. Thomas 1911 `Taboo`: sanctions separated by mechanism

The Britannica `Taboo` article gives a classification highly relevant to 005.

Thomas defines taboo as prohibition enforced by **religious or magical sanctions**, then distinguishes among different mechanisms rather than treating all prohibitions as moral law.

### Automatic / natural sanction

Some sanctions operate automatically, without a personal will intervening. Thomas compares this to kinds of magic that work without a spirit or personal agent.

### Animistic sanction

Other sanctions are explained through the wrath of a god, dead person or spirit.

### Social sanction

A community may punish a violator because the breach is believed to expose the whole group to supernatural danger. Thus collective punishment can arise from ritual danger or solidarity even where individual moral guilt is not the original causal category.

### Mana / transmissible danger

Thomas treats sacred or dangerous power as transmissible by contact; people and things may become charged with power. The strength/status of the person involved changes the danger relation.

### Taboo and moral obligation

A later section explicitly titled **`Taboo and Moral Obligation`** asks how prohibitions can become customs and moral obligations after their original sanctions have been forgotten or transformed. Thomas links the historical transformation of taboo/custom to the emergence of apparently categorical obligation.

This is very close to the analytical problem in 005 pp.14–20: Lovejoy repeatedly asks whether apparent punishment results from taboo, ritual qualification, clan solidarity, social exclusion or genuinely moral desert.

## 4. Thomas's `Taboo` bibliography points back into the exact French source field

The Britannica article's bibliography cites:

- **Léon Marillier**, `Sur le caractère religieux du tabou mélanésien`, *Bibliothèque de l'École des Hautes Études, Sciences religieuses*, vol. VII, pp.35–74;
- Marcel Mauss's work in the *Revue de l'histoire des religions*;
- Hubert and Mauss;
- other contemporary French comparative-religion literature.

This independently confirms that Marillier's 1896 taboo essay remained a recognized source for the mechanism-sensitive analysis of taboo and sanction.

## 5. Thomas 1911 `Sacrifice`: Marillier is an explicit theoretical authority

The `Sacrifice` article is even more valuable for 005 pp.46–119.

Thomas explicitly cites **L. Marillier, *Revue de l'histoire des religions*, vol. XXXVI, p.243** in his discussion of Robertson Smith's communion/totem theory. He summarizes Marillier's objection: if the bond of kinship between worshippers and victim is not independently established and is inferred merely from the common meal, the common meal itself cannot prove that the victim was a totem or original kinsman.

Thomas then notes the closely related Hubert–Mauss criticism: historical/logical connection between the alleged stages cannot simply be assumed, and simple forms need not be chronologically prior.

This is exactly the kind of evidentiary refusal that appears in 005: outward similarity or simplicity does not establish historical origin.

## 6. Thomas explicitly summarizes Marillier's theory of sacrifice as originally magical

Most importantly, Thomas states that for **L. Marillier**, sacrifice was originally essentially a **magical rite**: the effusion of the victim's blood releases a magical force intended to bend or constrain divine powers to human purposes. The gift theory then arises in connection with the cult of the dead.

Thomas also says Marillier retained a separate communion/alliance theory but did not fully clarify its relation to the preceding magical theory.

This relay maps very closely to Lovejoy 005:

- PDF p.64: magical sacrifice can act **directly on forces of nature** and is described in machine-like terms;
- pp.84–90: blood and body parts carry transferable power/qualities;
- pp.88–90: intrinsic efficacy is distinguished from an indwelling spirit;
- pp.98–105: blood/life power can be transferred fecundatively to crops;
- p.105: one mechanism is `quasi-mechanical and magical rather than sacramental`;
- p.117: sacramental union is treated as comparatively exceptional;
- the notebook repeatedly separates funerary provision/gift, propitiation/expiation, magic and communion rather than forcing them into one origin theory.

The result is stronger than generic thematic overlap. A former Marillier student, writing independently in 1911, identifies **Marillier himself** with a magical/blood-force theory and a critique of Robertson Smith that closely matches the classificatory pressure of 005.

## 7. Marillier had a separate `Tabou` article in *La Grande Encyclopédie*

A further French bibliographic target has now emerged.

Pascal Le Maléfan's Marillier bibliography separately lists:

> **`Tabou`, article de la *Grande Encyclopédie*.**

Arnold Van Gennep's primary 1904 *Tabou et totémisme à Madagascar* — dedicated **`A LA MÉMOIRE DE LÉON MARILLIER`** — independently cites:

> `L. Marillier, article Tabou dans la Grande Encyclopédie.`

This proves that the encyclopedia `Tabou` article is a real Marillier work and a recognized source among his students.

## 8. The encyclopedia `Tabou` article is in volume XXX

*La Grande Encyclopédie* volume XXX covers **Sigillateur–Thermopole**, so `Tabou` necessarily falls in this volume. Wikimedia/Wikisource preserve the public-domain scan:

- file: `Grande Encyclopédie XXX.djvu`;
- Internet Archive source: `lagrandeencyclop30dref`;
- approximately 1,250 scans.

Wikisource scan 839 begins the letter `T`, putting `Tabou` close to that position. The exact printed page range and article text remain to be captured directly.

This is distinct from Marillier's 1896 `Sur le caractère religieux du tabou mélanésien`.

## 9. Relationship to the mysterious 1901 `Notes sur la coutume, le tabou et l'obligation morale` remains unresolved

The project now has three distinct Marillier taboo objects:

1. 1896 — `Sur le caractère religieux du tabou mélanésien`, pp.35–74;
2. encyclopedia article — `Tabou`, *La Grande Encyclopédie*, volume XXX;
3. 1901 — `Notes sur la coutume, le tabou et l'obligation morale`, Félix Alcan, Sudoc `086800310`.

The thematic and chronological overlap is obvious, but there is currently **no bibliographic proof** that item 3 is an offprint, revision or expanded version of item 2. They must remain separate until title pages, pagination or catalogue notes establish the relation.

## 10. Van Gennep gives a second post-Marillier student trajectory

Van Gennep's 1904 *Tabou et totémisme à Madagascar* is part of the EPHE Sciences religieuses series and is dedicated to Marillier. Its early bibliography directly cites Marillier's encyclopedia `Tabou` article alongside Frazer, Crawley and Jevons.

The book's architecture — including a chapter on **`Édiction et sanction du tabou malgache`** and separate treatments of sick persons, the dead, chiefs, family/property/place and animals/plants — shows another Marillier-trained student reorganizing the taboo problem after Marillier's death.

Together with Söderblom, Thomas and Lovejoy, this makes the Marillier environment increasingly visible as a **generative analytical field**, not a single teacher-to-one-student influence line.

## 11. Consequence for Lovejoy as Orientalist

For the main Lovejoy argument the important point is not that he later repeated Thomas or Van Gennep. It is that notebook 005 was produced inside a French comparative-religion environment in which several students independently learned to:

- distinguish moral from ritual/social/magical sanction;
- classify sacrifice by causal mechanism rather than outward form;
- resist simple evolutionary sequences;
- treat blood/power/contact as potentially direct or quasi-mechanical efficacy;
- separate spirit-mediated explanation from impersonal or intrinsic power;
- ask whether later moral categories transform older ritual obligations.

Lovejoy's 1906 `Primitive Philosophy` then isolates one of these operations — impersonal, transferable, quasi-mechanical efficacy — and turns it into a general causal category.
