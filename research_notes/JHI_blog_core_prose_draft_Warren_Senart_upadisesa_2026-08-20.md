## Draft

A few pages into Arthur Lovejoy’s notebook on “Sankhya + Buddhism,” a small disagreement with Henry Clarke Warren becomes unusually revealing. Warren had warned that the Buddhist formula of dependent origination should not be treated as a simple chain of causes. The Pāli term *paccaya*, he argued, marked different kinds of dependence in different links: antecedent condition, cause and effect, interdependence, even identity. Yet when he reached the relation between *taṇhā* (desire) and *upādāna* (attachment), Warren classified it as one of “identity.” Lovejoy copied the judgment and then broke from it: “But not so if we are to follow the present text.” He turned instead to the canonical and commentarial classifications of the two terms, comparing four kinds of attachment with six forms of desire. The point was not that Warren’s general method was wrong. It was that the relation Warren himself had assigned to this pair did not survive a closer test.

That small correction captures a recurrent problem in the notebook. What kind of relation has the evidence actually established? Similarity is not necessarily identity; adjacency in a causal sequence is not necessarily one uniform kind of causation; a concept may precede another temporally while falling within it logically. Lovejoy did not invent this discipline of relational discrimination. Warren himself had insisted that the links in dependent origination had to be examined one by one. What the notebook preserves is Lovejoy using that shared comparative repertoire against the judgments of the authorities he was reading.

The same problem appears at a larger scale in Lovejoy’s engagement with Émile Senart. Senart’s 1896 essay on the twelve *nidānas* argued that the formula contained scholastic categories drawn from different sources and fused into what he called an “ordre plus ou moins accidentel.” His treatment of *upādāna* was central to the case: if the term was merely a reduction of *upādānaskandhas*, then the skandhas effectively appeared twice in the series. Repetition and heterogeneity became evidence for a secondary and composite construction.

Lovejoy’s notebook translates Senart’s conclusion almost literally: the categories had been “placed together in an order more or less accidental.” But then the voice changes. “On Senart’s account of the matter there is simply this to be said,” Lovejoy writes. He concedes much of Senart’s premise. The terms of the formula need not be original to Buddhism; the sequence mixes objective and subjective elements; its relations are strange to a Western reader; it does not form a homogeneous temporal series; repetitions do occur. Yet he refuses the inference that follows. It is “entirely false,” he writes, to conclude that the formula is therefore illogical or unintelligible, or that its terms have merely been assembled in an accidental order.

This is a sharper distinction than a simple disagreement over whether Buddhism borrowed from other Indian traditions. Lovejoy could grant borrowing and compositeness. What he would not grant was that evidence about provenance automatically settled the character of the resulting configuration. In his 1898 article, the same dispute becomes public and more tightly controlled. Senart’s interpretation, Lovejoy argues, makes the formula derivative, composite, and “practically meaningless”; the textual passages Senart cites do not, in Lovejoy’s view, prove the interpretation attributed to them. The article then states the larger principle with unusual clarity: the terms of the Buddhist formula, “perhaps even the ideas,” may be largely borrowed, while their “arrangement and application” remain original and characteristic.

The move from notebook to article is therefore not a simple condensation of content. The notebook ranges widely across the chronology of Sāṃkhya and Buddhism, the composition of philosophical systems, psychological taxonomies, textual witnesses, and competing genealogies. The published article retains a narrower set of propositions that can be supported by local textual, semantic, and causal controls. The manuscript preserves the branching proof problems; publication selects those that can bear a more explicit burden of demonstration.

The treatment of *upādisesa* makes that change especially visible. In the notebook Lovejoy already argues that *anupādisesa* cannot simply mean physical death or the extinction of the skandhas: for a person freed from desire, whether one dies or lives is morally indifferent, and to make death itself the *summum bonum* would be profoundly un-Buddhistic. Much of this passage moves almost verbatim into the 1898 article. But print adds something the notebook states less explicitly: a scale of confidence. Lovejoy concedes that more than one etymology is possible; the meaning of *upādi*, he says, must therefore be settled “rather by an examination of its use than by etymological arguments.” The evidence he adds is “relevant but not necessarily conclusive.” The oldest and probably most numerous passages support one reading, but contrary texts remain. “The matter,” he concludes, “cannot be regarded as finally settled.”

Across these cases, the notebook does not disclose a hidden theory of intellectual history waiting to be named. It shows something more modest and historically more useful: a scholar working inside a transregional comparative field in which resemblance, identity, function, provenance, configuration, and genealogy were not interchangeable claims. Warren supplied one relation and Lovejoy reassigned it. Senart established compositeness and Lovejoy denied that compositeness alone explained the system. A lexical problem survived into print, but publication made the limits of the evidence more explicit. The recurring question was not simply whether two terms, doctrines, or traditions were related. It was what kind of relation the evidence was entitled to establish.
