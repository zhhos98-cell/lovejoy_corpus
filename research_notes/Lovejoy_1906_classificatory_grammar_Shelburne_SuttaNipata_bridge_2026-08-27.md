# Lovejoy 1906 — classificatory grammar, *Shelburne Essays*, and the Sutta Nipāta bridge

Date: 2026-08-27  
Status: **CANONICAL / FINAL TARGETED GRAMMAR AUDIT CLOSED**

## Executive result

The final targeted audit does **not** support a strong single-source claim such as `Tylor → Lovejoy method`. It supports a more discriminating three-level result:

1. **field-grammar precedent:** E. B. Tylor already combined developmental scale, `primitive philosophy`, and a methodological warning against premature whole-object synthesis;
2. **actor-explicit political genealogy:** in `Democracy in the Twentieth Century` Lovejoy himself traces staged political relativity through Lessing/Herder and especially John Stuart Mill, not through Spencer;
3. **actor-level source continuity:** Lovejoy's April 1906 review of Paul Elmer More directly reuses the *Sutta Nipāta* as a textual control on Nirvāṇa — the same canonical source to which his 1898 JAOS paper had explicitly turned for the original signification of Buddhist technical terms.

The third result is the strongest new JHI-facing bridge. It shows that early Buddhist source practice was still active in Lovejoy's published work in 1906, while the scale and function of comparison had changed.

This audit therefore closes the textual/conceptual web branch. The only remaining argument-sensitive return is MD-006, the original 004/005 notebook page images, which affects material-form claims rather than the textual spine.

## 1. Tylor: divided study and developmental totalization already coexist

Primary control: Edward B. Tylor, *Primitive Culture*, especially vol. I, ch. II and the animism chapters. Public text controls:

- Project Gutenberg, vol. I: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/70458/70458-h/70458-h.htm
- Wikisource, ch. II: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Primitive_Culture/Chapter_2

Tylor's classificatory program explicitly distinguishes **industrial, intellectual, political, and moral stages of culture**. He also warns that these dimensions do not advance in equal steps and that a single combined measure of civilization is methodologically crude.

Most importantly, Tylor says that much labor has been wasted in premature attempts to treat as a whole what is, at the present state of inquiry, only susceptible of **divided study**. Yet he immediately retains a developmental scale from savagery through barbarism to civilization.

His book also repeatedly uses `primitive philosophy` as an aggregate cognitive address: savage intellectual life forms `schemes of primitive philosophy`; animism is treated as a theoretical conception of primitive philosophy.

### Consequence

`decomposition / divided study ≠ anti-totalization` is **not** a Lovejoy invention. It is available within the comparative-anthropological field before Lovejoy's 1906 paper.

This materially tightens the project's delta claim. Lovejoy's documentary novelty should be located in **selection, reweighting and proof-resolution**, not in inventing either analytical decomposition or evolutionary reaggregation.

### Firewall

Do **not** write that Tylor directly caused Lovejoy's 1906 political or methodological operations merely because the grammar is parallel. The direct evidence is asymmetrical:

- `Primitive Philosophy` explicitly opens against Tylor among other anthropomorphic/animist theorists;
- `Democracy` does not name Tylor as the source of its stage/race model.

The safe formulation is **field-grammar precedent plus direct argumentative engagement in the anthropology paper**.

## 2. Lovejoy 1906: substantive rejection, classificatory redeployment

Direct primary control remains `The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy`, *The Monist* 16.3 (July 1906), 357–382, already controlled in the repo.

The paper opens against Brinton, Guyau, Tylor and the Spencer/Grant Allen line. Lovejoy accepts anthropomorphism/animism as real but rejects making personal/spiritual agency the foundational category of early religion. He proposes instead a more fundamental category of **impersonal, diffusive, transferable, quasi-mechanical efficacy**.

The paper nevertheless preserves an aggregate address structurally familiar from the field:

`local linguistic / ritual evidence`
→ `recurrent causal mechanism`
→ `Primitive Energetics`
→ `fundamental concept of primitive philosophy`
→ `savage thought / broad populations`.

This is best described as:

> **substantive rejection of a master explanation + redeployment of an inherited classificatory grammar around a different causal category.**

This sentence is project analysis, not Lovejoy's actor-declared account of his relation to Tylor.

The already-canonical `lovejoy_1906_primitive_predicate_topology_reaggregation_batch210.md` remains the detailed control for the upward movement of the predicate `primitive`.

## 3. `Democracy`: the political stage genealogy is explicit and is not Spencerian in this text

The returned HathiTrust volume gives the full direct body of Arthur O. Lovejoy, `Democracy in the Twentieth Century`, *Bulletin of the Washington University Association* 4 (1906), 81–102.

Source:

- HathiTrust handle: https://hdl.handle.net/2027/hvd.hnav7c
- user-return text: `hvd-hnav7c-1787789071.txt`

Lovejoy says that the idea of society evolving through `a sequence of inevitable and consecutive stages` had been familiar since **Lessing and Herder**. He then says the political consequence was clearly realized by **John Stuart Mill as early as 1830**, and quotes Mill's autobiographical formulation:

- human progress has an order in which some things precede others;
- political institutions are relative, not absolute;
- different stages of human progress ought to have different institutions;
- political philosophy presupposes a philosophy of human progress/history.

Mill's *Autobiography*, ch. V, independently confirms the quotation and identifies the broader intellectual inputs as the nineteenth-century Continental reaction against eighteenth-century thought — Coleridge/Coleridgians, Goethe, Carlyle, and French thought.

Public primary control:
https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Autobiography_(Mill)/Chapter_V

A full-text search of Lovejoy's 1906 `Democracy` recovered no `Spencer`. Therefore:

> **do not insert Spencer into Lovejoy's actor-explicit political genealogy.**

Spencer remains relevant to other late-nineteenth-century evolutionary contexts and to the anthropology paper's opening target field, but that is a different evidentiary edge.

The race/nationality axis is also not reducible to the stage axis. Lovejoy says nineteenth-century thought split generic Man in two directions:

- longitudinally: stages of social evolution/culture;
- cross-wise: racial/national genius.

The developmental stage constraint is stated more strongly than the race constraint. Institutions suitable to a later stage are said to be certainly inappropriate to an earlier one; institutions suited to one racial/national genius **may** be inappropriate to another, and Lovejoy explicitly refuses `must` at that point. He also says the race category has been overworked.

The final position is nevertheless politically consequential and hierarchical because Lovejoy applies the model to colonial policy, women's suffrage and Black enfranchisement, while retaining `general welfare` / concrete consequences / the `standard of civilization` as a higher-order evaluative criterion.

## 4. NEW: Lovejoy's 1906 *Shelburne Essays* review closes a Wilson bibliography gap

Direct primary newly recovered from the same returned Bulletin volume:

Arthur O. Lovejoy (`A. O. L.`), review of Paul Elmer More, *The Shelburne Essays*, First, Second, and Third Series, *Bulletin of the Washington University Association* 4 (1906), 151–155.

Daniel J. Wilson's annotated bibliography listed this item as no. 174 but explicitly recorded:

> `Unable to obtain copy.`

The returned HathiTrust volume therefore supplies a primary text not incorporated into Wilson's annotation.

### 4.1 Actor-explicit methodological vocabulary

Lovejoy evaluates More by several operations directly relevant to the present project. He says More shows less than some critics of:

- `painstaking patience in the induction of evidence`;
- `psychological analysis of temperament`;
- inquiry into `historical conditions and antecedents`.

He then praises More for:

- `wide-ranging comparisons`;
- correlating manifestations of thought/style across literatures;
- comparison among French, Italian, Greek and `Oriental` materials;
- cultivating a mental habit of comparison that sees beyond the immediate object.

This is actor-explicit 1906 vocabulary for a comparative operation:

`induction / local historical antecedents`
→ `comparison`
→ `correlation across heterogeneous literatures`.

### Evidentiary ceiling

This is strong evidence for Lovejoy's published methodological preferences in 1906. It is **not** evidence that he had already formulated a mature general method of history of ideas, and it should not be projected backward as a conscious 1898 methodological manifesto.

## 5. NEW: 1898 → 1906 direct *Sutta Nipāta* source continuity

This is the strongest new bridge.

### 1898

In `The Buddhistic Technical Terms upādāna and upādisesa`, JAOS 19 (1898), Lovejoy explicitly says:

> for light upon the original signification of Buddhist terms, one naturally turns first to the *Sutta Nipāta*.

He then uses the *Sutta Nipāta* repeatedly in adjudicating the meaning of `upādisesa` and its relation to Nirvāṇa.

Repo primary:
`The Buddhistic technical terms upadana and upadisesa..html`

Lovejoy identifies the edition as Fausbøll in the 1898 apparatus.

### 1906

In the newly recovered More review, Lovejoy again turns to Buddhist textual evidence to police a claim about Nirvāṇa. More, he says, unsuccessfully tries to read a concrete positive worth/meaning into the Buddhist goal; Lovejoy says More can hardly have been mindful of **Sutta Nipāta 1073–1076**.

The review thus uses a text-specific citation, not a generic appeal to `Buddhism` or `Oriental thought`.

### What this establishes

> **A source Lovejoy explicitly privileged in 1898 for Buddhist technical-semantic adjudication remains active in his published interpretive practice in 1906.**

This is a direct source-practice continuity across eight years.

### What it does not establish

- not the same physical copy/edition unless further provenance emerges;
- not continuous annual Buddhist research from 1898 to 1906;
- not that Buddhism `caused` Lovejoy's political relativism or primitive energetics;
- not that the mature history-of-ideas method derived from the *Sutta Nipāta*.

The correct historical claim is continuity of **source-controlled interpretive practice** across changing objects and scales.

## 6. The More review also gives a direct 1906 America / Orient / democracy junction

Lovejoy describes More's Buddhist/Indian formation as an `historically significant incident in the story of the intellectual relations of America and the Orient`.

He distinguishes More's austere response to `Indian Buddhism` from a more sentimental version of negative philosophy associated with Vedānta. He then says More's resulting philosophy is mainly unsound/illogical and is not a natural product or interpretation of `our American and democratic life`, criticizing its inability to assign adequate significance to personality and social relationships.

This is not a claim about Buddhist influence on Lovejoy's politics. It is nevertheless a direct 1906 textual junction in which Lovejoy himself places:

`Indian Buddhism / Vedānta`
↔ `America–Orient intellectual relations`
↔ `American and democratic life`.

That junction is JHI-relevant because it demonstrates that the Indic materials were not a discarded student episode by 1906.

## 9. JHI-facing consequence

The new bridge permits a more precise sentence than a general claim that non-Western materials `mattered` to early Lovejoy:

> **The Buddhist source practice was still active in 1906. In April, Lovejoy used the *Sutta Nipāta* to challenge an American critic's positive reconstruction of Nirvāṇa, while explicitly praising comparison and correlation across Greek, European and Oriental literatures. Three months later he correlated heterogeneous ethnographic evidence into a cross-regional `primitive philosophy`. The connection is not Buddhist causation; it is continuity in the discipline of moving from local evidence to claims at different scales.**

For a 2400-word JHI piece, the most valuable detail is probably the *Sutta Nipāta* recurrence rather than an extended Tylor genealogy.
