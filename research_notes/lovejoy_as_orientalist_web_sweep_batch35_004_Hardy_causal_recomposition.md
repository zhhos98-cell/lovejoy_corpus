## Core advance

The corrected 004 transcription contains a direct source-control hit that changes the argument materially. On notebook PDF p.33 / manuscript p.97, under the heading **“Upādāna, Relation to Karma,”** Lovejoy asks, in substance:

> may `upādāna` be called the cause of the **fact** of continued existence, and karma the cause of the **manner** of continued existence? — `Hardy, p. 394`.

The wording is transcriptionally medium-confidence, but the argumentative opposition and the page citation are clear. The distinction published in Lovejoy’s 1898 JAOS article — karma as cause of the **particular condition** in which a person is reborn and `upādāna` as cause of the **fact of rebirth in itself** — is therefore not an isolated Lovejoy invention. It is visibly worked up in 004 from **R. Spence Hardy, _A Manual of Budhism_ (1853), p.394**.

This supersedes the provisional claim in batch 34 that Lovejoy’s strongest remaining analytical specificity lay simply in allocating causal roles between karma and `upādāna`. The allocation itself is upstream. What remains potentially Lovejoy-specific is the **recomposition and historical explanation of that allocation**.

## 1. Hardy 1853 is now a direct notebook source, not merely contextual background

Hardy’s _Manual_ is already present elsewhere in 004: the corrected transcription cites `Hardy, p.386` during the `saṅkhāra = karma` discussion. The p.33 note now establishes a second, much more consequential use: `Hardy, p.394` is explicitly attached to the causal distinction between `upādāna` and karma.

Jacobi 1898 independently preserves Hardy’s p.394 wording. He quotes Hardy to the effect that a new existence is produced by `upādāna`, while the manner of its operation is controlled by the karma with which it is connected; Hardy admits that the causal relation can look ambiguous. Jacobi then uses this passage in support of his proposal that the Buddhist `upādāna` contains or parallels the Sāṁkhya/orthodox `adṛṣṭa` or karma.

Thus the historical source chain no longer needs to be inferred:

`Hardy 1853 p.394 → 004 p.33 explicit notebook uptake → Lovejoy 1898 published causal distinction`

Jacobi 1898 supplies an independent contemporary witness to the same Hardy locus, but **Jacobi is not needed as the transmission route to Lovejoy**, because Lovejoy’s notebook directly cites Hardy p.394.

## 2. The most important finding is divergence from one shared source

Hardy’s p.394 observation generated at least two different late-1890s reconstructions.

### Jacobi 1898

Jacobi quotes Hardy and moves toward **assimilation**:

- `upādāna` is connected with `adṛṣṭa` / karma;
- passions and sinful dispositions permit karmic effects and generate new karma;
- the role of `upādāna` is treated as parallel to `adṛṣṭa` in orthodox philosophy.

The point of Hardy’s two-factor description is therefore partly absorbed into a genealogical/systematic analogy with Sāṁkhya-Yoga.

### Lovejoy 004 → 1898

Lovejoy moves in the opposite direction: **differentiation**.

The notebook already frames Hardy p.394 as a question about two distinct causal jobs: fact versus manner of continued existence. In the 1898 article this becomes fully explicit:

- karma determines the **particular condition** of rebirth;
- `upādāna` determines the **fact of rebirth as such**.

Lovejoy therefore does not merely repeat Hardy’s ambiguity. He turns it into a stable division of explanatory labour.

This is much more historically useful than a priority claim. The same Hardy passage becomes a branching point:

`Hardy p.394`

→ Jacobi: `upādāna` pulled toward karma / `adṛṣṭa`

→ Lovejoy: `upādāna` and karma assigned different causal functions

## 3. Oldenberg 1898 supplies the opposing textual control

Oldenberg’s recovered `Buddhistische Studien` section attacks Jacobi’s construction precisely at this point. He says the proposed Buddhist transformation of `adṛṣṭa` is speculative and insists that the meaning of `upādāna` must first be established from Buddhist usage of `upādāna / upādiyati`.

Oldenberg then reconstructs the causal sequence:

`phassa → vedanā → taṇhā → upādāna`

as contact → feeling → desire → grasping/appropriation, and explicitly distinguishes grasping from both contact and desire. He thereby drives `upādāna` **away from karma/adṛṣṭa**.

This creates a more precise triangle:

- **Hardy:** two causal factors, but causal language remains partly unstable;
- **Jacobi:** interprets Hardy through a Sāṁkhya/`adṛṣṭa` analogy;
- **Oldenberg:** rejects that analogy and insists on Buddhist lexical/textual use.

Lovejoy’s published position is best understood against this triangle, without assuming direct dependence on Oldenberg’s 1898 article.

## 4. Lovejoy’s narrower increment: preserve Hardy’s bifurcation while changing its explanation

The 1898 article does three things together that should now be treated as the stronger Lovejoy-specific configuration.

### A. It preserves Hardy’s two-factor causal architecture

The notebook’s `fact` versus `manner` question is carried directly into print as `fact of rebirth` versus `particular condition` of rebirth.

### B. It refuses to collapse `upādāna` into karma

Lovejoy’s extended Pāli testing defines `upādāna` as attachment / habitual identification of will and interests with the skandhas. Its semantic identity is therefore separated from karma even though both have causal roles in rebirth.

This is the direction Oldenberg also strongly favors, but Lovejoy’s notebook shows that the fact/manner distinction itself was being built through Hardy before publication. We therefore do not need an Oldenberg-dependence claim to explain the published structure.

### C. It historicizes why Buddhism would need two different causal terms

This is the clearest addition beyond Hardy’s descriptive statement. Lovejoy argues that `karma` came to Buddhism with a prehistory: it already named moral causation determining a better or worse future state. Buddhism, however, introduced the possibility of ending rebirth altogether. Once rebirth itself could cease, the **fact of rebirth as such** required its own causal explanation; Lovejoy assigns that role to `upādāna`.

The published argument therefore converts Hardy’s practical two-factor description into a historical-systematic thesis:

> inherited `karma` explains **which** rebirth; the specifically Buddhist problem of attachment explains **whether** rebirth occurs at all.

This is a more defensible Lovejoyian increment than the causal distinction by itself.

## 5. 004 now shows a source-to-operation transformation at unusually fine resolution

The sequence is unusually explicit:

1. Lovejoy encounters/records Hardy p.394.
2. He rewrites the passage as an analytic question: fact versus manner of continued existence.
3. He tests `upādāna` separately through Nidāna-Saṁyutta, khandha materials, Burnouf, Childers, Warren, Oldenberg and other textual apparatus.
4. In print he turns the question into a definite causal distinction.
5. He then supplies a history of why the two terms have distinct functions in the Buddhist configuration.

This gives the project something stronger than a vague “methodological continuity.” It gives a **documentable transformation of a named source statement into an authorial explanatory rule**.

The historiographical value is that Lovejoy’s operation is neither invention ex nihilo nor passive borrowing. It is **reweighting and recomposition**:

`source observation → analytic distinction → textual testing → historical-functional reconstruction`.

## 6. Consequence for batch 31’s “functional non-equivalence” claim

Batch 31 treated `karma ≠ upādāna` as one of the strongest examples of Lovejoy’s functional discrimination. The operation remains real, but its provenance must now be stated more carefully.

What is inherited / upstream:

- Hardy already distinguishes reproduction from the manner in which it occurs;
- Jacobi quotes the same source;
- Oldenberg independently differentiates adjacent nidāna functions.

What Lovejoy demonstrably does:

- selects Hardy’s two-factor distinction;
- turns it into the explicit `fact` / `manner` question in 004;
- stabilizes it in print as `fact of rebirth` / `particular condition`;
- embeds it in a historical account of inherited versus specifically Buddhist causal vocabulary.

So the bridge toward later history-of-ideas practice should be reframed from **“Lovejoy invents functional non-equivalence”** to:

> Lovejoy learns to separate the historical provenance of a term from the explanatory function it performs in a later configuration, and he can turn an inherited distinction into a new historical account of why the distinction exists.

That is closer to what the surviving documents actually show.

## 7. Relation to `upādisesa`

The second half of the 1898 article should now be treated similarly. Oldenberg had already argued for an ethical “residue of attachment” reading of `upādisesa`; Senart had already discussed `upadhi/upādi` and the skandha problem. The notebook nevertheless extends the testing across Sutta-Nipāta and related passages and explicitly argues that positive/negative `upādisesa` forms classify residual attachment/impurity rather than the physical persistence of present skandhas.

Here too the likely Lovejoy contribution is **synthesis and controlled recombination**, not first discovery of the semantic direction.

The two halves of the article therefore become structurally parallel:

- `upādāna`: inherited causal distinction + independent textual testing + historical-functional reconstruction;
- `upādisesa`: inherited Oldenberg/Senart/Childers problem + wider textual testing + recomposed ethical interpretation.

## 8. Revised `004 → 1898 → 1902` thesis

The 004 route can now be stated more precisely:

> Notebook 004 records Lovejoy working inside a live and heavily mediated Indological field. Its historical value lies not in revealing wholly new propositions before publication, but in showing how he converts source propositions into distinctions whose provenance and present function are analytically separated. The Hardy p.394 note is the clearest case: an inherited distinction between two causes of renewed existence is recast as `fact` versus `manner`, then published as a historical explanation of why Buddhism can retain the older category of karma while giving `upādāna` a different causal role. By 1902 the same division of explanatory labour — historical genealogy on one side, logical/functioning configuration on the other — is operating at the scale of philosophical categories.

This keeps the `004 → 1898 → 1902` continuity, but grounds it in a named source transformation rather than in a retrospective claim about proto-`unit-ideas`.
