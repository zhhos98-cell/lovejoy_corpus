## Cambridge Conferences 1896–97: actor-bearing participation

A newly promoted contemporary witness adds a different kind of formation evidence. *Prabuddha Bhārata, or Awakened India*, vol. I, no. 9 (Madras, March 1897), prints a collective address to Swami Vivekananda from members of the Cambridge Conferences. The signatory block names **A. O. Lovejoy** and identifies him as secretary of the Harvard Graduate Philosophical Society for 1896–97. The address itself defines the Conferences as devoted to comparative study in ethics, philosophy, and religion, praises expositions of Vedānta by Vivekananda and Saradananda, and includes `great Aryan Family` language in its transnational-fraternity rhetoric.

This moves one proposition beyond institutional proximity: Lovejoy is represented in a contemporary actor-bearing carrier as participating in the Cambridge Conferences collective communication. It does not establish individual authorship of the address, attendance at any particular Conference lecture, or proposition-level uptake from Vivekananda, Saradananda, Dharmapala, Lanman, or another participant.

Sara Chapman Bull's contemporary account of the 1896–97 Cambridge Conferences in *The Outlook* documents a programme containing Saradananda on Hindu ethics and modern India, Dharmapala on Buddhism, Lanman on the practical teachings of Buddha, Indian-literature lectures, Vedānta lectures, and additional Buddhism lectures. This is now usable as the immediate institutional problem-field around Lovejoy's documented participation, while **programme availability remains distinct from attendance and influence**.

## Paris 1898–99: actor-level evidence

The strongest formation evidence is JHU MS-0873, Daniel J. Wilson's archival transcription witness of Lovejoy correspondence.

**9 Oct 1898:** before Paris lectures began, Lovejoy described his intended year as work in `philosophy and comparative religion`.

**20 Oct 1898:** he proposed to elucidate Buddhist theoretical presuppositions/doctrines philosophically and explicitly diagnosed a jurisdiction problem: philologists expounded Buddhist literature while caring little for philosophy; philosophers reconstructed Buddhist conceptions while neglecting textual technicalities. He identified Sylvain Lévi as the scholar he wished to study under for this purpose.

**Dec 1898:** Lovejoy reported that no Pāli Buddhism course was offered that year. The substitute practice was occasional private reading at Lévi's house. Wilson's typescript reads `Petakas`, cautiously normalizable as likely `Piṭakas` but not diplomatically replaceable. The same letter shows Hebrew Wisdom/Ecclesiastes, Maurice Vernes, Jean Réville, Faculty of Theology library use, and a Sabatier course tried and abandoned as outside the year's line of work.

Paris was therefore not a single-track Buddhist apprenticeship.
