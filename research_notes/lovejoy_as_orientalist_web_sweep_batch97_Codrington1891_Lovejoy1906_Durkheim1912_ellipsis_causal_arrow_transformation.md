# Batch 97 — One Codrington passage, two source operations: Lovejoy 1906, Durkheim 1912, and ellipsis-mediated causal-arrow transformation

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced
Scope: follow Batch96's highest-value test by collating R. H. Codrington, *The Melanesians* (1891), ch. VII / p.118 n.1–p.120, against Lovejoy 1906 and Durkheim 1912. The issue is not whether all three authors use the word `mana`, but what each allows the **same source passage** to prove.

## Core result

The Codrington comparison exposes a source-transformation difference sharper than ordinary selective citation.

Codrington's famous mana definition contains propositions pulling in different directions:

1. mana is a non-physical, in some sense supernatural **power or influence**;
2. it is not fixed in one object and can be **conveyed** through many media;
3. yet spirits and ghosts possess and impart it;
4. most importantly, Codrington says it **essentially belongs to personal beings to originate it**;
5. Melanesian religious practice consists in obtaining or directing this mana.

The surrounding chapter strengthens propositions 3–4. Codrington says that although the power itself is impersonal, it is **always connected with some person who directs it**; a powerful stone works because a spirit is associated with it; a bone has mana because a ghost is with it; a successful fighter has the mana of a spirit or dead warrior; magic and related practices are done through ghosts and spirits.

Lovejoy and Durkheim both need Codrington's efficacy/transferability, but they handle the personal-origin clause differently.

### Lovejoy 1906

The direct primary audit in Batch78 preserved the adverse Codrington evidence as a live tension. Lovejoy foregrounds efficacy, transmissibility and unusual productivity and later denies that `supernatural` is the semantic essence of mana, but the Codrington packet remains visibly mixed: the `best authority` also says mana is originated by personal beings. Lovejoy's operation is therefore best described as **proposition-relative reweighting**.

### Durkheim 1912

Durkheim quotes the same Codrington definition while constructing mana as the exact equivalent of Sioux `wakan` and Iroquois `orenda`. His quotation keeps the non-physical force, physical manifestation, non-fixity and conveyability clauses; then a **visible ellipsis** removes the sentence saying spirits have/impart mana and personal beings originate it; the quotation resumes with the proposition that Melanesian religion consists in obtaining mana.

Immediately afterward Durkheim says this is the same `anonymous and diffuse force` found in Australian totemism and stresses the `same impersonality`.

This yields the central result:

> **the ellipsis is typographically visible but epistemically consequential.**

The omitted material is not peripheral description. It bears directly on the **causal direction** between personal beings and impersonal force.

A compact contrast:

> **Lovejoy preserves adverse evidence and reweights it; Durkheim's excerpt removes the adverse causal clause from the local warrant sequence.**

This does not prove deceptive intent. It does show a different quotation architecture.

---

## 1. Codrington's actual causal arrow

The famous note reproduced from Codrington's earlier letter to Max Müller says that mana:

- is a power/influence distinct from ordinary physical power;
- manifests through physical force or excellence;
- is mobile and transferable;
- is possessed and imparted by spirits;
- **essentially belongs to personal beings to originate**;
- can then operate through water, stones, bones and other media.

The surrounding prose removes any possibility that the personal-origin sentence is an isolated verbal accident.

Codrington continues:

`impersonal power`
→ `always connected with some person who directs it`;

`powerful stone`
→ powerful because a spirit is associated with it;

`dead man's bone`
→ mana because the ghost is with it;

`human efficacy`
→ mana received from spirit/ghost;

`religious/magical action`
→ performed by aid of ghosts and spirits.

Thus the source's architecture is not simply:

`impersonal force -> personal manifestations`.

It is at least strongly compatible with:

`personal/spiritual agents -> impart/originate mana -> mana acts through persons/things/media`.

This directionality is exactly what later universalist impersonal-power theories must either incorporate, reinterpret or demote.

---

## 2. Durkheim's quotation is a marked ellipsis, not a silent falsification

This distinction matters.

Durkheim's French quotation is not fabricated and does not silently replace Codrington's wording. The printed text visibly marks ellipses. It retains:

- `force absolutely distinct from material force`;
- non-physical / in a sense supernatural force;
- manifestation in physical power and superiority;
- non-fixity and conveyability;
- religion as obtaining/using mana.

Between `mana can be brought upon all sorts of things` and `all Melanesian religion consists...`, Durkheim prints an ellipsis.

What falls inside that ellipsis in Codrington is precisely:

- spirits possess mana;
- spirits impart mana;
- mana essentially belongs to personal beings to originate;
- impersonal media transmit a power whose origin remains personal.

The correct description is therefore:

> **marked selective quotation / ellipsis-mediated source transformation.**

Not:

> `Durkheim secretly fabricated a quotation.`

The evidentiary issue concerns **selection**, not quotation authenticity.

---

## 3. The omitted clause is propositionally load-bearing for Durkheim's use

Durkheim does not cite Codrington merely as an example that Melanesians have some notion called mana. He needs the passage at a proof-bearing point.

His sequence is:

`Australian analyst-inferred totemic force`
→ `North-American generalized impersonal forces`
→ `Melanesian mana as exact equivalent`
→ `same impersonality / same ubiquity`
→ `cross-cultural comparison legitimates attribution back to Australian societies`.

The Codrington quotation is therefore part of the **cross-cultural reproof** recovered in Batch96.

Within that proof, Codrington's personal-origin clause would be adverse evidence because it complicates `same impersonality`. A power that is itself impersonal but whose origin is essentially personal does not support the same causal genealogy as a primordial impersonal force from which spirits later differentiate.

So the ellipsis has a specific logical effect:

`mixed Codrington mana`
→ excerpted as
`mobile impersonal efficacy`
→ recruited as warrant for
`primordial impersonal totemic force`.

This is more precise than saying Durkheim simply `misread mana`.

---

## 4. Durkheim later reverses Codrington's causal arrow

The source transformation becomes still clearer a few pages later.

Durkheim argues that:

- spirits, demons, genii and gods are **concrete forms taken by the prior energy/potentiality** as it becomes individualized;
- particular sacred beings are individualized forms of the essential impersonal principle;
- rites can possess efficacy independently of divine/spiritual intervention;
- even divinities retain an impersonal halo because religious forces cannot fully individualize.

This is nearly the reverse of the adverse Codrington clause.

### Codrington

`personal beings / spirits`
→ originate or impart
`mana`
→ acts through impersonal media.

### Durkheim

`anonymous impersonal religious force`
→ individualizes / concentrates
→ `spirits, demons, gods`.

The omitted sentence therefore concerns not merely `how personal mana is`. It contains a **genealogical arrow opposite to Durkheim's theory**.

A useful term for the transformation is:

> **causal-arrow normalization**

or, more conservatively:

> **ellipsis-mediated removal of a directional contradiction.**

The second formulation should be preferred in finished prose because it describes textual effect without claiming private intention.

## 5. Lovejoy's treatment supplies an unusually strong control

Batch78 already established Lovejoy's proposition-relative use of Codrington.

Lovejoy calls Codrington a `best authority` and uses him for:

- mana as power/influence;
- transferability/conveyability;
- efficacy/productivity;
- distributed operation through persons and objects.

But Lovejoy's source audit does not allow Codrington unrestricted interpretive authority. It keeps visible two adverse clauses:

- mana is `in a way supernatural`;
- it `essentially belongs to personal beings to originate it`.

Lovejoy later reweights the first: `supernatural/supernormal` is not the semantic essence; efficacy/power/productivity is. Yet the personal-origin tension is not made to disappear through the quotation itself.

Thus:

### Lovejoy

`mixed witness`
→ decompose propositions
→ retain adverse clause
→ reweight semantic center.

### Durkheim

`mixed witness`
→ excerpt propositions
→ ellipsis removes adverse causal clause
→ deploy remaining packet as cross-cultural warrant.

This makes Codrington a rare **same-source laboratory** for two distinct proof styles.

---

## 6. This deepens `source-function partition` into `quotation architecture`

Batches77 and 89 established:

`source × proposition -> evidentiary competence`.

Batch97 adds another operation before that judgment can even occur:

`source text`
→ `quoted/excerpted proposition set`
→ `evidentiary function`
→ `licensed claim`.

A source can therefore be transformed not only by accepting one proposition and rejecting another, but by controlling **which propositions enter the visible quotation packet at all**.

The reception/proof model should now distinguish:

1. source-level presence;
2. proposition-level extraction;
3. quotation/excerpt architecture;
4. source-function assignment;
5. claim licensed;
6. adverse evidence retained, corrected, omitted, or displaced elsewhere.

This is especially important in comparative religion, where canonical ethnographic passages are repeatedly re-quoted and can acquire a stable `citation shadow` detached from their surrounding qualifications.

---

## 7. Codrington's own chapter prevents `impersonal mana` from being treated as a clean native theory

The broader Chapter VII makes the tension systematic.

Codrington explicitly says the power is itself impersonal but always connected to a person who directs it. He then introduces personal intelligent beings full of mana and says they are invoked and approached. Sacrifice and offerings go to spirits/ghosts, and magic/witchcraft/weather work is done by their aid.

Therefore one should not reduce Codrington's position to either extreme:

- `mana is simply a personal spirit` — false;
- `mana is an autonomous impersonal energy independent of personal/spiritual agency` — also false for Codrington's exposition.

His source gives a **hybrid causal configuration**:

`impersonal efficacy`
+
`personal/spiritual origination/directorship`
+
`material mediation/transfer`.

Later theorists can universalize different axes of that hybrid.

---

## 8. Meylan / Jonathan Z. Smith control: substantively right, wording needs precision

Meylan, following Jonathan Z. Smith, already identifies Durkheim's treatment as a problematic translation that removes passages suggesting mana's link with persons.

The direct primary collation confirms the substantive point: the personal-origin clause is absent from Durkheim's excerpt at exactly the point where the quotation is used to establish impersonality.

But primary typography requires a refinement:

- the omission is **marked by ellipsis**;
- therefore `erased` should be understood as analytical description of what the excerpt excludes, not as a claim that Durkheim concealed all evidence of textual omission.

A strong, source-faithful formulation is:

> **Durkheim's ellipsis openly signals abbreviation while removing the clause that most directly conflicts with the causal genealogy for which Codrington is being recruited.**

---

## 9. Relation to Batch93: fingerprints reside in transformations, not vocabulary

Batch93 showed that `mechanical force`, `energy`, electricity imagery and mana-like power are distributed field vocabulary and cannot identify Lovejoy reception.

Batch97 supplies a stronger diagnostic object: **source transformation patterns**.

Two writers can cite the same authority and use the same nouns while doing different intellectual work.

What matters is:

- which source clauses survive quotation;
- which adverse statements remain visible;
- what causal arrow is assigned;
- what proposition the excerpt is made to warrant;
- whether contradiction is solved by correction, reweighting, ellipsis, or reclassification.

Thus the project can shift from lexical fingerprinting toward **inferential and documentary fingerprints**.

---

## 10. Paper-level formulation

A strong paragraph now available is:

> **Codrington's famous description of mana provides an unusually controlled comparison between Lovejoy and Durkheim because both authors work from the same internally divided witness. Codrington calls mana an impersonal and transferable power, yet also insists that spirits impart it and that it essentially belongs to personal beings to originate. Lovejoy's 1906 discussion preserves this tension and subsequently reweights the semantic evidence toward efficacy, power, and productivity. Durkheim, by contrast, quotes Codrington at the point where Melanesian mana is made to validate the impersonal totemic principle inferred for Australia, but a marked ellipsis removes the personal-origin clause before the quotation resumes with the claim that Melanesian religion consists in obtaining mana. The textual operation matters because Durkheim later reverses the omitted genealogy: spirits, demons, and gods become individualized forms of a prior anonymous religious force. The difference is therefore not simply two theories of mana but two procedures for making an adverse ethnographic sentence bear—or cease to bear—on a comparative causal abstraction.**

Compact formula:

> **Lovejoy partitions the witness; Durkheim excerpts the witness.**

More exact:

> **Lovejoy preserves and reweights adverse evidence; Durkheim's marked ellipsis removes a directional contradiction from the proof-bearing excerpt.**

## Evidence limits

1. The ellipsis is visible; no claim of secret textual falsification is warranted.
2. Textual effect does not establish subjective intent. `Warrant shaping` is an analytical description, not a psychological accusation.
3. Durkheim discusses spirits and personal religious beings elsewhere, but he explains them as individualizations of prior impersonal force; this does not restore Codrington's personal-origin causal arrow.
4. Lovejoy also selectively interprets Codrington; retaining adverse evidence does not make his final abstraction neutral or exhaustive.
5. The shared Codrington source proves a common documentary object, not direct Lovejoy→Durkheim transmission.
6. Exact quotation punctuation should be checked against page images if reproduced in publication; the facsimile-linked Wikisource pages are currently direct enough for proposition-level collation.
