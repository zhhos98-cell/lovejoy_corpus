# JHI Blog — same problem or shared inferential form?

Date: 2026-08-20
Status: conceptual correction before pitch submission

## Core correction

The Warren, Senart, upādisesa, Bankim/Mitra, and Skinner materials should **not** be described as literally the same historical problem.

They are different first-order problems:

- Warren: what relation holds between *taṇhā* and *upādāna* inside dependent origination?
- Senart: what does repetition/compositeness imply about the historical formation and intelligibility of the twelve-nidāna formula?
- upādisesa: how should the meaning of a technical term be decided when etymology and usage pull apart, and how certain can the result be?
- Bankim/Mitra: what can similarities and antecedent materials establish about historical borrowing, direction, and the existence of a complete system?
- Skinner/Lovejoy: under what conditions may a historian identify an idea as the same across changing linguistic/historical contexts?

These are not one actor-defined controversy, and the actors do not share one explicit meta-problem.

## What is genuinely shared

They exhibit a **recurring inferential form**:

> evidence establishes relation A; may the historian infer relation B from it without further warrant?

Examples:

- semantic proximity → identity?
- compositeness → accidental/unintelligible arrangement?
- etymology → meaning in use?
- similarity → historical dependence/direction?
- conceptual resemblance across texts → identity of an idea across contexts?

This is a second-order homology reconstructed by us, not a single nineteenth-century problem stated by the actors.

## Safer vocabulary

Prefer:

- `a family of related proof problems`
- `a recurring inferential form`
- `structurally analogous disputes`
- `different controversies that repeatedly asked how far one relation could license another`
- `a shared problem-form rather than a single shared problem`

Avoid:

- `the same problem`
- `one transregional controversy` if it implies direct coordination among all actors
- `the nineteenth-century problem of historical inference` as if actor-native and unitary

## Pitch consequence

Do not write:

`these local disputes formed part of the same transregional problem of historical inference`.

Better:

`Taken together, these otherwise distinct disputes reveal a recurring problem of historical inference: how far one established relation could justify another.`

Or even safer:

`The piece asks what becomes visible when these distinct disputes are read together: again and again, scholars had to decide whether one kind of relation — resemblance, repetition, derivation, textual use — was enough to establish another.`

This clearly marks the unification as the author's reconstruction.

## Relation to Skinner

The Skinner comparison should therefore be framed as **structural analogy**, not continuity of the same problem.

Safe:

`The later Lovejoy-Skinner dispute posed a different first-order question, but it turned on a structurally similar issue of warrant: when may resemblance across contexts count as identity of an idea?`

Unsafe:

`Skinner and Lovejoy were still debating the same problem first posed in 1890s Buddhology.`

No transmission or continuity of problem-language has been established.

## Bottom line

The strongest final formulation is:

> **Not the same problem, but the same kind of inferential difficulty.**

This distinction should govern the pitch and final essay.
