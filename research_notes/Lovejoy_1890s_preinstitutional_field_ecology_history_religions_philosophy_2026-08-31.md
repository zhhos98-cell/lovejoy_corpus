# Lovejoy in the 1890s — pre-institutional field ecology of history of religions and history of philosophy

Date: 2026-08-31
Status: **DIRECT / STRONG CONTEXTUAL CALIBRATION / NO CAUSAL GENEALOGY**

## Core result

Lovejoy's 1898 Buddhist work should not be described as occurring before historical/relational methods existed and then later becoming `history of ideas` through individual invention.

The 1890s field already exhibits a characteristic mismatch:

`SCHOLARLY PRACTICES / CARRIERS / PROBLEMS EXIST`
while
`DISCIPLINARY JURISDICTION / PERMANENT INSTITUTIONAL FORM REMAINS UNSETTLED`.

Two adjacent histories matter:

1. the American `historical study of religions`, growing out of Oriental-language/source scholarship and comparative study of institutions/customs while still fighting for university recognition;
2. nineteenth-century history of philosophy organized around the formation/development of problems and conceptions, later identified by Catana as an important context for Lovejoy's actual historiographical practice.

This is useful calibration for the blog because it explains why Harvard and JAOS could already juxtapose philology, Buddhist/Indic source work, comparative religion and large historical/philosophical questions without transmitting one unified `method`.

## I. Jastrow 1893: history of religions is a recent science built from two evidentiary streams

Morris Jastrow, Jr., `Recent Movements in the Historical Study of Religions in America`, *The Biblical World* 1.1 (Jan. 1893), 24–32.

Direct public PDF discovery:

https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0e/THE_BIBLICAL_WORLD_1893-01-_Vol_1_Iss_1_%28IA_sim_biblical-world_1893-01_1_1%29.pdf

The opening directly characterizes religion as an ancient object of speculation but a very recent object of historical/scientific investigation.

Jastrow says a properly historical study requires an impartial/broad investigation of facts and argues that the field only becomes recognizable around the middle of the nineteenth century.

He identifies two major impulses:

1. research into the **history and literatures of the ancient Orient**;
2. investigation of **widely distributed institutions and customs** connected with religious phenomena.

This is a compact late-nineteenth-century architecture:

`ORIENTAL TEXT / LANGUAGE / HISTORY`
+
`DISTRIBUTED CUSTOM / INSTITUTION COMPARISON`
->
`HISTORICAL STUDY OF RELIGIONS`.

It strongly resembles the jurisdictional plurality visible in Lovejoy's Harvard curriculum and later notebook objects, but it does not prove personal uptake from Jastrow.

## II. 1891–97: American Lectures create a cross-city history-of-religions carrier before stable university chairs

Daniel G. Brinton, *Religions of Primitive Peoples*, American Lectures on the History of Religions, Second Series 1896–97.

Project Gutenberg:

https://www.gutenberg.org/files/61220/61220-h/61220-h.htm

The printed announcement reconstructs an organizational event beginning in December 1891. Fifteen people interested in promoting the historical study of religions called a conference in Philadelphia to create annual popular lecture courses on the model of the Hibbert Lectures.

The proposed carrier was geographically distributed across cities including:

- Baltimore;
- Boston;
- Brooklyn;
- Chicago;
- New York;
- Philadelphia;
- other participating centers.

Jastrow proposed using existing institutions and lecture associations, with a committee coordinating lecturers and topics.

Brinton's 1896–97 *Religions of Primitive Peoples* is explicitly the **Second Series**.

Historical significance:

> `HISTORY OF RELIGIONS` could acquire lecture-series, publication and intercity organizational infrastructure before it possessed fully stabilized departmental/chair form across American universities.

This is an important comparative institutional pattern for later JHI history:

`CARRIER CAPACITY`
may precede
`STABLE DISCIPLINARY JURISDICTION`.

Do not infer that this lecture organization directly shaped Lovejoy's career without actor-level evidence.

## III. Jastrow 1899: active scholarship coexists with complaint about weak university institutionalization

Morris Jastrow, Jr., `The Historical Study of Religions in Universities and Colleges`, JAOS 20 (1899), 317–325.

Direct JSTOR issue/metadata:

https://www.jstor.org/stable/i225070

JSTOR stable article route:

https://www.jstor.org/stable/592338

A recent historiographical study by Donald Wiebe directly quotes Jastrow's complaint that the scientific study of religion had entered the curriculum of only a small number of American universities and colleges, and especially notes Jastrow's statement that even Harvard lacked a chair specifically for the historical/scientific study of religion.

Wiebe 2024:

https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/rsr.17064

Jastrow nevertheless says conditions are favorable for further measures and calls for official recognition and special/permanent provision.

Thus 1899 gives an especially useful controlled mismatch:

`HARVARD / JAOS CONTAIN ACTUAL RELIGION-SCHOLARSHIP PRACTICES`
while
`JASTROW SAYS NO DEDICATED HARVARD CHAIR / INSTITUTIONAL RECOGNITION`.

This protects the analysis from equating:

`COURSE / ARTICLE / LECTURE EXISTENCE`
with
`DISCIPLINE FULLY INSTITUTIONALIZED`.

`ORIENTAL SOCIETY JOURNAL`

`JOURNAL TITLE / INSTITUTION NAME`
!=
`ONE RESOLUTION / ONE METHOD / ONE OBJECT TYPE`.

## V. Windelband 1893: problem-history is already explicit inside history of philosophy

Wilhelm Windelband, *A History of Philosophy: With Especial Reference to the Formation and Development of Its Problems and Conceptions*, authorized English translation by James H. Tufts (New York/London: Macmillan, 1893).

Direct public-domain scan:

https://digicoll.lib.berkeley.edu/record/206523

PDF:

https://digicoll.lib.berkeley.edu/record/206523/files/b22240124_C024473888.pdf

The title itself is historically important:

`HISTORY OF PHILOSOPHY`
=`FORMATION + DEVELOPMENT OF PROBLEMS AND CONCEPTIONS`.

The book's contents are organized partly by historical periods and partly by theoretical/practical problems rather than simply by isolated philosopher biographies.

Open Library's descriptive metadata explicitly notes Windelband's problem-centered mode of exposition.

https://openlibrary.org/books/OL7209504M/A_history_of_philosophy

Use carefully: metadata description is secondary; the primary title/contents are direct.

## VI. Catana 2010 converts this from generic background into a direct Lovejoy historiography problem

Leo Catana's JHI article:

`Lovejoy's Readings of Bruno: Or How Nineteenth-Century History of Philosophy Was "Transformed" into the History of Ideas`, JHI 71.1 (2010), 91–112.

Abstract control:

https://philpapers.org/rec/LEOLRO

Catana argues that Lovejoy's methodological declarations in *Great Chain* were institutional/rhetorical declarations that did not adequately describe his actual historiographical practice; the Bruno practice can instead be contextualized inside nineteenth-century general histories of philosophy.

This licenses a key historical correction:

`HISTORY OF IDEAS PROGRAMME DECLARATION`
!=
`ZERO POINT OF THE PRACTICES IT CLAIMS`.

And:

`DISCIPLINARY RENAMING / BOUNDARY-MAKING`
may reclassify practices that have older carrier histories.

Catana's claim is case-specific and should not be overextended to every Lovejoy object.

## VII. Calibration against the current JHI blog

The blog's Harvard paragraph currently says Harvard placed close textual study, comparison among related traditions and broad comparative religion within the same graduate environment, while refusing to call this one unified method.

The 1890s web sweep materially strengthens that formulation.

### New larger context

Harvard's curricular plurality is not an isolated institutional curiosity. It belongs to a wider moment in which:

- Oriental source research;
- comparative customs/institutions;
- historical/scientific study of religions;
- history of philosophy organized by problems/conceptions;
- new lecture organizations;
- older journals carrying newer historical-religion functions

coexist while disciplinary recognition remains unsettled.

Possible backstage formula:

> **Lovejoy entered Buddhist scholarship at a moment when the practices later distributed among Indology, comparative religion, history of religions and intellectual/history-of-philosophy work were already active, but their institutional boundaries were still being negotiated.**

This is stronger than treating Harvard as the sole explanatory environment.

### Publication-facing caution

Adding this entire institutional prehistory to a 1,880-word blog would likely dilute the notebook-centered architecture.

The immediate use is calibration:

- keep Harvard paragraph;
- understand it as a local window on wider field ecology;
- if one endnote is ever expanded, Jastrow 1899 / JAOS 20 could supply a compact external control.

## VIII. Comparative bridge to JHI without direct genealogy

A revealing structural comparison now exists:

### 1890s history of religions

`PRACTICE + ARTICLES + LECTURES + CROSS-CITY ORGANIZATION`
-> `CALL FOR OFFICIAL / PERMANENT UNIVERSITY RECOGNITION`.

### 1930s–40 history of ideas

Existing repo evidence:

`CLUB + ACLS PLANNING + BOOK PROJECTS + ARTICLES`
-> `JHI INSTITUTIONALIZATION`.

Do not write:

`HISTORY OF RELIGIONS PROVIDED THE MODEL FOR JHI`.

No such direct transmission is established.

The comparative point is more general:

> **Historical disciplines can exist as distributed practices and carriers before a later institutional boundary makes them look like a coherent field.**

This is highly relevant to the article's general problem of retrospective classification.

## Compact restart shorthand

> **1890s FIELD ECOLOGY: Jastrow 1893 defines historical study of religions as a recent science driven by ancient-Orient textual/literary research plus comparison of widely distributed institutions/customs. American Lectures on the History of Religions organize cross-city lecture/publication infrastructure from 1891; Brinton 1896–97 is Series 2. Therefore Harvard 1896–97 should be read as a local window on a wider unsettled field ecology, not as a single transmitted method or isolated origin point.**
