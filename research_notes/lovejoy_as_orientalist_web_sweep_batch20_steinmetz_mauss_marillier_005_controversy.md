# Batch 20 — 005 inside a live controversy: Steinmetz → Mauss → Marillier’s 1898–99 seminar

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced

## Research move

The early `survivance` section of notebook `MS38_004_001_061_005` can now be placed inside a much more precise argumentative context. Marillier’s official 1898–99 EPHE report says that his Monday conference did not merely repeat his 1893/94 memoir on the survival of the soul. Its explicit task was to **refute objections by S. R. Steinmetz** to the thesis that early eschatology is amoral and that the other life is conceived as a continuation rather than compensation, reparation or punishment of terrestrial life.

Marcel Mauss’s 1896 two-part review of Steinmetz, *La religion et les origines du droit pénal d’après un livre récent*, directly reconstructs this dispute and repeatedly deploys Marillier’s `Survivance` against Steinmetz. The methodological operations in Mauss’s critique correspond strikingly to Lovejoy 005 pp.16–30: source criticism, contamination by Christian/Hindu/Buddhist contact, separation of manner-of-death or ritual conditions from moral desert, and refusal to infer a moral category from differential afterlife destinations.

This makes 005 look less like a passive summary of Marillier’s 1893 memoir and more like the working residue of a **controversy being actively re-argued in seminar**.

## 1. The target of the 1898–99 Monday conference is explicit

Marillier’s official EPHE report states that the Monday conference studied beliefs concerning `la survivance de l’âme`. Its essential task was to establish that no moral element intervenes at the origin of the eschatology of non-civilized peoples. The report then specifies:

- **refutation of objections made by Steinmetz** against Marillier’s earlier memoir;
- defence of the claim that the other life is continuation, not compensation/reparation/punishment;
- addition of **new facts especially from the two Americas**.

The same report lists Lovejoy among the auditors who `ont pris une part active aux travaux`.

Therefore Lovejoy’s documented participation coincides not merely with a topic but with a named scholarly dispute and a stated evidentiary programme.

## 2. The Steinmetz book is identified

Mauss’s 1896 review concerns:

> S. R. Steinmetz, *Ethnologische Studien zur ersten Entwickelung der Strafe: nebst einer psychologischen Abhandlung über Grausamkeit und Rachsucht*.

Modern bibliographic controls and the Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek identify the Groningen/Noordhoff work, with the first volume published in 1894. Mauss treats it as an important ethnological synthesis on punishment, vengeance, the cult/fear of the dead and religious sanctions.

For the present project the crucial issue is narrower: Steinmetz rejected what Mauss calls the theory of continuity between this life and the next and argued that post-mortem belief was never truly amoral.

## 3. Mauss makes Marillier the principal empirical counterweight

In the critical second part, Mauss says Steinmetz’s attack on the `continuation` theory is important enough to discuss immediately. His first move is explicitly to **oppose Marillier’s observations to Steinmetz’s**.

The decisive methodological sentence is:

> `la critique des textes donne de tout autres résultats que l'enregistrement de leur teneur brute et générale`

The point is not simply that Marillier collected different facts. The dispute turns on how ethnographic statements are criticized before being counted as evidence.

This is highly relevant to Lovejoy because the notebook repeatedly interrupts its own classifications to ask whether a report has been altered by contact, whether a custom has been redescribed through an imported moral vocabulary, and whether apparent reward/punishment is actually produced by ritual, status or manner of death.

## 4. Contact contamination is central to Mauss’s refutation

Mauss says many of Steinmetz’s supposed counterexamples are compromised by historical contact:

- Macusi, Kinipetu, Inuit, Narrinyeri, especially Andaman and Philippine cases show Christian influence;
- Nias and Baduwi/Javanese cases show Buddhist/Hindu influence;
- the use of the Sanskrit word `naraka` for hell is itself evidence of borrowing.

Thus an observer’s report of a heaven/hell distinction cannot simply be entered into a comparative table as proof of an indigenous retributive eschatology. The historical provenance of the idea must be tested.

### 005 correspondence

Lovejoy 005 pp.16–20 repeatedly raises exactly this problem:

- apparent moral change may result from missionary influence;
- an early North-American report is treated as suspect because missionary contact may have altered the testimony;
- diffusion from more developed peoples can introduce moral/social elements into an earlier eschatology.

PDF p.19 is especially clear: the Murray/Pawnee happy-hunting-ground source becomes a problem of **chronology and missionary contamination**, not merely an example to be classified.

The notebook therefore preserves the same source-critical operation that the Steinmetz controversy made central.

## 5. Differential destinations do not prove moral retribution

Mauss argues that the existence of different post-mortem destinies remains fully compatible with continuation theory. Differences can arise because:

- inequalities of present life continue;
- **performance of rites** determines the condition of the dead;
- **manner of death** determines the nature of future existence.

This is almost the precise diagnostic problem that dominates 005 pp.3–30.

Lovejoy tests wealth/status, ritual fidelity, social membership, suicide, drowning, childbirth, bodily condition and other selectors before allowing `strictly moral guilt/desert` to enter the classification. The Greenland/Eskimo case dated `Dec. 14` is particularly revealing: two destinations exist, but the notebook refuses to infer reward/punishment from that fact alone.

## 6. Lovejoy sharpens the controversy into a taxonomy of determinants

Marillier’s 1893 memoir and Mauss’s defence establish the negative proposition: differential afterlife does not necessarily imply justice/retribution. Lovejoy’s notebook goes a step further by asking **what positive mechanism does explain the differentiation**.

The clearest outline on PDF pp.14–15 separates roughly:

1. naturalistic continuation / terrestrial status;
2. ritual/religious performance;
3. social/community obligation or membership;
4. other collective allocation mechanisms;
5. strictly moral guilt/desert.

The p.16–30 continuation then tests suicide, mode of death, cultural contact and bodily continuity against this scheme.

This matters for the genealogy of Lovejoy’s later method. The notebook is already converting a polemical question — `is this morality?` — into a more general analytical question — **what kind of determinant or causal mechanism is actually operating?**

That same move becomes dominant in the sacrifice half of 005 and, in a different theoretical register, in the 1906 `Primitive Philosophy` article.

## 7. The seminar was adding new American evidence, not merely rereading the old memoir

The official 1898–99 report says Marillier introduced new supporting facts drawn especially from **the two Americas**. This matters for interpreting the heavy North-American material in 005 and opens a second contextual line: Elizabeth Laetitia Moon Conard’s contemporaneous research on Algonquian future-life beliefs, begun during her Paris study in 1897–98 and subsequently translated/published by Marillier.

That line is treated separately in Batch 21 because direct evidence that Conard attended Marillier’s course or supplied the specific `new facts` used in 1898–99 is not yet established.

## 8. Revised interpretation of 005 pp.1–30

The strongest formulation is now:

> **005 pp.1–30 preserve Lovejoy’s participation in a live EPHE controversy over how comparative evidence should be read. The problem was not simply whether “primitive peoples” believed in moral retribution after death. It was whether heterogeneous reports could be historically and causally decomposed before being classified: imported Christian/Hindu/Buddhist elements separated from earlier strata; status, ritual and manner-of-death selectors separated from moral desert; and differential destinations interpreted only after their operative mechanism had been identified.**

This is a much more precise pedagogical genealogy than a generic claim that Marillier influenced Lovejoy’s interest in comparative religion.

## 9. Evidence limits

### Secure

- Marillier’s official 1898–99 conference was explicitly organized around refuting Steinmetz’s objections.
- Lovejoy was officially listed among active auditors.
- Mauss’s 1896 primary critique uses Marillier’s `Survivance` directly against Steinmetz.
- Mauss’s critique turns on source/text criticism, contact contamination, ritual performance and manner of death.
- 005 pp.16–30 independently contain those same analytical operations.

### Strong inference

- The Steinmetz controversy is part of the immediate argumentative context of 005’s early section.
- Lovejoy’s emphasis on missionary/diffusion criticism is very likely connected to the seminar’s active refutation programme rather than being an isolated personal aside.

### Still unproved

- That a particular 005 sentence records words spoken by Marillier in direct reply to Steinmetz.
- That Lovejoy had personally read Mauss’s 1896 article before/during the seminar.
- The exact source of every North-American case added in the 1898–99 conference.
