## Core advance

The anti-Senart argument published by Lovejoy in 1898 has a **very close Oldenberg parallel**. In later editions of Hermann Oldenberg’s *Buddha*, the discussion of the twelve nidānas explicitly rejects Senart’s equation of `upādāna` with the five `upādānakkhandha`, cites the same Pāli loci, and defines the relevant relation through attachment/desire (`chandarāga`) toward the five khandhas. Oldenberg’s later note points readers back to his **“Buddhistische Studien,” ZDMG 52 (1898), p.690ff.**

This changes the immediate research question. The important issue is no longer whether Lovejoy was uniquely “recovering coherence” from Senart. Oldenberg was doing something sufficiently close that **priority, circulation, and precise argumentative difference must be established before novelty is assigned**.

At present there are three live possibilities:

1. **Oldenberg priority:** the same anti-Senart argument already appears in the 3rd edition of *Buddha* (1897), in which case Lovejoy’s 1898 article worked with an available prior argument even if he did not cite it at that point.
2. **1898 Oldenberg priority or parallel development:** the argument first appears in Oldenberg’s ZDMG 52 article before Lovejoy’s JAOS second-half publication.
3. **Lovejoy priority / immediate European uptake:** Oldenberg’s ZDMG p.690ff postdates Lovejoy and cites or absorbs the JAOS argument. This would be direct immediate reception and would be a major result.

The current evidence does **not** distinguish these possibilities.

## 1. What is secure about the Oldenberg parallel

Later witnesses reproduce an argument remarkably close to Lovejoy 1898:

- Senart’s p.284 claim is quoted: `upādāna` is merely a reduction/abbreviation for `upādāna-skandhas`, hence the five skandhas occur twice in the causal formula;
- the reply distinguishes `upādāna` from `upādānakkhandha`;
- Majjhima I 299ff. and Saṃyutta III 100 are used to support the distinction;
- `upādāna` is related to `chandarāga` directed toward the five khandhas;
- the supposed duplication therefore disappears.

A 1912 Hungarian exposition by Jenő Lénárd reproduces this Senart→Oldenberg controversy and confirms that Oldenberg’s later treatment was understood as a revision of his earlier account of the twelve nidānas.

This later relay is useful for identifying the argument but cannot settle its date of origin.

## 4. Why the 1897 control matters for Lovejoy’s originality

Lovejoy’s 1898 article visibly cites Oldenberg elsewhere, especially in the second half on `upādisesa`, where he praises Oldenberg’s discussion. In the first anti-Senart `upādāna` section, however, Lovejoy’s published presentation frames the dispute directly as **Senart versus his own textual/semantic rebuttal**; no Oldenberg citation is visible in the immediate argument.

That asymmetry becomes historically meaningful only after dating Oldenberg’s parallel.

- If Oldenberg 1897 already contains the same argument, Lovejoy’s omission must be explained cautiously: independent return to the same Pāli texts, unacknowledged dependence, or a difference in argumentative use.
- If Oldenberg first publishes it in ZDMG 52 later in 1898, then publication chronology and citation practice become central.
- If Oldenberg cites Lovejoy, we obtain immediate reception rather than merely later disciplinary convergence.

Until the target pages are recovered, no direction of influence should be stated.

## 8. The working novelty claim is now deliberately narrower

Even before the Oldenberg primary is recovered, one correction is already warranted. We should not claim that Lovejoy’s distinctive contribution was simply to show that a composite causal formula could nonetheless be coherent. Warren and other contemporary scholars already discussed compositeness; Oldenberg appears to have mounted a closely parallel anti-Senart defense.

The strongest Lovejoy-specific operation still visible in his 1898 paper is **functional non-equivalence**:

> common vocabulary, repetition, or shared provenance does not establish conceptual identity; components must be distinguished by the semantic and causal work they perform in the configuration.

Lovejoy does this by separating attachment from the aggregates attached to and by assigning `karma` and `upādāna` different explanatory roles in rebirth. The Oldenberg p.690ff collation will tell us whether this causal-functional distribution is genuinely distinctive or also shared.
