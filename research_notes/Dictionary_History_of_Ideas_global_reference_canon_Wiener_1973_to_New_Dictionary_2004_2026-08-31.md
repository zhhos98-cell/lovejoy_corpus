# Dictionary of the History of Ideas: global networks, Western reference canon, and the 2004 recoding

Date: 2026-08-31
Status: **DIRECT 1973 CONTENTS CONTROL + RETROSPECTIVE JHI-BOARD SELF-DESCRIPTION + 2004 GLOBAL-CORRECTION CONTROL**

## Core result

The 1973–74 *Dictionary of the History of Ideas* supplies an unusually useful test of the difference between three things which must not be conflated:

`GLOBAL SCHOLAR NETWORK`
!=
`NON-WESTERN MATERIAL PRESENT`
!=
`CANONICAL SYMMETRY IN REFERENCE ARCHITECTURE`.

Under Philip P. Wiener, the original *Dictionary* did include direct conceptual treatments of Buddhism and Islamic thought and several major East–West transmission entries. Yet the later Directors and Board of Editors of JHI themselves retrospectively characterized the work as a confident reference canon of the **Western tradition** in which non-Western traditions made only a **marginal appearance**. The 2004 *New Dictionary of the History of Ideas* then explicitly described the old work as focused primarily on European thought and advertised its own expansion to Africa, Asia, Latin America and the Middle East.

This creates a high-value longitudinal control:

> **By the early 1970s JHI/Wiener networks could be institutionally international and include non-Western specialists, while the reference canon they produced remained structurally Western-centered. Globalization of personnel and circulation did not automatically produce symmetry in the field's canonical architecture.**

---

## I. 1973 *Dictionary*: direct non-Western entries are real

Authoritative electronic edition:
University of Virginia Library, *Dictionary of the History of Ideas*, ed. Philip P. Wiener.
https://xtf.lib.virginia.edu/xtf/view?docId=DicHist/uvaBook/tei/DicHist1.xml

Volume I's contents directly place the following among `Studies of Selected Pivotal Ideas`:

- **BUDDHISM**;
- **CAUSATION IN ISLAMIC THOUGHT**;
- **CHINA IN WESTERN THOUGHT AND CULTURE**.

They appear in the same alphabetical/reference architecture as:

- `CAUSATION`;
- `CAUSATION IN HISTORY`;
- `CHAIN OF BEING`;
- `CULTURAL DEVELOPMENT IN ANTIQUITY`;
- `CULTURE AND CIVILIZATION IN MODERN TIMES`.

This matters. Non-Western thought is not wholly absent or confined to bibliography. Buddhism and Islamic causation are granted direct entry status as intellectual objects.

Bibliographic controls identify:

- Hajime Nakamura, `Buddhism`, vol. I, pp.247–257;
- Michael E. Marmura, `Causation in Islamic Thought`, vol. I, pp.286–289;
- Seyyed Hossein Nasr, `Islamic Conception of Intellectual Life`, vol. II, pp.638–652;
- Donald F. Lach, `China in Western Thought and Culture`, vol. I, pp.353–373;
- Carl T. Jackson, `Oriental Ideas in American Thought`, vol. III, pp.428–439.

These entries show at least two different non-Western roles inside the old Dictionary:

### R1 — internally addressed conceptual object

- `Buddhism`;
- `Causation in Islamic Thought`;
- `Islamic Conception of Intellectual Life`.

### R2 — source / antecedent / encounter inside Western intellectual history

- `China in Western Thought and Culture`;
- `Oriental Ideas in American Thought`.

The role distinction is essential. Counting all five simply as `global coverage` would erase the fact that some entries reconstruct a non-Western tradition internally while others organize Asia through its relation to Western thought.

## II. Lach's China entry makes the West-facing address explicit

Donald F. Lach's `China in Western Thought and Culture` is a clear case where China is a historical object primarily through changing Western representations, reception and use.

The article tracks European knowledge of China from antiquity through travelers, missionaries, Jesuit reports, Enlightenment admiration, nineteenth-century reaction and modern political fear.

Its governing question is not primarily:

`What is the internal history of Chinese thought?`

but:

`How did China become an intellectual/cultural object in Western history?`

This is exactly the `Asia as source / representation / external antecedent` role already visible in mature Lovejoy's `Chinese Origin of a Romanticism` and later JHI China-Europe work.

Thus the Dictionary contains both:

`NON-WESTERN INTERNAL OBJECT`

and

`NON-WESTERN OBJECT AS FUNCTION OF WESTERN HISTORY`.

Those should remain separately coded in the execution corpus.

---

## III. 2006 JHI-board retrospective: the old Dictionary's Western center is actor-level self-description

A 2006 description of the digitized Dictionary, issued on behalf of the Directors and Board of Editors of JHI and preserved by Clio-online, is unusually candid.

It describes the 1973–74 Dictionary as a culminating product of a tradition energized by the fight against fascism and committed to disinterested scholarship and free expression.

More importantly, it says the articles return readers to a period when scholars could write with confident authority about central ideas in the **Western tradition**, and explicitly adds that **non-Western traditions make only a marginal appearance**, despite such entries as `China in Western Thought and Culture` and `Islamic Conceptions of Intellectual Life`.

Source:
https://www.clio-online.de/webresource/id/webresource-15647?language=en

This is methodologically valuable because it prevents anachronistic coding of every Asian/Islamic entry as evidence that the 1973 Dictionary had already become a globally symmetrical canon.

The later JHI governance itself distinguishes:

`WORLD-WIDE CONTRIBUTORS / SOME NON-WESTERN ENTRIES`
from
`REFERENCE ARCHITECTURE CENTERED ON WESTERN TRADITION`.

---

## IV. 2004 *New Dictionary*: explicit recoding as global/cross-cultural

Google Books and the 2004 *New Dictionary* reader's guide describe the original Dictionary as a landmark concerned **primarily with European thought** and advertise the new six-volume work as extending the project across:

- Africa;
- Asia;
- Europe;
- Latin America;
- North America;
- the Middle East.

The contrast is visible at entry level.

The 2004 guide includes explicit items such as:

- `Africa, Idea of`;
- `African-American Ideas`;
- `Aesthetics: Africa`;
- `Arts: Africa`;
- `Capitalism: Africa`;
- `Colonialism: Africa`;
- `Humanism: Africa`;
- `Philosophies: African`;
- `Communication of Ideas: Africa and Its Influence`;
- `Communication of Ideas: Asia and Its Influence`;
- `Communication of Ideas: Middle East and Abroad`;
- `Causation in East Asian and Southeast Asian Philosophy`;
- `Chinese Thought`;
- `Education: India`;
- `Education: Islamic Education`;
- `Japanese Philosophy, Japanese Thought`;
- `Law, Islamic`;
- `Mysticism: Chinese Mysticism`;
- `Mysticism: Islamic Mysticism`;
- `Pre-Columbian Civilization`;
- `Westernization: Africa / Middle East / Southeast Asia`;
- `World Systems Theory, Latin America`.

The difference is not merely more geographical names. The ontology of the reference work changes from occasional non-Western entries inside a predominantly Western set of pivotal ideas to a design in which **regions, communication routes, colonialism, globalization, area-specific philosophies and cross-cultural categories are themselves canonical organizing objects**.

## V. A strong longitudinal asymmetry: infrastructure globalized before reference canon

By the time the old Dictionary appeared, the wider Wiener/JHI ecology already included:

- Chinese Thought committees and area-studies networks;
- the International Society for the History of Ideas;
- Wiener–Nakamura East-West Center collaboration;
- Hajime Nakamura as JHI Corresponding Editor;
- Leopoldo Zea and a Latin-American history-of-ideas tradition linked to JHI/Society;
- Arthur F. Wright on the JHI editorial board;
- Islamic-philosophy specialists contributing to JHI and the Dictionary.

Yet the 2006 JHI-board retrospective still describes the Dictionary's non-Western coverage as marginal.

Therefore the developmental sequence cannot be reduced to:

`MORE INTERNATIONAL PEOPLE`
→ `GLOBAL CANON`.

A better model is:

`NETWORK INTERNATIONALIZATION`
→ `SELECTIVE NON-WESTERN ENTRY / TRANSMISSION STUDY`
→ `WESTERN-CENTERED REFERENCE CANON PERSISTS`
→ `LATER EXPLICIT GLOBAL RECODING`.

This is likely to matter for the proposed Lovejoy/JHI article because the same type of mismatch appears much earlier in the 1935 primitivism volume:

`non-Western evidence present`
+
`main historiographical address still Occidental/Western`.

The 1935 and 1973 cases may therefore be two different institutional moments of the same general problem: **inclusion does not settle jurisdictional symmetry**.

## VI. Relation to Lovejoy's Asian career

The 1973 Dictionary also creates a striking long-range bookend for Lovejoy's 1890s Buddhist work.

### Lovejoy c.1898

Buddhism is an internally reconstructed technical system. The scholarly operation is philological/classificatory/source-critical.

### Wiener/Nakamura 1960s–73

Buddhism becomes a direct reference entry authored by Hajime Nakamura, after a transpacific research infrastructure in which Buddhism had also served as a comparative moving tradition across India, China, Tibet and Japan.

This should **not** be narrated as a direct Lovejoy→Nakamura inheritance.

The stronger field-history question is:

> **How did a body of material that Lovejoy encountered inside late-nineteenth-century Orientalist scholarship become, by the second-generation JHI ecology, both a comparative vehicle for cultural intellectual history and a named canonical entry in the history-of-ideas reference system?**

The answer will require keeping separate:

- object role;
- contributor/translator/editor network;
- institutional infrastructure;
- reference-canon position.

---

## VII. Africa as a bounded negative in the 1973 original

The first broad title-level sweep of the original Dictionary has not yet recovered an Africa-centered entry comparable to `Buddhism`, `Causation in Islamic Thought`, or the China/Oriental entries.

This should currently be coded:

`AFRICA — DIRECT 1973 PIVOTAL-ENTRY PRESENCE = NOT YET RECOVERED`.

Do not promote this to `Africa absent` until all four volume contents and index have been exhaustively searched.

The 2004 New Dictionary's explicit proliferation of Africa-specific entries makes this bounded negative especially worth checking systematically.

---

## VIII. Recommended reference-canon matrix

For every regional/non-Western item, code:

1. **entry title**;
2. **volume/pages**;
3. **author institutional location**;
4. **role**:
   - R1 internal conceptual history;
   - R2 source/antecedent for West;
   - R3 comparative co-case;
   - R4 transmission/diffusion;
   - R5 recipient/reaction/transformation;
   - R6 region as historiographical field;
5. **canonical status**:
   - independent headword;
   - subsection inside general headword;
   - bibliography/example only;
6. **network relation to JHI / International Society / ACLS area committees**;
7. **1973→2004 continuity/reclassification**.

This makes the Dictionary useful as a reproducible `reference-canon layer`, rather than another source of anecdotal titles.
