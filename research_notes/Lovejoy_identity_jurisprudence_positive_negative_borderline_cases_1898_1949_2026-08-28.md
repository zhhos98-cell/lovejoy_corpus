# Lovejoy — identity jurisprudence: positive, negative, and borderline judgments, 1898–1949

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: **CANONICAL CASE MATRIX / IDENTITY-WARRANT BRANCH / ANALYST FORMALIZATION ONLY**

## Governing question

Rather than asking abstractly whether Lovejoy supplied a definition of the `same idea`, this note reconstructs his actual **judgments of identity, nonidentity, partial identity, and uncertain genealogy**.

The emerging result is that Lovejoy's practice is not well represented by a single binary relation:

`A = B` or `A ≠ B`.

A better analyst notation is scale- and relation-indexed:

`A =ₓ B`

where `x` may be:

- lexical/semantic;
- propositional/doctrinal;
- inferential-profile;
- functional/systemic;
- common-core/conceptual;
- configurational/complex;
- historical/genetic.

**This notation is ours, not Lovejoy's.** Its purpose is to prevent the historian from allowing one kind of sameness to silently settle another.

The strongest current result is:

> **Lovejoy repeatedly permits `different at one scale, same at another`, and `same in content, uncertain in genealogy`. His identity judgments are therefore typed and scale-relative in practice, even though he never formalizes them as such.**

# II. 1898 already shows scale-relative identity

## A. Negative case: semantic proximity is not identity

Notebook 004 takes up Henry Clarke Warren's characterization of one nidāna relation as `identity`. Lovejoy checks this against canonical classifications and rejects the identification.

The logic of the judgment is:

`adjacent / semantically close`
≠ `same classificatory item`.

The identity predicate is therefore constrained by the relevant classification scale.

## B. Positive case: lexical difference can coexist with systemic equivalence

Elsewhere the notebook treats `upādhi`, `āsava`, and `saṁyojana` as having somewhat different derivations/connotations while allowing them, at the scale of Buddhist system-function, to converge on essentially the same fundamental idea of attachment to objects of physical experience.

Thus:

`lexically/genealogically different`
+
`functionally/systemically same`.

This is the earliest clear control against a binary identity model.

Do not publish the p.56 wording verbatim before direct image verification; the current manuscript reading is medium-confidence.

---

# III. 1906 — identity can be argumentative rather than nominal

The two 1906 Kant papers are useful early positive controls.

Lovejoy argues that Kant's advertised historical antithesis between `criticism` and predecessor `dogmatism` breaks down because the allegedly novel critical procedure repeats structures already found in Wolff and others. Wilson summarizes `On Kant's Reply to Hume` as arguing that Kant's critical view of causality was `essentially the same` as that of dogmatic predecessors, with likeness located in philosophical methodology, ultimate criteria of truth, and special arguments.

This suggests a practical identity criterion:

`different actor label / self-positioning`
+
`substantially same argumentative or methodological structure`
→ historical claim of continuity/sameness at the method level.

The exact primary wording should be controlled before publication-grade use.

---

# IV. 1923 — a direct negative identity rule: same term does not make one doctrine

`The Supposed Primitivism of Rousseau's Discourse on Inequality` opens by treating the received interpretation as a historical error produced partly by failure to register ambiguity.

Lovejoy states that `state of nature` has at least three distinguishable senses.

The operative rule is:

`same recurrent phrase`
↛ `one stable idea/doctrine`.

This is a direct precursor to the semasiological control made explicit in 1941.

The case is important because it demonstrates that Lovejoy's long-range method can produce **nonidentity** rather than continuity when usage requires it.

---

# V. 1924 — macro-nonidentity with local overlap

`On the Discrimination of Romanticisms` gives a particularly clear scale-relative judgment.

## Macro scale

The same scholarly name attached to German, English, French and other episodes is not evidence that they are identical in essentials. A common denominator, if present, must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

Therefore:

`same historiographical label`
↛ `same historical movement/complex`.

## Component scale

At the same time, two Romanticisms may genuinely share a significant presupposition/impulse while another pair shares a different one.

Therefore:

`complex A ≠ complex B`
can coexist with
`component x in A = component x in B`.

The relation of sameness is scale-indexed.

## Composition scale

Shared or co-present unit-ideas are mostly not bound by logical necessity; Lovejoy explicitly invokes alogical associative processes.

Therefore:

`same component`
↛ `same mode of combination`
↛ `same complex`.

---

# VI. 1932 — Lovejoy can reaggregate to a higher-scale positive identity

`The Parallel of Deism and Classicism` is the strongest counterexample to an invariant atomizing method.

Lovejoy starts by distinguishing elements of the Enlightenment rationalist idea-complex but makes the explanatory target the complex's systematic unity. He then argues that deism applies this complex to religion while neo-classical aesthetic theory applies substantially the same kind of preconceptions to aesthetics.

This is a **pattern identity** judgment:

`different domain/application`
+
`recurring organized set of preconceptions`
→ `same or homologous idea-complex at a higher scale`.

The important point is not that every component is token-identical in every application. The claim rests on a sufficiently stable configuration/pattern.

This is precisely why `unit-idea` cannot be treated as Lovejoy's sole explanatory scale.

---

# VII. 1933 — content identity is separated from genealogy

In `Monboddo and Rousseau`, Lovejoy identifies a cluster of six connected theses in both writers. He then asks why the similarity exists.

The competing historical explanations include:

- independent/spontaneous occurrence;
- direct influence;
- common ancient sources;
- common exposure to contemporary comparative anatomy and travel/ethnographic evidence.

Lovejoy explicitly leaves the causal question uncertain.

Thus:

`same/sufficiently similar thought-content`
≠ `direct influence`
≠ `priority/direction`
≠ `common-source derivation`.

This is a mature, explicit instance of the same relation-upgrade problem already visible in the 1898 Orientalist field.

No direct causal continuity from 1898 to 1933 is established by this recurrence alone.

---

# VIII. 1936 — proposition + inferential profile as a practical identity technology

In Lecture I of *The Great Chain of Being*, Lovejoy narrows the operative units in the book to especially definite and explicit items: a specific proposition/principle, together with propositions treated as corollaries.

This implies a practical recognition technology:

`propositional content`
+
`inferential/corollary profile`
→ candidate cross-context identity.

This is stronger than word identity and weaker than total contextual identity.

It also explains why Lovejoy can recognize an idea despite changes in terminology or compound.

But the threshold remains unspecified. The text does not provide a rule for how much change in wording, corollaries, function, or implication is compatible with sameness.

# IX. 1941 — three simultaneous identity judgments

## 1. `Infinite`: partial identity through a minimal common core

Lovejoy distinguishes at least five senses/applications of `infinite`, then says they share an historically important negative element: not-limited/not-completed.

This is neither full identity nor sheer equivocation.

It is:

`different positive senses`
+
`same minimal conceptual core`.

This shows that identity can be established at a deliberately thinner scale of abstraction.

## 2. `Romanticism`: reject one essence, retain period/movement scales

Lovejoy rejects the idea that the 1780s–90s new ideas constituted one harmoniously unified Romantic idea. He calls them heterogeneous, logically independent, and sometimes antithetic.

Yet he still allows talk of a Romantic **period** and several Romantic **movements**.

Thus:

`no single idea/systematic unity`
≠ `no historically meaningful larger-scale object`.

The correct scale depends on the question.

## 3. Basic/germinal ideas: trans-context recognition after semantic control

Lovejoy's first task for the historian is to discriminate basic/germinal ideas and identify them so they can be recognized across differing contexts, labels and phrasings.

But this instruction follows semasiological control of recurrent words.

Therefore the sequence is:

`word-use discrimination`
→ `candidate idea individuation`
→ `cross-context recognition`
→ `separate testing of logical / psychological / historical-genetic relations`.

Identity does not settle genealogy or historical operation.

---

# X. 1944 — identity is a historical working hypothesis, not an a priori essence

In the Spitzer reply, Lovejoy defends two working hypotheses: discriminable ideas can recur in diverse writers/schools/periods, and individual thought can consist of compounds of discriminable ideas.

Most importantly, he says their truth and range are questions of historical fact to be tested by textual examination.

This shifts the strongest criticism.

The problem is not best formulated as:

`Lovejoy assumes eternal idea-atoms.`

It is:

> **Lovejoy treats recurrence as empirically testable but never fully specifies the threshold by which textual variation becomes identity, modification, analogy, or nonidentity.**

---

# XI. 1948–1949 — identity through difference, plus recurrence without persistence of the whole

The 1948 preface permits long-range recognition of identity against changed accompaniments and emphasizes that Lovejoy's general methodological conceptions grew out of special inquiries rather than simply preceding them.

The 1949 disclaimer clarifies that whole idea-complexes seldom persist as originally constituted, because later actors select, recombine, and add to their components. Individual components can display persistence or recurrence.

This makes two relations particularly important:

`persistence` ≠ `recurrence`.

- persistence may involve historical continuity through modification;
- recurrence can be independent and need not imply a transmission chain.

The distinction should be explicitly coded in future long-range narratives.

# XII. Reconstructed identity decision tree — analyst model, not actor formalism

The case law supports the following provisional reconstruction.

## Step 1 — fix the scale

What is the candidate object?

- word/phrase;
- proposition/principle;
- conceptual core;
- function in a system;
- configuration/idea-complex;
- movement/period;
- genetic lineage.

Never ask `same?` before specifying this.

## Step 2 — identify candidate invariants

Depending on scale, invariants can include:

- lexical usage;
- explicit propositional content;
- corollaries / inferential role;
- minimal conceptual core;
- system-level function;
- organized pattern of preconceptions;
- documented historical carrier/transmission.

## Step 3 — register permitted variation

Possible variants include:

- wording/label;
- positive elaboration;
- affective association;
- historical function;
- co-components;
- domain of application;
- motive;
- mode of transmission.

Variation at one level does not automatically destroy identity at another.

## Step 4 — type the conclusion

Do not use bare `same idea` if the evidence supports only one of:

- `same lexical item`;
- `same proposition`;
- `same conceptual core`;
- `same systemic function`;
- `same configurational pattern`;
- `same historical lineage`;
- `independent recurrence of same/similar content`.

## Step 5 — keep genealogy separate

Even strong content identity requires additional historical evidence for:

- influence;
- borrowing;
- priority;
- direction;
- common source;
- continuous persistence.

This is the strongest recurrence from 1898 to mature Lovejoy currently visible.

# XIII. The real epistemic vulnerability

The case matrix shows that Lovejoy's practice is more discriminating than the caricature of fixed idea-atoms suggests. But the matrix does not dissolve the problem.

The strongest vulnerability is now much more precise:

> **How does the historian choose the abstraction scale at which two historically different formulations count as the same, and what evidence constrains that choice rather than allowing the analyst to select whatever invariant yields the desired continuity?**

This combines the `scale` problem with the `identity threshold` problem.

A historian can make A and B identical by thinning the invariant from a rich proposition to a minimal conceptual core. Conversely, one can make them different by thickening the relevant inferential, pragmatic, or contextual profile.

Lovejoy has actual controls against arbitrary identification—semantic discrimination, proposition specificity, corollary profiles, textual verification, and separate genetic inquiry—but no single explicit rule governs the choice of granularity.

That is a stronger Skinner-facing question than `Did context matter?`.

---

# XIV. Consequence for the governing model

The current branch model should now be stated as:

> **problem-dependent scale selection → typed relation → calibrated warrant → identity/genealogy conclusion**

or compactly:

> **scale → relation → warrant**.

The portable feature, if there is one, is not decomposition alone and not formal logic alone. It is the recurrent discipline of determining:

1. what object/scale is under judgment;
2. what relation is actually claimed;
3. what evidence can license that relation;
4. what additional proof is required to upgrade it.

This is compatible with Lovejoy's 1948 retrospective self-description that his general conceptions grew out of special-topic inquiries.
