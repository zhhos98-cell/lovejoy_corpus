## 1. Garbe 1892 makes the proof problem explicit before the 1894 source Lovejoy copied

Richard Garbe, _Aniruddha's Commentary and the Original Parts of Vedântin Mahâdeva's Commentary on the Sâṃkhya Sûtras, translated with an Introduction on the Age and Origin of the Sâṃkhya System_ (Calcutta: Baptist Mission Press, 1892), opens by making the Sāṃkhya/Buddhism relation the starting point for dating and reconstructing the Sāṃkhya system.

The bibliographic object is secure in Google Books / Bibliotheca Indica records (1892, 320 pp.; Bibliotheca Indica 131 / no. 782). The full introduction is also text-accessible in a public-domain scan mirror.

### The proof threshold

Garbe explicitly names Max Müller and Oldenberg as the two scholars who had failed to find `definite similarities` between Sāṃkhya and Buddhism. He disagrees with their negative conclusion, but credits them with showing that the traditional dependence claim had not been sufficiently proved.

He then rejects earlier arguments of too general a kind. Shared atheism, plurality of souls, liberation, and generic replacement of ritual by knowledge/meditation are either widespread in India, imperfect correspondences, or too general to establish dependence.

This is important because Garbe is not merely collecting parallels. He is explicitly ranking **kinds of resemblance by historical diagnostic value**.

### `solid ground`: extant documents rather than a pliable lost proto-system

Garbe says that an investigator who does not want to disappear into conjecture must renounce an indefinitely malleable hypothetical `original` Sāṃkhya. In his formulation, to keep `solid ground under our feet`, one must compare the Sāṃkhya documents actually extant with original Buddhist records (or Oldenberg's reconstruction of them).

That rule has two consequences:

1. the comparison must be document-controlled rather than rescued by a hypothetical lost system;
2. the historical inference must be made from diagnostic features of surviving evidence.

### Why details may count more than general likeness

Garbe then makes the key move. Since a borrower may alter fundamental conceptions, a historical connection may betray itself chiefly in **details**. He singles out `external peculiarities` involuntarily retained in expression as especially probative. He quotes Müller's view that such similarities could decide direction — Buddha from Kapila or Kapila from Buddha — and says his own series of correspondences is intended to satisfy Müller's demand for `definite similarities`.

The resulting proof sequence is explicit:

`resemblance` → ask whether it is diagnostic rather than generic → use documentary/formal detail → infer connection → separately infer direction.

### Garbe's first test case: classificatory form as a historical trace

Garbe's first example is not a shared metaphysical proposition. It is a formal habit: the predilection for dry numerical classifications in Buddhist sermons and Sāṃkhya texts. He argues that the agreement cannot be explained merely by a general Indian appetite for systematization; it indicates `the continuity of a peculiar scholastic method`. He then separately asks who transmitted this method to whom and answers in favor of Kapila.

Later examples pursue the same evidentiary logic: near-literal formulaic agreement (`not mine / not I / not my Self`) and obscure inherited metaphors whose original sense has become opaque in Sāṃkhya but remains intelligible from Buddhist usage.

This is precisely a theory of how **unintended formal residue can carry genealogy**.

## 2. Garbe 1894 explicitly carries the 1892 programme forward

The 1894 book directly used by Lovejoy does not start from zero. Its historical introduction states that Garbe had already treated the problem elsewhere, in the introduction to his translation of the _Sāṃkhya-tattva-kaumudī_, and claims that by assembling `eine ganze Reihe von Uebereinstimmungen` he had proved the indigenous tradition that the Sāṃkhya system was older than Buddha and served as a principal source for Buddhist doctrine.

Thus the 1894 `Method` passage copied by Lovejoy belongs to the same evidentiary project as the 1892 introduction.

At printed pp.150–151 Garbe says that orthodox Indian systems show extraordinarily close agreement in the terminology, definitions, treatment of details, and examples used in inference. He explains this by adoption of the Vaiśeṣika–Nyāya logical apparatus. Therefore, when the detailed theory of inference occurs in Sāṃkhya writings, `so erkennen wir hier ein fremdes Element` — an alien element.

The diagnostic object is again not generic resemblance but **high-density formal agreement in terminology, definitions, details, and examples**.

Notebook 004 reproduces exactly this passage and its sequence: close agreement in terminology / definitions / examples → adoption from Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika → similar theory in Sāṃkhya writings → `we must recognize an alien element`, followed by the three `pramāṇa`s.

Therefore the secure transmission chain is:

> Müller / Oldenberg proof objection → Garbe 1892 explicit genealogy protocol → Garbe 1894 reapplication/generalization of the same diagnostic logic → Lovejoy 004 direct uptake from Garbe 1894.

What is **not** yet secure is that Lovejoy himself read the 1892 English introduction. No direct 004 citation to the 1892 volume has been located. The 1892 text should therefore be treated as the genealogy of the rule in Garbe, not yet as a direct Lovejoy source.

## 3. The sharpest Garbe → Lovejoy transformation is scope control

The strongest direct comparison is not `Garbe taught Lovejoy borrowing`. It is that Lovejoy copies a Garbean rule for diagnosing an `alien element`, then uses a stricter scale distinction when evaluating early evidence for Sāṃkhya.

In the Brahmajāla discussion, 004 says of one passage:

> `I am at a loss to understand how the passage can be considered good evidence [that] the Sankhya system existed as a whole, just as we find it in the many centuries later.`

The same page then marks an explicit authorial intervention: `To R.D.'s remark it should be added ...`, after which Lovejoy asks what feature would be more genuinely diagnostic of Sāṃkhya and isolates the separation of Self and World / thorough-going dualism, with a further Majjhima-Nikāya parallel.

This gives a historically bounded transformation:

- Garbe 1894: sufficiently dense formal agreement can identify an **alien element** inside a text/system;
- Lovejoy 004: a passage may be diagnostic of a **doctrine or element** without proving that a later **system existed as a whole**.

That is not simply skepticism. It is a change in the **unit of historical inference**.

A particularly useful control is that Garbe 1894 himself already warns that the Brahmajāla's sixty-two enumerated schools cannot all have existed historically: the catalogue systematically exhausts theoretically possible opinions and presents them as de facto schools. Yet Garbe still identifies some positions as immediately recognizable Sāṃkhya `Hauptlehren` and uses them as evidence for antiquity. Lovejoy's notebook tightens this further by refusing to let a doctrinal identification establish whole-system existence.

Current safe formulation:

> Lovejoy did not simply inherit Garbe's genealogical conclusion. He directly inherited one of Garbe's diagnostic rules for identifying borrowed material and then restricted what such evidence could establish at the level of a whole philosophical system.

This is an analytical synthesis, not a priority claim.

## 4. Why this materially changes the first-order article

This makes `The Method of the Sankhya — Garbe p.150` more than a source note.

Notebook 004 physically opens its substantive work with a problem of **historical attribution from formal correspondence**. Its later Sāṃkhya/Buddhism pages then ask a related but stricter question: what level of historical entity has actually been proved — a phrase, a doctrine, an element, an arrangement, or a system?

The core nineteenth-century problem can therefore be stated without importing a modern method vocabulary:

> When two traditions resembled one another, what exactly did the resemblance prove, and how far could a scholar move from resemblance to borrowing, from borrowing to direction, and from a borrowed doctrine to a genealogy of systems?

The 004 evidence now supports a chain of progressively differentiated proof questions rather than a generic story about `comparison`.

## 5. Lanman: the finding aid now gives concrete transmission targets

The existing repo already knew the April 1896 public lecture and Lovejoy's documented Lanman training. The new step is archival targeting.

Harvard's current finding aid for the **Papers of Charles Rockwell Lanman, 1825–1941**, call no. **HUG 4510.xx**, explicitly states that the professional papers contain:

- research and teaching records;
- articles, manuscripts, notes, and lectures from Harvard / Yale / Germany;
- **course materials and records of Lanman's students at Harvard**;
- correspondence with students and colleagues;
- Harvard Oriental Series correspondence, mailing lists, proofs, and notices.

The collection has 187 components and no digital content. It therefore cannot be closed by further ordinary web searching.

### Highest-value specific series / routes

**A. Engagement calendars, 1896–1936 — HUG 4510.20**

The series begins exactly in 1896. The 1896 calendar is the cheapest possible day-level control for Lanman's April 29 Buddhism lecture and for teaching / meetings around Lovejoy's Harvard period. It will not prove Lovejoy's attendance by itself, but it could identify preparatory teaching, named appointments, conference events, or student meetings.

**B. Teaching materials + records of Harvard students — within HUG 4510.xx**

This is the highest-value transmission target. The finding aid explicitly says such material exists, but the web index does not expose a sufficiently granular box/folder title for the 1895–98 Sanskrit/Buddhist courses. An archivist request should ask specifically for course materials, student lists/records, exercises, lecture notes, and class records for 1895–96, 1896–97, and 1897–98.

**C. Harvard Oriental Series, 1891–1917**

Because Warren's _Buddhism in Translations_ (1896) is a direct 004 source and Lanman/Warren institutional product, this series may document the local production and circulation of the exact textual infrastructure Lovejoy used.

**D. History of Religions Club records, 1892–1952 — HUD 3454.xx**

The Lanman finding aid itself identifies these as related Harvard Archives material. This is a separate institutional route worth checking for programs, minutes, speakers, members, or lecture records overlapping Lovejoy's years.

### Evidentiary ceiling

At present we can say:

- Lovejoy studied Sanskrit and Buddhist texts with Lanman;
- Lanman publicly taught the historical relation of Buddhism to Sāṃkhya/Vedānta in April 1896;
- 004 uses a Harvard/Lanman-linked Warren volume heavily;
- the Lanman papers contain the kinds of course/student records that could directly test transmission.

We still cannot say:

- Lovejoy attended the April 29 public lecture;
- a surviving Lanman lecture note was the source of any particular 004 passage;
- Garbe 1892 was assigned or read under Lanman;
- Warren reached Lovejoy through a specific classroom transaction rather than ordinary reading.

Those are the next archival questions, not established facts.
