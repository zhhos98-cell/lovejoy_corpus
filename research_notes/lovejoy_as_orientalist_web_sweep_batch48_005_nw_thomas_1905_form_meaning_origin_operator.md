# Batch 48 — Lovejoy 005 only: N. W. Thomas as an independent control for the `form → mechanism → origin` operator

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced
Scope lock: **MS38_004_001_061_005 only**.

## Research move

Batch 28 already established Northcote Whitridge Thomas as (1) an eyewitness to Léon Marillier’s student-work seminar pedagogy and (2) a later 1911 synthesizer of `Taboo`, `Sacrifice`, `Magic`, `Cannibalism`, `Animism`, and `Fetishism`. The present batch does **not** repeat that result.

The new delta is methodological and earlier. Thomas’s 1905 paper `The Religious Ideas of the Arunta` shows a former Marillier student actually **performing** the same source-critical operation that is visible in Lovejoy 005: conflicting ethnographic reports are not resolved by choosing one authority or by fitting them into a ready-made evolutionary sequence. Thomas instead tests chronology, missionary contamination, translation, diffusion, geographical distribution, and internal change as rival historical explanations. Six years later, in the 1911 `Animal Worship` article, he formulates an unusually explicit distinction between **outward form**, **inward meaning**, and the **cause that first produced a practice**.

Read together with his 1911 `Cannibalism`, `Fetishism`, `Taboo`, `Sacrifice`, and `Animism` articles, these sources supply an independent former-student control for an analytical grammar reconstructed from 005:

> **surface category / outward form → operative meaning or causal bearer → historical genesis**
>
> with contamination, borrowing, syncretism, and negative cases tested before a developmental claim is licensed.

This is stronger than a thematic resemblance. It identifies a reproducible research operation across different people and different bodies of evidence.

Important limit: Thomas is a control, not a source for Lovejoy. No claim is made that Lovejoy read the 1905 or 1911 texts, and not every feature of Thomas’s later method can be assigned exclusively to Marillier.

## 1. Thomas 1905: source criticism as an active research procedure

Primary source:

N. W. Thomas, `The Religious Ideas of the Arunta`, *Folk-Lore* 16 (1905), 428–433.

Thomas begins from a concrete documentary contradiction. Spencer and Gillen’s recent description of the Arunta leaves little room for mythology, gods, or a conventional future life, whereas the earlier Horn Expedition and missionary reports describe a sky-being and an eschatology in which a soul survives after death.

Rather than decide the issue from printed authorities alone, Thomas writes directly to Carl Strehlow at Hermannsburg and prints material supplied in response. The procedure is therefore itself documentary:

> conflicting published reports → new correspondence with a linguistically competent field observer → comparison of terminology and eschatology → rival historical explanations.

This is not merely a later encyclopedic taxonomy. It is a worked research case.

## 2. Contamination is tested chronologically, not invoked generically

Thomas explicitly considers the possibility that the reported Arunta theology is missionary contamination. His argument is chronological:

- missionaries had arrived only about ten years before the earlier reports;
- substantially similar beliefs were recorded again roughly twenty years later;
- the material did not show the rapid transformation one would expect if a newly imported Christian system had crystallized in the interval.

Thomas also isolates **translation** as a distinct contamination mechanism. A phrase rendered as `immortal virgins`, he suggests, may reflect an English/German translation choice rather than either an indigenous category or a Christian borrowing; the original term might have meant only an unmarried woman.

This distinction matters for 005. Lovejoy’s early future-life pages repeatedly ask whether apparently moralized eschatology is indigenous, produced by missionary contact, or introduced by diffusion; he also insists on the date and provenance of ethnographic testimony. In PDF p.19, for example, an 1819-era Pawnee statement is treated as suspect because missionary influence may already have altered the report. In pp.20 and 28, imported moralized ideas can coexist with older naturalistic/non-moral conceptions.

The shared operator is therefore not simply `beware missionary influence`. It is:

> **name a contamination mechanism → establish a chronology → compare independent/serial reports → decide how much of the recorded category can safely be treated as historical evidence.**

## 3. Thomas states three rival historical hypotheses and assigns burdens of proof

After arguing that the disputed beliefs are substantially indigenous, Thomas refuses to move directly to an origin story. He formulates exactly three alternatives:

1. Christian influence;
2. borrowing from neighbouring peoples;
3. internal evolution in one portion of the Arunta, replacing an earlier non-theistic/non-eschatological complex.

His treatment of diffusion is especially revealing. The burden of proof lies with the person proposing borrowing. Similarity in general is insufficient: correspondences of **names and incidents must be shown in detail** before even a prima facie case exists. If diffusion is alleged, one should also be able to show advance from a proposed centre and define the present boundary between the competing belief-complexes.

Thomas then declines to sketch an attractive evolutionary sequence until the recalcitrant facts have first been explained.

This provides an unusually close independent control for Lovejoy 005 pp.16–30. Lovejoy likewise separates:

- moral desert from manner of death / ritual qualification / social position;
- local development from missionary or Hindu/Buddhist diffusion;
- present coexistence from historical priority;
- recurrence of an image or destination from evidence of a single origin.

The important commonality is **evidentiary sequencing**. Developmental history is not banned; it is postponed until provenance, contamination, and alternative mechanisms have been tested.

## 4. Thomas 1911 `Animal Worship`: the operator becomes explicit

The 1911 Britannica article `Animal Worship` gives the clearest formal statement yet recovered from a former Marillier student.

Thomas opens by calling `animal worship` an **ill-defined term** that groups highly heterogeneous reports. He then says animal cults can be classified in two distinct ways:

- by **outward form**;
- by **inward meaning**, which can itself undergo transformation.

He immediately adds a third historical level. The present meaning of a cult is **not necessarily identical with the cause that first produced the deification**. He lists multiple possible meanings and notes that outward forms may themselves change through processes including syncretism. Elsewhere in the same article, association between a deity and an animal is explicitly refused as sufficient proof that the deity was originally theriomorphic; and in the Gilyak bear festival he leaves historical priority between a funerary interpretation and a hunting-cult interpretation as an open question.

This gives a compact three-level operator:

> **FORM** — what is visibly done / what class label the observer supplies
>
> **MEANING / MECHANISM** — what relation currently makes the act efficacious or intelligible
>
> **ORIGIN** — how that form-mechanism combination came historically to exist

The three levels can diverge because meaning changes, forms persist, and syncretism combines previously distinct elements.

## 5. This three-level distinction is already structurally present throughout 005

The 005 notebook repeatedly enforces the same separation without stating it as a single abstract formula.

### Present form is not sufficient for present mechanism

- PDF p.50: eating a human sacrificial victim does not by itself make the act mystical communion.
- p.70: ritualized killing of a rain-maker may be punishment for failed performance rather than sacrifice.
- p.76: the same visible human sacrifice may be funerary provision, protective magic, communication with the dead, or another mechanism.
- p.84: a foundation victim and blood in a building do not automatically indicate mystical union; the material may operate protectively.
- pp.106–107: repetition, public ceremony, solemnity, or fixed rules do not establish sacramental/religious cannibalism.

### Present mechanism is not sufficient for historical origin

- p.65: several motives may become syncretically fused after originally distinct sacrifices have existed separately.
- p.73: funeral destruction of property can acquire a new rationale over time.
- p.97: a present communal/agricultural complex may reorganize older funerary, alimentary, local, or borrowed rites.
- p.106: a customary cannibal practice can survive after its original motive has disappeared.

This is precisely why `outward resemblance` cannot bear the weight of genealogy.

## 6. Thomas 1911 `Cannibalism`: same formal act, heterogeneous psychological classes

Thomas’s `Cannibalism` article is an especially close control because it concerns the exact topic dominating the end of 005.

He says cannibalistic practices may be classified by motive and by ceremonial regulation and then makes the stronger methodological observation that `cannibalism`, psychologically considered, groups customs whose only common feature is the eating of human flesh.

The article then distinguishes hunger/taste, magical acquisition of qualities, protective magic, divine sacrifice/meal, revenge/penal motives, and initiation. In the magical class Thomas uses the familiar causal grammar:

> consume a body / body part → acquire the mental or bodily quality attributed to its former bearer.

He then criticizes Steinmetz’s endo/exo-cannibalism division as often of minor importance and insufficiently delimited, argues that multiplicity of forms and ceremonial rules points toward **multiple origins**, and uses magical foods such as eating a lion’s heart as a counterexample to the claim that magical cannibalism must derive from an earlier ordinary food practice.

This is almost proposition-level parallelism with Lovejoy 005 pp.106–119:

- p.106 lists heterogeneous motives beneath one outward act;
- p.108 treats selected-organ efficacy as sympathetic magic rather than communion;
- p.117 makes sacramental union comparatively exceptional;
- p.119 maps organ to quality: brain/head → intelligence; heart → courage/life; sexual organs → sexual power.

Again, no Thomas→Lovejoy reading is proposed. The value is that a former Marillier student independently arrives at nearly the same **category-breaking operation** around the same Steinmetz problem.

## 7. `Fetishism`: classification by the location of efficacy

Thomas’s 1911 `Fetishism` article provides an even cleaner causal-bearer control. He begins by calling fetishism an ill-defined term covering, among other things:

- an object inhabited by a spirit;
- a charm deriving power from a god/spirit;
- an object considered magically potent in itself.

He then separates a `god-house` from a charm whose efficacy derives from association with a spirit, and notes cases in which a spirit communicates part of its power to objects in which it does not dwell.

Lovejoy 005 pp.88–89 performs the same analytical disaggregation:

- fetish: efficacy depends on an indwelling/present spirit;
- charm/amulet: no spirit need inhabit the object;
- an object can instead possess efficacy intrinsic to the thing.

Thus the classificatory variable is no longer `sacred object` or `fetish` as an ethnographic label. It is **where causal efficacy is located and how it is transmitted**:

> indwelling agent / communicated power / intrinsic efficacy.

This is one of the strongest bridges from notebook classification to the later 1906 problem of impersonal, transmissible, quasi-mechanical efficacy.

## 8. `Taboo`: classification by sanction and causal mode

Thomas’s `Taboo` article, already used in Batch 28, becomes more useful when placed inside this operator framework.

Thomas insists that taboo is not intrinsically moral and separates:

- direct/natural mana;
- communicated/indirect mana;
- automatic sanction without volition;
- animistic sanction caused by a god, dead person, or other spirit;
- social sanction imposed by the group to avert danger.

The important point is not merely that these categories resemble 005. It is that the same observed prohibition is reclassified according to the **causal bearer of the sanction**.

That is exactly the early 005 future-life move: exclusion, punishment, or a separate fate after death is not sufficient evidence of moral retribution. Lovejoy asks whether the determinant is taboo, clan solidarity, ritual status, mode of death, imported ethics, or genuine desert.

The continuity between early eschatology and later sacrifice therefore becomes clearer: the notebook repeatedly asks **what variable actually produces the observed differential outcome?**

## 9. `Animism`: determinant decomposition and refusal of global origin claims

Thomas’s `Animism` article independently reproduces two more operations central to 005.

First, in eschatology he says the post-mortem lot may depend on rank, sex, mode of death, burial, funeral ritual, and other determinants. This is closely aligned with the 005 distinction between differentiated afterlives and genuinely moral retribution.

Second, Thomas refuses to infer a universal origin of religion from one regional sequence. Ancestor worship may be important without being demonstrably prior; Australia cannot simply be generalized to the world; and the available science of religions lacks sufficient data for a secure original form. Animistic origin is therefore not proven.

This is the same evidentiary issue sharpened in batches 45–47 around Steinmetz: disciplined comparison does not automatically license a universal primitive reconstruction.

## 10. `Sacrifice`: permanence of form does not imply permanence of meaning

Thomas’s `Sacrifice` article makes the historical rule explicit. After summarizing Marillier’s magical-blood theory and Hubert/Mauss’s refusal to derive all sacrifices from one primitive form, Thomas states that primitive sacrificial forms are too poorly known to determine a single origin with confidence. Most importantly, even where ritual form persists, **permanence of form does not imply permanence of meaning**.

This sentence-level rule gives a retrospective formal vocabulary for what 005 repeatedly does empirically:

- old funeral practices acquire new rationales;
- food sacrifice can acquire propitiatory or mystical interpretations;
- magical and sacramental elements become syncretic;
- agricultural rites can reorganize older materials;
- visible continuity is compatible with causal discontinuity.

The methodological inheritance, if we use that term at all, therefore lies less in any settled theory of sacrifice than in a discipline of **not letting morphology dictate genealogy**.

## 11. Thomas changes the student-comparison architecture

Until now, the strongest student comparison has been Söderblom because documentary transfer is explicit: Marillier supplied notes, Söderblom marks material taken from them, and Söderblom says he adopted different divisions/a somewhat different point of view.

Thomas adds a different kind of evidence.

### Söderblom

What he proves:

- teacher paper actually moved;
- a student could reorganize supplied material;
- the result was not doctrinal copying.

### Thomas

What he proves:

- the seminar pedagogy was research-by-pupils under direction;
- a former student later **performs** close source criticism in 1905;
- he later formalizes form/meaning/origin distinctions across the same problem domains as 005.

### Lovejoy

What 005 proves:

- the same workshop year/problems generated an extensive working notebook;
- the notebook repeatedly breaks broad ethnographic labels by mechanism, determinant, and historical layer;
- negative cases are used to withdraw diagnostic force from visible features;
- one cross-cutting causal family is later abstracted in 1906.

The triangulation therefore no longer depends on saying that all three students accepted the same propositions. The stronger object is a **generative research grammar**.

## 12. Revised historical claim: a school of redivision, not a school doctrine

The evidence does not justify inventing a fixed `Marillier school` with a stable creed. In fact, the recovered material points in the opposite direction.

Marillier’s published work attacks unilateral explanations and external resemblance; Söderblom explicitly uses different divisions; Mauss receives Marillier’s bibliographies/course notes but reclassifies endocannibalism as communion; Lovejoy can move the same transfer mechanism out of communion into sympathetic efficacy; Thomas later says the same formal name can conceal heterogeneous meanings and multiple origins.

What reproduces across these trajectories is not a taxonomy. It is an instruction for **undoing taxonomies when evidence requires it**.

A paper-level formulation:

> **Marillier’s comparative-religion seminar is best understood not as transmitting a stable theory of primitive religion but as training a practice of redivision. Across Söderblom, Thomas, and Lovejoy, outward form, operative meaning, and historical origin are repeatedly pulled apart; contamination and diffusion are tested before genealogical inference; negative cases withdraw diagnostic force from inherited categories; and formally similar rites are reassigned by causal mechanism. The reproducible product of the workshop was therefore less a doctrine than an evidentiary grammar for remaking comparative classes.**

This formulation is stronger than `French sociology influenced Lovejoy`, but still stays within the evidence.

## 13. Consequence for the 1906 endpoint

This deeper Thomas control clarifies what becomes abstract in Lovejoy’s 1906 `Primitive Philosophy`.

005 repeatedly asks where efficacy is located:

- in a personal spirit;
- intrinsically in a thing;
- in blood/body substance;
- in contact or similarity;
- in transferred qualities;
- in a social relation;
- in a bargain/propitiatory relation with an agent.

Thomas’s later `Fetishism` and `Taboo` independently make exactly this causal-bearer distinction explicit. The 1906 Lovejoy paper can therefore be read as stabilizing one mechanism-family — intrinsic / impersonal / transmissible / direct efficacy — that had repeatedly been extracted from ritual classes inside the notebook.

The methodological genealogy is now:

> **paper/source criticism → form/meaning separation → causal-bearer reclassification → historical-layer control → portable causal abstraction.**

This is more precise than saying the notebook simply anticipates `primitive energetics`.

## 14. Evidence limits

1. Thomas’s 1905 and 1911 texts are not demonstrated Lovejoy sources.
2. The 1905 paper was written roughly a decade after Thomas’s Marillier study and reflects Thomas’s wider anthropological networks; it cannot be treated as a pure fossil of seminar instruction.
3. The 1911 articles are later formalizations and must be used as independent controls, not evidence for what Marillier literally said in 1898–99.
4. Similar analytical operations can arise in a broader comparative-religion field; the claim becomes historically stronger only because Thomas is independently documented as Marillier’s student and eyewitness to his pedagogy.
5. `Marillier school` should therefore be avoided unless placed in quotation marks and carefully qualified. `Workshop`, `research grammar`, or `practice of redivision` is safer.
6. No direct relation between Thomas’s 1905 publication and the `Hist. Relig. — Dec. 20, 1905` reuse of Lovejoy 005 is claimed. The contemporaneity is at most contextual.

## 15. Highest-value next primary target

Thomas’s own Marillier-supervised EPHE diploma study is now much more valuable than it looked earlier:

N. W. Thomas, `La survivance du culte totémique des animaux et les rites agraires dans le Pays de Galles`, *Revue de l’histoire des religions* 38 (1898), 295–347.

Why this moves upward in priority:

- it is an actual student research product from Marillier’s training rather than a 1905/1911 later control;
- it was published in 1898, immediately before/at the threshold of Lovejoy’s Paris period;
- its title already places `survivance`, animal cult/totemism, and agrarian rites in one analytical field that overlaps 005;
- if it already separates outward survivals from current function/origin, it would move the `research grammar` claim much closer to the seminar itself.

A complete digital volume exists in BSB/DDB/Google Books; the article pages themselves remain to be recovered and collated.
