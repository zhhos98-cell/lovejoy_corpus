# Lovejoy 1907–1910 — formal-logic structural homology

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: **FORMAL-LOGIC CONTROL / STRUCTURAL HOMOLOGY SECURE / CAUSAL TRANSFER OPEN**

## Core result

The distinction between Lovejoy literally doing logic and displaying a broader `logician-like` style can now be stated precisely.

In 1907–1910 formal-logical work, Lovejoy repeatedly performs this sequence:

1. construct or audit the relevant class/possibility space;
2. require the partition to be consistent, discriminating and sufficiently exhaustive;
3. map a proposition onto the cases/relations it actually permits;
4. preserve uncertainty when several cases remain compatible;
5. derive only the inference licensed by those relations;
6. limit the jurisdiction of the formal exercise.

This is a technical formal analogue of several operations visible in his historical work. It proves **structural homology**, not that formal logic caused the historical method.

## 1907 — classification audit

In `Kant's Classification of the Forms of Judgment`, Lovejoy asks whether a stated classificatory principle actually yields the canonical classes.

The operation is:

`classification principle`
→ `generate possible cases`
→ `test whether the proposed classes are coordinate and consistently distinguished`
→ `ask whether further cases/classes are possible`.

A named or inherited class is not enough; its discriminating criterion must do the classificatory work.

The same paper also reconstructs the history of Kant's table, keeping logical role separate from historical genesis.

## 1910 — exhaustive relation-space before inference

In `The Treatment of 'Opposition' in Formal Logic`, Lovejoy constructs five mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive denotative relations between two terms/classes.

He then treats a categorical proposition as retaining some of those possible relations and excluding others. A true proposition need not identify one unique real relation; if several cases survive, judgment remains suspended among them.

Only after this mapping does Lovejoy derive inference rules from inclusion, overlap, disjointness and exhaustion among the compatible-case sets.

Thus:

`possible relation-space`
→ `claim-to-case mapping`
→ `licensed inference`.

The key rule is non-transfer: eliminating some possibilities does not automatically establish a stronger remaining relation.

## Relation to the current two-stage historical model

The history-of-ideas branch now distinguishes:

### Gate A — aggregation/equivalence
What counts as the same unit/type?

### Gate B — relation/mediation
What relation connects the identified units, and what stronger inference does it license?

The formal-logic control is structurally similar:

### Formal Gate A
What are the legitimate classes/possible relations? Is the partition coherent and exhaustive?

### Formal Gate B
Given which cases a proposition permits, what inference follows?

The analogy is useful because it specifies what `logician-like` means: explicit class/state-space construction, explicit relation assignment, and inferential restriction.

## Why this does not establish a causal origin

Notebook 004/1898 already contains identity tests, temporal/logical distinction, causal-function separation, genealogy/configuration control and proposition-relative confidence before Lovejoy's 1907/1910 formal-logic publications.

Notebook 005 and comparative-religion work likewise develop mechanism-sensitive classification before 1906. Many constituent proof practices were also available in philology, history of philosophy and comparative religion.

Therefore do not write:

`formal logic → historical method`.

Historically open alternatives include:

- formal logic technically sharpened an already recurring analytical style;
- prior multidisciplinary practice made formal logic especially congenial;
- several fields shared overlapping late-nineteenth-century repertoires of classification and proof;
- Lovejoy later recombined techniques from multiple jurisdictions.

## Jurisdiction control

The 1910 paper itself limits the extensional exercise's reach in relation to H. W. B. Joseph's contrary position; 1911 `Existence and Formal Logic` makes the jurisdiction boundary still stronger.

Thus the logic papers most useful for establishing structural homology also contain actor-level reasons **not** to treat formal logic as a universal historical method.

## 1910 vs 1941

### 1910
`enumerate possible relations`
→ `map proposition to compatible cases`
→ `derive only licensed inference`.

### 1941
`semasiological discrimination / identify basic ideas`
→ `ask logical, psychological, historical-genetic relations`
→ `treat analytical relations as hypotheses`
→ `return to historical evidence`.

Shared architecture:

> classification/identification precedes relation, and relation precedes stronger inference.

Crucial difference:

> formal case-space can be constructed as exhaustive; historical identity and causation remain empirical, open-ended and evidence-dependent.

## Revised genealogy question

The strongest open question is now:

> **How did Lovejoy stabilize a cross-disciplinary habit of making classification spaces, relation types and inferential limits explicit, and what role did direct formal-logical practice play in sharpening that habit relative to philology, comparative religion and history of philosophy?**

## Current shorthand

> **The formal-logic control shows what is specifically `logician-like`: construct/audit the class or possibility space, identify what relation a claim actually licenses, and restrict inference to that relation. Historical analogues are real, but chronology and Lovejoy's own jurisdiction limits block a simple formal-logic-origin story.**
