# Batch 197 — Wilson's Buddhism / otherworldliness partition: entries 19, 21–22, 243–244, and the surviving Vedānta line

Date: 2026-08-26
Status: synced / INDEX-TOPOLOGY CONTROL
Scope: follow-up to Batch196. This batch isolates the way Daniel J. Wilson's 1982 bibliography routes Lovejoy's early Buddhist, Indian, otherworldly, and temporal materials through different bibliographic and index categories. It uses entries 19, 21–22, 176, 243–244 and the subject index as direct controls.

## Core result

Wilson's bibliography preserves several early non-Western / Indic materials, but it does **not** route them through one unified `India → Buddhism → otherworldliness → Great Chain` genealogy.

Instead, the recovered architecture separates at least three channels:

1. **Buddhism as an explicit subject**
   - entry19, 1898 technical article;
   - entry243, 1901 Buddhism lecture syllabus;
   - joined by Wilson's subject index `Buddhism, 19, 243`.

2. **Otherworldliness / temporalism as a conceptual problem**
   - entries21–22;
   - Wilson's index gives `Otherworldliness, 21–22`;
   - entry21's own annotation explicitly says the argument ranges across both Western and **Indian civilizations**.

3. **Vedānta / Hindu philosophy as a review object**
   - entry176, Lovejoy's 1907 review of Paul Deussen's *Outline of the Vedanta System of Philosophy, according to Shankara*;
   - Wilson records Lovejoy's recommendation that scholars study Rāmānuja rather than produce still more studies of Śaṅkara.

These channels coexist in the bibliography but are not automatically cross-wired.

The key historiographical fact is therefore not `Wilson omitted India`. He did not. The sharper fact is:

> **Wilson's reference and index architecture partitions explicit Buddhist scholarship, the conceptual category of otherworldliness, and later Vedānta reviewing into different retrieval routes.**

That partition becomes especially visible because entry21 itself contains Indian material while Wilson indexes it under `Otherworldliness`, not under the recovered `Buddhism` heading.

---

## 1. Entry19 is a complete bibliographic node with no outgoing `See`

Entry19 is:

`The Buddhistic Technical Terms upadāna and upādisea` (1898).

Wilson summarizes it as a technical attempt to establish the correct meanings of two terms essential to interpreting the philosophical system of Buddhism and to correct previous misinterpretations through close analysis.

The OCR page places the annotation at the bottom of the page. The following recovered page begins with entry20. Therefore, unlike the more cautious formulation in Batch196, we can now say at the **entry level**:

> entry19 has no outgoing formal `See` line in Wilson's bibliography.

This remains different from the global claim `no later entry anywhere says See 19`, which still requires a complete incoming-edge audit.

The distinction matters because Wilson frequently uses `See` lines to create long-range bibliographic continuity. Entry19 itself does not generate such a forward route.

---

## 2. Entry243 is likewise complete and has no outgoing `See`

Entry243 is:

`Syllabus: The Philosophy of Buddhism`, Washington University Association, 1901.

Wilson records a four-lecture sequence:

1. `The Place of Buddhism among the Philosophies of India`;
2. and 3. `Two Essential Formulas of Buddhism`;
4. `Buddhist Ethics and its Goal`.

This entry occurs in Part I.E, `Brief Notes, Poems, Letters, and Speeches`, so the Buddhism syllabus is already separated from the 1898 journal article by **bibliographic genre** even before subject indexing is considered.

The recovered page ends after Wilson's syllabus annotation; the next page begins directly with entry244. Therefore:

> entry243 has no outgoing formal `See` line.

Again, this is a secure entry-level claim, not yet a proof that no incoming `See 243` exists elsewhere.

---

## 3. The index deliberately reunites 19 and243 under `Buddhism`

Wilson's index gives the explicit subject heading:

`Buddhism, 19, 243`.

This performs work that the chronological sequence and formal `See` network do not.

The two objects differ by:

- date: 1898 vs 1901;
- genre: journal article vs lecture syllabus;
- bibliographic section: essay/article vs brief note/syllabus;
- evidentiary form: published technical argument vs teaching outline.

The subject index nevertheless reunites them.

Thus the correct relation type is:

`19 —[W-IDX: Buddhism]— 243`.

It is not:

`19 → 243 [W-SEE]`

and not yet:

`19 caused 243`.

The index proves thematic classification, not intellectual transmission.

---

## 4. Entry21 contains India but is routed under `Otherworldliness`

Wilson's annotation of entry21, `Religion and the Time-Process` (1902), explicitly says that Lovejoy traces otherworldliness back to the ancients in both **Western and Indian civilizations**.

The annotation then emphasizes:

- Platonism as a basis of Western otherworldliness;
- its influence on Christianity;
- the modern rejection of otherworldliness;
- deism;
- declining influence of the chain of being;
- evolutionary thought;
- a new theory of value centered on process and activity.

In Wilson's index the relevant subject heading is:

`Otherworldliness, 21–22`.

The recovered Buddhism heading is only:

`Buddhism, 19, 243`.

Therefore the Indian content of entry21 is not enough to place 21 inside Wilson's explicit `Buddhism` bucket. Wilson routes 21 primarily through the **conceptual problem** it treats, not through the geographical/civilizational presence of India in the article.

This is a crucial control against a simplistic interpretation of the index. A text can contain Indian material and still be classified under a trans-civilizational philosophical problem.

---

## 5. `Buddhism` and `Otherworldliness` are non-overlapping index sets in the recovered headings

The two recovered subject headings are:

`Buddhism = {19, 243}`

`Otherworldliness = {21, 22}`.

At the level of these explicit headings, the sets do not overlap.

This is striking because the primary corpus gives reasons to expect conceptual contact:

- Buddhism is centrally concerned in Lovejoy's early materials with formulas, attachment, release, ethics, and goal;
- entry21's historical problem concerns otherworldliness, time, process, value, and explicitly Indian as well as Western civilization;
- Batch192 established direct Indic/Vedānta material inside entry22's 1904 Bruno–Spinoza argument.

Yet Wilson's index presents the retrieval topology as two cleanly separated headings.

This is not evidence that the primary texts themselves were cleanly separated. It is evidence that the **bibliographic retrieval system** creates a separation.

---

## 6. Entry244 proves that Wilson's subject index is selective, not mechanical

The entry immediately after the Buddhism syllabus is entry244:

`Syllabus: Robert Louis Stevenson, Moralist` (1901).

Wilson's annotation says that Lovejoy describes an opposition between an **otherworldly ethics** and a new **temporally based ethics**.

Yet Wilson's subject index `Otherworldliness` points only to 21–22, not to 244.

This is methodologically important.

If Wilson's index were a mechanical keyword concordance, the occurrence of `otherworldly` in his own annotation of 244 would strongly predict inclusion. It is not included. Therefore:

> the index is an editorial classification device, not a word-frequency map.

This strengthens the interpretation of the Buddhism / Otherworldliness split. The index selects which works count as central exemplars of a concept.

It also shows why subject-index absence should not be translated into primary-text absence.

---

## 7. Genre itself produces a partition

Entries243 and244 sit under Part I.E:

`Brief Notes, Poems, Letters, and Speeches`.

This matters because both are lecture syllabi with potentially important conceptual content.

Entry243 outlines:

- Buddhism's place among Indian philosophies;
- two essential formulas;
- ethics;
- goal.

Entry244 outlines:

- modern moral tendencies;
- otherworldly ethics;
- temporal ethics.

Yet their placement under a `brief` genre category makes them structurally remote from entries19 and21 even when their conceptual vocabulary touches the same problems.

This yields a second partition mechanism distinct from subject indexing:

`bibliographic genre`

can separate objects that

`conceptual content`

might bring together.

Therefore Wilson's bibliography needs to be read on at least three axes simultaneously:

- chronology;
- genre/section;
- cross-reference/index topology.

Any one axis by itself is misleading.

## 8. Entry176 shows that the Indic line remains active after 1904

Wilson's entry176 reviews Paul Deussen's *Outline of the Vedanta System of Philosophy, according to Shankara* in 1907.

Wilson records two specific judgments by Lovejoy:

- the translated outline will be useful for those teaching Hindu philosophy;
- scholars should turn to the Vedānta system of **Rāmānuja** rather than continue producing more studies of **Śaṅkara**.

This is significant for the repository chronology.

The early Indic line is therefore not limited to:

`1898 Buddhist terminology`

or even

`1901 Buddhism syllabus`.

It remains visible in 1907 as an active evaluative engagement with current Vedānta scholarship and teaching.

The safe statement is:

> Lovejoy's documented engagement with Indian philosophy persists into at least 1907 in Wilson's bibliography.

The stronger claim `there is an uninterrupted intellectual programme from 1898 to 1907` still requires primary-text and archival controls.

## 9. The 19 → 243 → 176 chronology is not a Wilson genealogy

It is tempting to build:

`1898 Buddhist philology`

→ `1901 Buddhism teaching`

→ `1907 Vedānta review`.

Chronologically and thematically this is worth investigating. But Wilson does not, in the recovered entries, encode this as a formal cross-reference chain.

The correct current status is:

- 19 ↔ 243: `W-IDX`, subject co-location under Buddhism;
- 176: documented later Indic/Hindu-philosophy review object;
- 19 → 243 → 176: `P-RESEARCH-HYPOTHESIS`, not `W-SEE`.

This boundary should remain explicit in the repository graph.

## 10. The 21–22 line is bibliographically denser than the 19/243/176 line

Entries21–22 have:

- reciprocal formal `See` edges;
- the `Otherworldliness, 21–22` index heading;
- Wilson's introduction-level elevation as early history-of-ideas work;
- later Great-Chain reception links;
- temporalism / religion / Neo-Platonism index routing.

By contrast, the recovered Indic-specific nodes have:

- 19: complete technical article annotation, no outgoing `See`;
- 243: complete syllabus annotation, no outgoing `See`, but `Buddhism` co-indexing with 19;
- 176: review annotation documenting continuing Vedānta engagement, without a recovered formal edge to 19 or243.

The density difference is real at the bibliographic level.

It does not establish a difference in historical importance. It establishes a difference in **how later bibliography makes continuity easy or difficult to retrieve**.

That is the historiographical object.

## 11. Wilson's architecture produces two different forms of continuity

The recovered evidence suggests a useful distinction.

### A. genealogical continuity

Produced by:

- formal reciprocal `See` edges;
- later backward/forward references;
- introduction-level narrative;
- secondary reception.

Example:

`21 ↔ 22 → later Great-Chain genealogy`.

### B. categorical continuity

Produced by:

- subject-index co-location;
- title indexing;
- genre grouping.

Example:

`19 — Buddhism — 243`.

These are not equivalent.

Genealogical continuity makes a developmental story readily visible. Categorical continuity tells a user that two objects concern the same field but does not itself narrate development.

Wilson gives the early Buddhist material primarily **categorical continuity**, while the 21–22 material receives much denser **genealogical continuity**.

This formulation is stronger and safer than saying that one material is `included` and the other `excluded`.

## 12. Relation to the primary-text findings

The bibliographic partition becomes historically interesting only because the primary corpus cuts across it.

### Batch192

Entry22's Bruno–Spinoza essay directly reuses Indic/Vedānta material, including the Muṇḍaka / Vedānta line, inside a history conventionally read as Western metaphysical genealogy.

### Batch194

That same essay distinguishes historical sources, structural comparanda, transmission evidence, and other roles rather than treating all analogies as one influence chain.

### Batch195

The 1904 review article reveals Lovejoy's active handling of French, translation, historical milieu, and classification problems.

Batch197 therefore adds a historiographical warning:

> Wilson's later categories can separate materials that Lovejoy's own early arguments place in the same operational field.

This does not invalidate Wilson. It tells us what must be reconstructed from the primary archive rather than inherited from the bibliography.

## 13. A stronger formulation of the `Indic disappearance` problem

The evidence no longer supports a simple question:

`Why did India disappear after 1898?`

Wilson alone already shows that it did not:

- 1901 Buddhism syllabus;
- 1902 Indian civilization inside `Religion and the Time-Process`;
- 1904 Indic/Vedānta material in Bruno–Spinoza, controlled from the primary text;
- 1907 Deussen / Śaṅkara / Rāmānuja review.

The better question is:

> **How did later bibliographic and historiographical architectures repartition a continuous or recurrent field of early Indic engagement into separate categories — Buddhism, otherworldliness, Western history of ideas, and book reviewing — such that the cross-connections become difficult to see?**

This is now a source-grounded research question, not a retrospective intuition.
