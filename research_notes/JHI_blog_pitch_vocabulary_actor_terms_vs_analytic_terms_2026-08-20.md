# JHI Blog pitch vocabulary audit — actor-native versus analytic language

Date: 2026-08-20
Status: pre-submission wording control

## Result

The current pitch is substantively sound but mixes three levels of vocabulary:

1. **actor-native / directly quoted language**;
2. **period-near but synthetic language** combining propositions from several sources;
3. **our analytic/historiographical vocabulary**.

For the pitch this is acceptable only if the analytic layer is clearly marked as our interpretation rather than made to sound like a shared nineteenth-century terminology.

## Actor-native / directly secured

- Warren: `the relation of desire to attachment is that of identity` / `Attachment to Desire is identity`.
- Lovejoy notebook: `But not so if we are to follow the present text.`
- Senart: `ordre plus ou moins accidentel`; `La série des skandhas figure donc en réalité deux fois...`; `série composite`.
- Lovejoy 1898: `derivative, composite, and practically meaningless character`; Senart's witnesses `prove nothing remotely like` the proposed interpretation.
- Lovejoy 1898: `The terms used there, perhaps even the ideas, are doubtless largely borrowed ones; but the arrangement and application of them is certainly original and characteristic.`
- Lovejoy 1898: meaning settled `by an examination of its use` rather than etymological arguments; evidence `relevant but not necessarily conclusive`; matter `cannot be regarded as finally settled`.
- Rhys Davids digest in 004 (not Lovejoy authorial wording): whether the Sāṃkhya `system existed as a whole`.

## Period-near but synthetic

These words occur in the first-order field, but the combinations are ours:

- `similarity/resemblance` as a general proof problem;
- `borrowing` / `borrowed` as a historical-dependence problem;
- `system` as the scale of the historical claim;
- `relation` as an umbrella category.

They may be used in pitch prose, but should not be presented as a common codified vocabulary of all participants.

## Our analytic vocabulary

- `transregional controversy` / `transregional field`;
- `what comparison could legitimately prove`;
- `relation-type discrimination`;
- `proof levels`, `proof burdens`, `proof threshold`;
- `provenance` versus `configuration`;
- `selective public proof` / `evidentiary compression`;
- `genealogy` as the umbrella word for claims of derivation, borrowing, dependence and direction;
- `borrowed materials implied a borrowed system`;
- `methodological figure that “Lovejoy” later became`;
- `disciplinary re-description`, `junction`, `repertoire`.

These are project-level reconstructions, not Lovejoy/Warren/Senart terminology.

## Main pitch wording risk

Current clause:

`a transregional controversy over what comparison could legitimately prove: whether resemblance established identity or borrowing, whether borrowed materials implied a borrowed system, and when textual evidence could sustain genealogy.`

This is a fair synthesis, but almost all of its high-level nouns (`transregional`, `comparison could legitimately prove`, `borrowed materials`, `borrowed system`, `genealogy`) are ours. `identity` is the one conspicuously actor-native term.

The sentence may therefore imply a more unified period vocabulary/methodological debate than the evidence warrants.

## Recommended replacement

Prefer a sentence that first gives actor-near propositions, then marks the abstraction as ours:

`By tracing several entries back to late-nineteenth-century Buddhist and Sanskrit scholarship, and forward into Lovejoy's 1898 article, the piece reconstructs disputes over whether desire and attachment were “identical,” whether a composite formula was merely an accidental arrangement, and whether terms or even ideas could be borrowed while their “arrangement and application” remained distinctive. I argue that these local disputes formed part of a wider, transregional problem of historical inference: what could comparison actually establish?`

Advantages:

- `identical` / identity: Warren/Lovejoy source chain;
- accidental arrangement: Senart + 004 direct wording;
- borrowed terms/ideas / arrangement and application: Lovejoy 1898 direct wording;
- only after those first-order propositions does the pitch introduce our analytic summary (`transregional problem of historical inference`, `what could comparison actually establish?`).

## Even shorter pitch-safe version

`The piece follows Lovejoy through disputes over whether desire and attachment were “identical,” whether a composite Buddhist formula was merely an accidental arrangement, and whether borrowed terms or ideas necessarily implied a borrowed arrangement. I use these disputes to reconstruct a wider, transregional problem of historical inference: what could comparison actually establish?`

## Terms to avoid presenting as period-native

- provenance/configuration
- diagnosticity
- scale of inference
- proof-level hierarchy
- historical warrant
- genealogy (unless plainly used as our shorthand)
- transregional repertoire
- contextualism

All remain useful analytic terms in drafting/argument, but should be flagged grammatically as our reconstruction (`I argue`, `the piece reconstructs`, `can be read as`, etc.).

## Bottom line

The pitch should keep one layer of explicit analytic vocabulary, but anchor it in 2–3 actor-native propositions first. The strongest wording pattern is:

`period proposition → period proposition → period proposition → I argue/reconstruct X`.

That prevents our conceptual compression from masquerading as a nineteenth-century shared metalanguage.
