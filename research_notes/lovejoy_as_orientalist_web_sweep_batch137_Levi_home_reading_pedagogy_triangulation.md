# Batch 137 — Lévi's home at 9 rue Guy-de-la-Brosse: spatial and pedagogical controls for Lovejoy's reported private readings

Date: 2026-08-19  
Status: synced  
Scope: test the most personal part of Daniel J. Wilson's reconstruction of Lovejoy's Paris year: when the Pāli-Buddhism course Lovejoy wanted was unavailable, he reportedly went occasionally to Sylvain Lévi's home to read sacred texts with him. The aim is not to substitute context for the missing Lovejoy letters, but to ask whether the reported place and teaching modality can be independently controlled.

## Core result

The episode now has a much firmer external frame.

In the exact **1898–99** EPHE V-Section report, the official address list gives:

`Lévi (Sylvain), rue Guy-de-Labrosse, 9.`

AGORHA independently identifies **9 rue Guy-de-la-Brosse, Paris Ve** as Lévi's **private address** from 1894 to 1935. The EPHE address lists for 1897–98 and 1899–1900 repeat the same address, establishing continuity across the mission year and Lovejoy's Paris residence.

The pedagogical side is independently controlled as well. The 1900–01 EPHE Sanskrit report explicitly states that **outside the two regular conferences** Lévi continued the reading of the Bhagavad-Gītā with Joseph Trumbull Stickney. Mauss's retrospective account likewise describes intensive and partly individualized training under Lévi and Finot, including Pāli/Buddhist translation and a Brāhmaṇa course that Lévi directed personally toward Mauss's needs.

A social-history reconstruction by Roland Lardinois further shows that after moving to 9 rue Guy-de-la-Brosse in 1894, the Lévi household became, around the turn of the century, a recurring scholarly salon bringing together colleagues, students and visitors.

The correct synthesis is therefore three-layered:

`verified private residence`

+

`verified supplementary / student-specific textual pedagogy`

+

`Wilson-mediated report that Lovejoy went to Lévi's home for sacred-text reading`.

This is substantially stronger than treating Wilson's sentence as an isolated biographical anecdote. It still does **not** identify the texts, language, exact dates, or prove from a Lovejoy primary witness that the sessions occurred at 9 rue Guy-de-la-Brosse. JHU MS-0873 remains indispensable for that final step.

## 1. The exact 1898–99 EPHE address is 9 rue Guy-de-Labrosse

The official address list printed in the V-Section report for the exercise 1898–99 gives the directors and maîtres de conférences, including:

`Lévi (Sylvain), rue Guy-de-Labrosse, 9.`

Primary source:

https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0002_1898_num_12_8_19519

The same page places other figures in Lovejoy's reconstructed Paris network at their contemporary addresses:

- Marillier — 7 rue Michelet;
- Foucher — 16 rue de Staël;
- Maurice Vernes — 97 bis rue Notre-Dame-des-Champs;
- Jean Réville — villa de la Réunion, Auteuil.

For the present problem the key point is not a map of Paris sociability but an exact contemporaneous locator for Lévi during Lovejoy's year.

---

## 2. The address is independently identified as Lévi's private residence

AGORHA's institutional authority record for Sylvain Lévi classifies:

**9, rue Guy-de-la-Brosse, 75005 Paris**

as an `adresse privée`, dated **1894–1935**.

Institutional source:

https://agorha.inha.fr/ark:/54721/85da9f6d-dbb5-4fc9-b665-200ba9d9285f

This matters because the EPHE list by itself could be read merely as an administrative correspondence address. The independent private-address classification shows that the same location was his residence.

A useful continuity control comes from the neighboring EPHE reports:

- 1897–98: Lévi, 9 rue Guy-de-Labrosse;
- 1898–99: same;
- 1899–1900: same.

Primary controls:

https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0002_1897_num_11_7_19496

https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0002_1899_num_13_9_19542

Thus the address spans his mission departure, return, and Lovejoy's year without a visible change.

---

## 3. Supplementary textual work outside advertised conferences is directly documented

Batch136 identified the strongest primary pedagogical control. The 1900–01 IV-Section Sanskrit report first describes Lévi's two regular conferences, then states:

`En dehors de ces deux conférences, M. Lévi a poursuivi, avec M. Stickney, la lecture de la Bhagavad-Gîtâ...`

Primary source:

https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0001_1901_num_1_1_2303

The phrase `en dehors de ces deux conférences` is critical. It directly establishes that the published timetable did not exhaust a student's actual textual work with Lévi.

This is exactly the evidentiary asymmetry needed for the Lovejoy case:

`no advertised Pāli course`

≠

`no supplementary text reading with Lévi`.

The report does not say where Stickney's supplementary reading occurred. It cannot be used as evidence for a home setting. It is a control only for the **teaching modality**.

---

## 4. Mauss supplies a second control for individualized pedagogy

Marcel Mauss later described his own Indianist apprenticeship under Lévi and Louis Finot in unusually concrete terms: rigorous Sanskrit grammar, chrestomathy, translation exercises and metrics; then Pāli and Buddhist translation; and further work on Manu and Indian theatre.

Current scholarly witness preserving the participant recollection:

https://books.openedition.org/editionscnrs/60559

The same reconstructed sequence indicates that Lévi's Brāhmaṇa teaching in 1896–97 could be directed specifically toward Mauss, feeding later work on sacrifice.

This supports a general but historically useful proposition:

> Lévi's pedagogy could adapt formal courses and supplementary textual work to the research trajectory of individual advanced students.

It does not prove Lovejoy received the same treatment. It makes Wilson's account institutionally non-anomalous.

---

## 5. The residence itself became a scholarly social space

Roland Lardinois's social history of French Indian studies reconstructs Lévi's move after his 1894 Collège de France election from Place Saint-Michel to **9 rue Guy-de-la-Brosse**, where he lived with his family. Lardinois describes the apartment as developing, around the turn of the century, into a regular scholarly salon attended by colleagues, friends, students and visitors from India and Japan.

Scholarly source:

https://books.openedition.org/editionscnrs/60557

This is useful spatial context, but chronology must be kept tight. The source says the weekly salon became regular **around the turn of the century**. It should not be rewritten as:

`Lovejoy attended Lévi's weekly salon in autumn 1898`.

There is no evidence for that.

The safe use is narrower:

> the residence Wilson identifies as a place of private study belonged to a household that was demonstrably used for scholarly/student sociability in the immediately surrounding period.

---

## 6. Wilson's Lovejoy claim now sits inside independent controls

Wilson's reconstruction, based on Lovejoy letters dated 17 October, 1 December and 5 December 1898, says that Lovejoy:

- hoped to take a Pāli-Buddhism course from Lévi;
- found that such a course was not being offered;
- occasionally went to Lévi's home to read sacred texts with him.

The letters themselves remain unrecovered in the present digital layer. Therefore the wording above stays **Wilson-mediated**.

But the surrounding claims now divide cleanly:

### Independently secure

- Lévi's private residence in the exact year: **9 rue Guy-de-la-Brosse**;
- Lévi's return from the 1897–98 mission before Lovejoy's cited October letter;
- no formal Pāli course in the recovered 1898–99 EPHE programme;
- recent pre-mission Pāli/Buddhist-translation pedagogy under Lévi/Finot;
- supplementary text reading outside regular courses was an actual Lévi teaching practice;
- the residence became a scholarly/student social site in the surrounding period.

### Still dependent on Lovejoy's letters / Wilson

- that Lovejoy personally went to Lévi's home;
- how often;
- whether the home was exactly 9 rue Guy-de-la-Brosse in the wording of the letters;
- which sacred texts were read;
- whether the reading language was Pāli, Sanskrit, translation, or mixed;
- whether these meetings began before or after the formal November/December course openings.

This separation substantially raises the evidentiary precision of the Paris narrative without pretending that contextual corroboration equals primary testimony.

---

## 7. Paper-level formulation

A defensible paragraph is now:

> **Wilson's account of Lovejoy's occasional private readings with Sylvain Lévi no longer stands as an isolated biographical detail. The EPHE's own 1898–99 address list places Lévi at 9 rue Guy-de-Labrosse, independently identified as his private residence, while later EPHE reports show that Lévi continued advanced textual readings with individual students outside his regular conferences. Mauss's recollection similarly presents Lévi's Indianist teaching as adaptable to particular students, and the same rue Guy-de-la-Brosse apartment became a recognized scholarly gathering place around the turn of the century. These controls do not replace the missing Lovejoy letters, but they make both the place and pedagogical form of Wilson's report historically specific and institutionally plausible.**

Shorter:

> **The missing Pāli course did not exhaust the available pedagogy: Lévi's Paris teaching extended beyond the timetable, and his private residence can now be fixed independently in Lovejoy's exact year.**
