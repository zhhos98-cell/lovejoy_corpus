Müller / Joseph Dahlmann, and clarify what Lovejoy is doing when he says both derivations are etymologically possible but meaning must be settled by usage. This batch uses Lovejoy 1898 as the primary map, Oldenberg 1882 as recovered primary control, a close reproduction of Childers’s original dictionary article, and bibliographically secure public-domain objects for Müller/Dahlmann/Rhys Davids. Exact Müller p.30, Dahlmann p.14, and the original Rhys-Davids locus remain page-level retrieval targets.

## Core advance

Lovejoy’s p.134 “etymological uncertainty” is not generic caution. It is a **conscious arbitration between two incompatible nineteenth-century lexical genealogies**:

1. **Childers / Rhys Davids line:** Pāli `upādi` is derived from `upādā / upāda`, and can denote the five khandhas; `upadhi` remains the Pāli representative of Sanskrit `upadhi`.
2. **Müller / Dahlmann line:** Sanskrit `upadhi` is treated as equivalent to Pāli `upādi`, bringing the term toward the Sanskrit philosophical vocabulary of phenomenal/material existence.

Lovejoy explicitly cites both sides and refuses to let either etymology determine the semantic result. His next sentence shifts the criterion:

> both derivations seem etymologically possible; **the meaning of `upādi` must therefore be settled without reference to its etymology**.

He then turns to usage and to `upādāna`-related ethical/path evidence.

This makes the second half of the article a cleaner methodological example than previously recognized: **when lexical genealogy is underdetermined, Lovejoy brackets the genealogy and asks what role the form performs across textual contexts.**

## 1. Childers’s position is now textually secure at high confidence, though the original scan page is still pending

Robert Caesar Childers, _A Dictionary of the Pāli Language_ (vols. 1872/1875), article `Upādiseso`, is reproduced in full in Ananda W. P. Guruge’s _From the Living Fountains of Buddhism_ web edition. The editor explicitly says the transcription system has been modernized, so this is a close reproduction of the dictionary article rather than a facsimile primary.

The reproduced article says:

- `Upādiseso` = **“Having the Skandhas remaining”**;
- `upādi` is a masculine noun formed from `Upādā` as `upadhi` from `upadhā`;
- `upādi` is a name for the five khandhas (`pañcakkhandhassa etaṁ adhivacanaṁ`);
- `upādisesa-nibbāna` / `anupādisesa-nibbāna` mean respectively “having the Skandhas remaining” / “not having the Skandhas remaining”;
- Childers treats northern `sopadhiśeṣa` as the result of scholars being puzzled by anomalous Pāli `upādi` and changing it toward `upadhi`.

This is exactly the lexical/semantic package Lovejoy labels the “usual” view before attacking it.

Important source-layer caution: until the Childers scan itself is extracted, quotations for publication should either cite the original dictionary after manual/page verification or explicitly mark Guruge’s modernized reproduction as the access witness.

Public object:
- Open Library / Internet Archive work: Childers, _A Dictionary of the Pali Language_, public-domain scan; search `Upādiseso` in the second-volume/full dictionary object.

## 2. The Childers line is stronger than a simple translation convention

The reproduced Childers entry does not merely gloss `upādisesa` as “with skandhas remaining.” It supplies a **derivational story**:

`upādā → upādi → name for the five khandhas`.

That genealogy performs real explanatory work. It licenses the standard two-Nirvāṇa reading:

- saint/alive: five khandhas remain;
- final extinction/death: khandhas no longer remain.

Lovejoy’s objection on p.133 is therefore aimed at two levels simultaneously:

1. semantic improbability — substantially the same verbal family would denote attachment in `upādāna` but the attached aggregates in `upādi`;
2. derivational overreach — the proposed relation to `upādā` does not by itself explain why the derivative should become a collective name for the skandhas.

His phrase that Rhys Davids’s proposal is “an explanation that hardly explains” is best read in this precise setting: derivation has been offered as if it were semantic explanation, but the shift in function remains unexplained.

## 3. Rhys Davids belongs to the same lexical genealogy, but the exact original locus is still unresolved

Lovejoy quotes Rhys Davids directly:

> a comprehensive name for all the skandhas is `upādi`, derived, in allusion to their cause `upādāna`, from `upāda`, “to grasp.”

The exact wording is independently reproduced in Richard Maurice Bucke’s _Cosmic Consciousness_ (1901), which shows that this Rhys-Davids formulation was circulating in contemporary English-language exposition. But the present web sweep has not yet pinned the sentence to the exact page of Rhys Davids’s own work.

The likely public-domain candidates are now bibliographically controlled:

- T. W. Rhys Davids, _Buddhism: Being a Sketch of the Life and Teachings of Gautama, the Buddha_, 1894 new/revised ed., Internet Archive identifier `buddhismbeingske00daviiala`;
- earlier 1887 scan, IA `buddhismbeingask00daviuoft`;
- Rhys Davids’s _Lectures on the Origin and Growth of Religion…_ remains another possible locus, but no exact phrase hit has been recovered there.

Until the exact locus is found, Lovejoy’s quotation itself is secure evidence for what **Lovejoy understood Rhys Davids to be arguing**; it should not yet be used as an independently page-verified Rhys-Davids primary quotation.

## 5. Lovejoy’s arbitration is structurally different from choosing a side

Once the two lineages are placed side by side, Lovejoy’s move on p.134 becomes unusually clear.

He does **not** say:

- Childers is etymologically wrong, therefore Oldenberg is right; or
- Müller/Dahlmann prove `upādi = upadhi`, therefore Oldenberg is right.

Instead he says both derivations are etymologically possible and **withdraws etymology as the decisive tribunal**.

The subsequent proof is then organized around usage:

- `sati va upādisese` as the state distinguishing Anāgāmin from Arahat;
- the already-deceased monk who can still be asked to be `saupadisesa`;
- the third Sutta-Nipāta passage where `anupādisesa` is presented as an ethical/religious goal and cannot plausibly mean physical death;
- `saṁyojana` / attachment comparison.

The sequence is methodologically important:

> **underdetermined word-history → suspend derivational adjudication → reconstruct semantic function from distribution and doctrinal contrast.**

This is stronger than merely saying Lovejoy “prefers usage over etymology.” It specifies why: **the available genealogies conflict, so etymology lacks discriminating power for the interpretive question at hand.**

## 6. Oldenberg 1882 makes the contrast with Lovejoy even sharper

Oldenberg’s recovered English excursus faces essentially the same ambiguity but eventually resolves it through a more confident text-historical/orthographic hypothesis. After canonical evidence for ethical residue, he declares that `upādi` is simply a Pāli spelling of `upadhi`, probably created through manuscript orthography/assimilation, and uses this to explain the troublesome physical reading.

Lovejoy retains Oldenberg’s semantic result but does not reproduce that level of genealogical certainty.

This yields a four-position map:

| Scholar | Lexical genealogy | Semantic result | Epistemic posture |
|---|---|---|---|
| Childers | `upādā → upādi`; distinct from `upadhi` | upādi = five khandhas | genealogy supports physical-residue reading |
| Rhys Davids | same broad derivation; `upādi` as collective khandha name related to cause `upādāna` | five khandhas remain | explanatory derivation accepted |
| Müller / Dahlmann | Sanskrit `upadhi` ↔ Pāli `upādi` | brings term toward Sanskrit philosophical `upadhi` | exact page argument pending recovery |
| Oldenberg | `upādi` effectively orthographic Pāli `upadhi` | ethical residue / sinful substratum | strong orthographic-text-history resolution |
| Lovejoy | both derivations possible | ethical residue / attachment favored by usage | genealogy bracketed; final textual contradiction remains open |

The line most specific to Lovejoy is therefore **not** a new etymology. It is a rule about what to do when etymology cannot discriminate between live semantic hypotheses.

## 7. This sharpens the broader `004 → 1898 → 1902` argument

The two halves of Lovejoy 1898 can now be stated with parallel structure.

### First half: Hardy / karma / `upādāna`

Historical sources provide more than one causal factor. Lovejoy separates their functions and then explains historically why inherited karma and Buddhist `upādāna` perform different jobs.

### Second half: Childers–RhysDavids / Müller–Dahlmann / Oldenberg / `upādisesa`

Historical-philological authorities provide incompatible genealogies. Lovejoy refuses to let genealogy decide semantics, reconstructs current function from textual distribution, and keeps the residual textual contradiction open.

Thus the common operation is now more exact:

> **origin and function are distinct explanatory questions; evidence sufficient for one is not automatically sufficient for the other.**

This gives a strong operational bridge to 1902, where Lovejoy likewise separates conceptual resemblance from demonstrated historical transmission and tracks a category/logical motive through configurations whose historical derivations differ.

Again, this is not proto-`unit-idea` terminology. It is an observable rule for allocating evidentiary burdens among different historical questions.

## 8. Modern afterlife — useful only as historiographical control

Modern scholarship confirms that the `upādi / upadhi` ambiguity remained a live issue rather than being cleanly settled by the nineteenth-century debate. Recent lexical resources explicitly preserve both “residual clinging/fuel” and “residue remaining” senses for `anupādisesa`, while modern historical work treats the two-Nirvāṇa interpretation as dependent on how `upādi/upadhi` is parsed.

This should be used only to demonstrate the later disciplinary afterlife of the ambiguity, not to validate Lovejoy retrospectively.

## Working paper formulation

> In the `upādisesa` section, Lovejoy did not solve a lexical controversy by proposing a superior etymology. He treated the competing etymologies themselves as underdetermined evidence. The interpretive burden was transferred from word-history to patterned usage and doctrinal function. That separation between genealogy and function—visible in a different form in the article’s karma/`upādāna` analysis—provides a concrete pre-1902 instance of Lovejoy allocating distinct kinds of evidence to distinct historical explanations.
