## Core result

The full primary text materially strengthens the argument.

Lovejoy does not merely describe Spinoza as internally contradictory. He explicitly defines the historical task as explaining a philosopher's **dialectical procedure through its historical sources and affinities**; reconstructs Neo-Platonism as a tradition in which apparently opposed propositions arise as necessary consequences of one principle; treats Bruno and Spinoza as later elaborations of that inherited structure; and closes by insisting that Spinoza's two opposed conceptions must be understood both **logically and historically** through their `single dialectical ground` and their source in an `ancient and well-established philosophical tradition`.

The direct article therefore gives a stronger 1904 operator than the previous review-based reconstruction:

`conflicting interpretations of a system`

→ `identify the precise propositions in tension`

→ `reconstruct the dialectical rule generating both sides`

→ `trace that rule through historical sources and affinities`

→ `explain why the later philosopher must retain both opposed sides`

→ `locate the present system inside a long historical combination rather than treating contradiction as mere error`.

The safest code is now:

`DIRECT PRIMARY / PROPOSITION-LEVEL SYSTEM DECOMPOSITION / HISTORICAL-DIALECTICAL GENEALOGY / FULL-TEXT CLOSED`.

## 1. The opening problem is historiographical classification, not simply influence

Printed p.141 opens by saying that attempts to **classify Spinoza's system** and define its true relation to other historical types of doctrine remain notoriously unsettled. Lovejoy immediately juxtaposes incompatible modern readings: atheist versus acosmist, mystic versus mechanistic nature-philosopher, realist versus nominalist. He then names contemporary interpreters such as Pollock, Joachim and Fullerton whose accounts place Spinoza at radically different points in the philosophical landscape.

This opening is methodologically important. The problem is not initially `Who influenced Spinoza?` It is:

`What kind of system is this?`

and

`What historical relation does its internal structure bear to other doctrinal types?`

Primary text control: printed p.141.

## 2. p.145 states Lovejoy's method directly: historical sources and affinities as a way into dialectical structure

Lovejoy says the paper will address the exegetical problem **not** by another minute reexamination of Spinoza's wording but by:

> `exhibiting the nature of his dialectical procedure through an examination of its historical sources and affinities`

and by comparing it with a demonstrably similar dialectic in Giordano Bruno.

This is the cleanest programmatic statement in the article.

The evidentiary order is therefore not a naive diffusion model:

`historical source / affinity`

→ used to reconstruct

`dialectical procedure`

→ which explains

`the apparent contradiction of the system`.

This matters for comparison with the Buddhist notebooks because `affinity`, `historical source`, `logical operation`, and `current systematic function` remain analytically separable objects.

Primary text control: printed p.145.

## 3. p.151 corrects the earlier batch: `metaphysical otherworldliness` is on p.151, not p.174

The earlier version of Batch188 used a lexical quotation index that assigned the `metaphysical otherworldliness` passage to printed p.174. Direct primary collation corrects that locator.

The phrase occurs on **printed p.151**.

Lovejoy argues that the Neo-Platonic transformation was not an independent graft on alien stock. The conception of the Absolute as both ground and sum of particular being arose, he says, naturally and even inevitably from the older Platonic/Aristotelian structure because that theology contradicted itself unless transformed.

He then distinguishes two possible responses:

1. deny reality to particular/relative things;
2. grant them reality but include them within the Absolute.

Neo-Platonism initially takes the second path, though Lovejoy says it repeatedly risks sliding into illusionism. He then writes that Platonism, beginning with divine transcendence, **`a sort of metaphysical otherworldliness`**, issued in monism and divine immanence.

This passage is much stronger than a lexical recurrence. It gives a causal-dialectical account:

`transcendent Absolute / otherworldliness`

contains an internal problem

→ `the Absolute cannot remain genuinely absolute while merely coexisting with independent beings`

→ therefore pressure toward `inclusive monism / immanence`.

In other words, otherworldliness and immanence are not treated as unrelated historical doctrines accidentally combined later. The opposition is generated from within a common philosophical demand.

Primary text control: printed p.151.

---

## 4. p.153: Neo-Platonism deliberately keeps both sides because both follow from one principle

Printed p.153 is especially important.

Lovejoy says Plotinus hardly attempts to hide the self-contradictory character of the combination. He then states that Neo-Platonism was **deliberately committed to both sides** of the contradiction between divine immanence and divine transcendence/negativity.

Why retain both?

Because both appeared to be equally necessary consequences of a **single metaphysical principle**.

This directly establishes the operator that the contemporary review only summarized:

`A contradicts B`

is not sufficient reason to discard either A or B if

`A` and `B` are both generated by the same governing principle.

Historical analysis must therefore preserve the contradiction long enough to explain its generative structure.

Primary text control: printed p.153.

---

## 5. Bruno is treated as a historical transformation, not a wholly new system

At printed p.160 Lovejoy acknowledges the striking freshness and originality of Bruno's philosophy, especially its anti-ascetic and this-worldly tone. Yet he immediately distinguishes **temper / emphasis / nuance** from the **essential outlines and dialectical method** of the system.

His conclusion is that Bruno is, in those essential respects, a Renaissance Neo-Platonist.

Lovejoy also records real modifications:

- Bruno makes the immanent/monistic side more conspicuous;
- he develops a distinctive pantheistic `cosmic emotion`;
- he alters the treatment of matter;
- he can deny a separate world of universals.

This is not reduction of Bruno to an unchanged inherited doctrine. The method is:

`historical continuity at one level`

coexisting with

`novel emphasis / transformation / correction at another`.

That is closely analogous to the 1898 Buddhist claim that inherited materials can be reorganized into an original and characteristic arrangement and application.

Primary text control: printed p.160 and following.

---

## 6. Spinoza: affinity is acknowledged, but the explanatory target is the inherited dialectic

Later in the article Lovejoy says the close affinity between Bruno's and Spinoza's systems is already broadly recognized. His own aim is more precise: the circuitous reasoning and `delicate balance of contradictions` visible in Neo-Platonism and written large in Bruno are of the essence of Spinoza's ontology.

The significance is not simply:

`Bruno resembles Spinoza`.

It is:

`Spinoza inherits/reproduces a historically established dialectical problem whose opposed requirements remain operative inside his system`.

Thus affinity becomes useful only after the historian identifies what exactly is recurring: not a surface doctrine but a structured inferential problem.

## 7. pp.172–173: Lovejoy's conclusion directly verifies `single dialectical ground`

The full article confirms the quotation previously recovered only through Bawden's 1905 review.

On printed p.172 Lovejoy rejects the view that Spinoza's two conceptions of substance stand in opposition merely **in spite of** his logical principles. His argument is the reverse: the fundamental logical principle of the system leads directly to both conceptions.

He says Spinoza `fully means them both` and that the philosopher's attempts to soften the contradiction preferentially weaken one side rather than eliminate the underlying dual structure.

Lovejoy then insists that interpretation must keep in mind:

- the double-sidedness of Spinoza's thought;
- the genuinely contradictory character of the duality;
- the attempt to mitigate the contradiction by treating one side as appearance;
- **the origin of both sides in a `single dialectical ground`;**
- the impossibility of giving up either side without abandoning the method;
- the **historic source** of the whole complex combination in an ancient philosophical tradition.

Printed p.173 completes the sentence: the tradition repeatedly attempted to conceive ultimate reality as simultaneously **absolute and inclusive**, or to combine Platonism with monism.

Lovejoy then states that only by remembering all these things can we understand where Spinoza stood **`both logically and historically`**.

This is direct primary proof of the exact distinction the project has been reconstructing:

`logical relation`

and

`historical provenance`

are separate questions, but adequate interpretation requires both.

Primary text controls: printed pp.172–173.

## 8. p.173 moral: historical reconstruction leads to a philosophical diagnosis

After reconstructing the historical-dialectical combination, Lovejoy says the moral of the history is the **hopelessness** of trying to construct a metaphysics around a genuinely `absolute` Absolute that is nevertheless also inclusive of all reality.

This is important because the article is not antiquarian source tracing. Historical reconstruction is used to diagnose a recurrent philosophical problem.

The sequence is:

`historical series of transformations`

→ `recurring structural contradiction`

→ `philosophical moral concerning the impossibility of the governing demand`.

Wilson was therefore correct to see the paper as an important precursor of the later *Great Chain of Being*. The primary text, however, lets us formulate the relationship more precisely than `early unit-ideas`: Lovejoy is already tracing a recurrent **problem-structure and dialectical demand** through historically changing systems.

Primary text control: printed p.173.

`internal contradiction`

→ isolate opposed propositions

→ recover their **common dialectical generator**

→ trace the generator through a historical tradition

→ explain why later systems preserve both sides in altered configurations.

The important continuity is not subject matter. It is the refusal to collapse proposition types:

- resemblance;
- logical consequence;
- historical inheritance;
- present systematic function;
- transformation of inherited material.

These propositions can support one another, but Lovejoy repeatedly treats them as requiring distinct arguments.
