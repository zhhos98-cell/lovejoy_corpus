## Core result

A targeted web/bibliographic sweep has still **not produced a secure 1898–1910 citation or review of Lovejoy’s article by a named contemporary Buddhist/Indological scholar**. This remains a negative search result, not evidence that no such citation existed.

At the same time, the article has a demonstrable later disciplinary afterlife: modern scholarship on `upādi / upadhi` and the two-Nirvāṇa problem explicitly groups **Oldenberg → Lovejoy → later advocates such as Peter Masefield** as a recognizable semantic interpretation. That later reception is useful because it confirms what part of Lovejoy’s article actually survived in Buddhist studies: primarily the **subjective/ethical reading of the remainder**, not his broader 004 reconstruction or his later history-of-ideas work.

## 3. Modern afterlife: Lovejoy becomes part of an Oldenberg–Lovejoy semantic line

Modern work on the two-Nirvāṇa problem explicitly describes a strand of interpretation in which `upādi` is treated subjectively as **attachment/defilement** rather than objectively as the surviving aggregates.

A modern doctrinal-history study states that scholars including **Hermann Oldenberg, A. O. Lovejoy and Peter Masefield** argued that `upādi` could not originally have meant the aggregates because early canonical passages use `upādisesa` in contexts such as the distinction between non-returner and Arahat. It specifically cites Lovejoy 1898 p.134 as using the non-returner passage and quotes Lovejoy’s statement that the oldest/more numerous texts point toward Oldenberg’s interpretation.

This is important for our historiography for two reasons.

First, it confirms that Lovejoy’s afterlife in Buddhist studies is **not imaginary** even if immediate reception remains hard to recover.

Second, it confirms the source-control result of batch 40: later scholarship itself reads Lovejoy as substantially **following/reinforcing Oldenberg’s semantic direction**, not as the originator of it.

## 4. Modern criticism also clarifies the limit of Lovejoy’s reading

The same later scholarship criticizes the Oldenberg–Lovejoy–Masefield line for privileging the subjective/ethical use of `upādi` and for not giving sufficient weight to canonical evidence for the objective/material “aggregate/substratum” use.

This is historiographically useful because it prevents us from turning Lovejoy’s 1898 solution into a timeless philological correction. The article belongs to an interpretive controversy whose later history remained open.

For the project, the value of Lovejoy 1898 therefore lies less in whether his final Buddhist semantics is now accepted and more in the **observable research operation**:

- identify rival genealogies;
- separate etymology from usage;
- test multiple contexts;
- preserve conflicting witnesses;
- allocate causal/semantic roles;
- state a provisional reconstruction.

That operation can be historically important even if the resulting interpretation is later revised.

## 5. Karttunen’s “never returned to Buddhist studies” remains too narrow

The current _Persons of Indian Studies_ entry lists only Lovejoy’s 1898 Buddhist paper and says he “apparently never returned to Buddhist studies.” That claim is not compatible with the primary/bibliographic sequence already recovered in this project:

- 1901: _Syllabus: The Philosophy of Buddhism_, four lectures;
- 1902: `Religion and the Time-Process`, which explicitly returns to Buddhist dependent origination and self-cites the 1898 technical paper;
- 1907: review of Deussen’s Vedānta outline, showing continuing engagement with Indian philosophy more broadly.

The appropriate use of this discrepancy is modest: existing Indological biographical reference works can under-register Lovejoy’s post-1898 Buddhist engagement because they index specialist publications rather than teaching and comparative-philosophy reuse.

This is a historiographical correction, not by itself an argument about influence on the later history of ideas.
