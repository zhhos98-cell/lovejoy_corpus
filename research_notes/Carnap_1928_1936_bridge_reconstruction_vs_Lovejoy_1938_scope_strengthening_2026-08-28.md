# Carnap 1928 → 1936–37 bridge reconstruction against Lovejoy 1938

Date: 2026-08-28  
Status: **DIRECT-PRIMARY CARNAP + DIRECT-PRIMARY LOVEJOY / BRIDGE TYPE RECONSTRUCTED / SCOPE-STRENGTHENING CONTROL ACTIVE / RESTART-SAFE**

## Core question

The active adversarial test was:

> After Lovejoy distinguishes A from B, what bridge did the target actor actually claim between A and B, and does Lovejoy preserve that bridge or replace it with a stronger identity/independence relation?

For Carnap the relevant pair is:

`meaning / cognitive significance`
↔
`empirical support / confirmation / testing`.

Current answer:

> **Lovejoy 1938 does identify a genuine philosophical fault line, but his two-criterion reconstruction does not simply reproduce Carnap's own architecture. In 1928 Carnap treats experiential support-conditions as the necessary-and-sufficient explication of theoretical factual content while explicitly distinguishing factual content from the stronger property of testability. Lovejoy splits Carnap's criterion into a broad criterion of psychological/intellectual thinkability and a second criterion of empirical settlement. By 1936–37 Carnap makes the scope restriction and bridge-status even more explicit: cognitive meaning is an empiricist methodological notion, not psychological meaning; testability is stronger than confirmability; choices of language restrictions are practical proposals rather than factual assertions. Lovejoy 1938 explicitly notices the possibility of this restricted `scientific meaning` reading but rejects it as philosophically trivial and deliberately construes positivism more broadly if it is to have philosophical consequence.**

This is stronger and more precise than the earlier generic `overpartition` hypothesis. The new actor-level phenomenon is **scope strengthening / bridge-status contestation**.

## 1. Carnap 1928 — one criterion with an internal experiential bridge, not two independent criteria

Primary text:

Rudolf Carnap, *Scheinprobleme in der Philosophie* (1928), English trans. in *The Logical Structure of the World and Pseudoproblems in Philosophy*, direct CMU Carnap editorial PDF:

https://www.phil.cmu.edu/projects/carnap/editorial/latex_pdf/1928-2e.pdf

### 1.1 Carnap's headline criterion

In §7 Carnap says the meaning of a statement lies in its expressing a **conceivable state of affairs**. A statement that expresses such a state of affairs is meaningful before its truth or falsity is known.

But he immediately specifies what must happen when a new or disputed concept occurs:

> it is **necessary and sufficient** to indicate the experiential conditions under which the statement is to be called true and the conditions under which it is to be called false.

Thus Carnap's own 1928 structure is not naturally:

`criterion A = thinkability`

plus independently

`criterion B = empirical testability`.

It is closer to:

`theoretical/factual content`

↔ explicated by

`conceivable experiential support/refutation conditions`.

The exact philosophical status of that biconditional/explication is contestable. But textually Carnap presents the experiential bridge as **necessary and sufficient**, not as a second criterion floating beside a first one.

### 1.2 Carnap then explicitly distinguishes factual content from testability

Carnap defines:

- `supported by experience E`;
- `testable` — conditions can be indicated under which an experience supporting p or not-p would occur;
- `factual content` — supporting experiences for p or not-p are at least conceivable and characterizable.

He then states directly:

`testable → factual content`

but

`factual content ↛ testable`.

His example is a statement about a red color whose sight causes terror. Carnap says it is not testable because no method is known for finding a supporting experience; nevertheless it has factual content because the relevant experience can be thought and described.

This is decisive against any flat representation of 1928 Carnap as:

`meaning = testability`.

Even the early text already has a weaker factual-content criterion than operational testability.

### 1.3 Theoretical content is not psychological accompaniment

In the other-minds discussion Carnap says psychological and physical languages can express the same **theoretical content**, even when the psychological formulation carries additional accompanying representations.

He explicitly excludes those representations from theoretical content: they do not themselves stand for an additional fact.

Therefore Carnap's `conceivable state of affairs` / `factual content` criterion must not be silently equated with:

`whatever actual idea/image/thought-content can occur psychologically`.

That is already a different scope.

## 2. Lovejoy 1938 — he transforms Carnap's architecture into two criteria

Primary text:

Arthur O. Lovejoy, `On the Criteria and Limits of Meaning`, written 1938, published 1942 in F. P. Clarke and M. C. Nahm (eds.), *Philosophical Essays in Honor of Edgar Arthur Singer, Jr.*, pp. 3–23.

Searchable reproduction:

https://dokumen.pub/philosophical-essays-in-honor-of-edgar-arthur-singer-jr-9781512815245.html

Lovejoy explicitly quotes/uses Carnap's 1928 definitions, so this is not a case of simply overlooking the factual-content passage.

### 2.1 Lovejoy's criterion A

Lovejoy reconstructs a first criterion as:

- a term/sentence is meaningful if its terms express **any actual thought-content**;
- the referent need only be **thinkable**;
- the empirical way to determine whether one has an idea is introspective observation of whether the idea is in fact present.

This is substantially broader and more psychological than Carnap's explicitly theoretical/factual notion of content.

### 2.2 Lovejoy's criterion B

Lovejoy reconstructs a second criterion as:

- a sentence has meaning only if a thinkable experience could stand in the appropriate evidential relation to the judgment so as to establish truth/falsity or answer the question.

He then says Carnap uses the two as if interchangeable and objects that:

`thinkable proposition`

need not be coextensive with

`empirically settleable proposition`.

### 2.3 What Lovejoy has done

Textually this is not merely `Carnap has two criteria and accidentally confuses them`.

The stronger reconstruction is:

1. Carnap says theoretical factual content requires an experiential support bridge and gives that bridge necessary-and-sufficient status for disputed scientific concepts;
2. Lovejoy extracts the `conceivable state of affairs` language into a broader category of **general psychological/intellectual entertainability**;
3. he then treats the experiential support condition as a second criterion;
4. the dispute becomes whether Carnap has a right to infer the second from the first.

Thus Lovejoy changes the **scope and status of the first term** before diagnosing criterion substitution.

This should be coded as:

> **bridge-status contestation + scope widening**, not simply detection of two already-independent Carnapian criteria.

## 3. Lovejoy explicitly strengthens the scope when `scientific meaning` is too weak

This is the most important new actor-level result.

Lovejoy considers a possible defense of neo-positivism: perhaps `scientific meaning` is simply a restricted species within a broader class of generally meaningful propositions, and the empirical requirement applies only to that scientific species.

Lovejoy says that if `scientific` is genuinely restrictive, the thesis becomes essentially definitional or tautological:

`scientifically meaningful = empirically verifiable/controlled`.

On that reading, he admits, **no consequence follows for the general meaningfulness** of the realist/idealist question.

He then makes the interpretive strengthening himself. In substance:

> if positivist claims are to have philosophical consequence and be important if true, `scientific meaning` and `meaning` must be construed as synonymous/coextensive.

This is not analyst inference. Lovejoy announces the rule by which he selects the stronger target reading.

### Operator form

restricted reading R1:

`empiricist scientific significance only`
→ philosophically limited / near-definitional result.

strong reading R2:

`general meaningfulness itself`
→ major anti-metaphysical consequence.

Lovejoy's interpretive move:

`R1 would be philosophically trivial`
→ therefore, to preserve philosophical consequence, read opponent as R2.

This is a **scope-strengthening rule based partly on consequence/significance**.

### Historical risk

A philosophically consequential reading is not automatically the historically best attributed reading.

The new adversarial question must therefore be:

> **Does the target actor actually claim the broader scope, or is Lovejoy selecting it because otherwise the doctrine would not have the philosophical significance he thinks its proponents claim for it?**

This is a sharper target-reconstruction issue than generic overpartition.

## 4. Carnap 1936–37 — the bridge is explicitly methodological, graded, and language-relative

Primary text:

Rudolf Carnap, `Testability and Meaning`, *Philosophy of Science* 3.4 (1936), 419–471; continued 4.1 (1937), 1–40.

Direct primary PDF (part I):

https://www.phil.cmu.edu/projects/carnap/editorial/latex_pdf/1936-10a.pdf

Accessible full two-part reprint:

https://www.hist-analytic.com/Carnaptest.pdf

### 4.1 Confirmation replaces definitive verification

Carnap rejects complete verification as a general standard. Even ordinary particular physical statements admit only increasing degrees of confirmation.

This already shifts the relevant empirical bridge from:

`definitively verifiable`

to

`confirmable in degrees`.

### 4.2 Conventional acceptance is not conventional truth

This point bears directly on Lovejoy's Baltimore objection.

Carnap says accepting/rejecting a synthetic sentence always includes a **conventional component** because observations do not uniquely dictate the threshold for acceptance.

But he immediately denies the stronger inference:

> this does **not** mean that truth or verification itself is conventional.

There remains a non-conventional / objective component consisting of observations.

Thus Carnap's published architecture explicitly distinguishes:

`conventional decision threshold`

≠

`objective observational component`

≠

`truth/confirmation as wholly conventional`.

This is extremely close to the objection Carnap's diary attributes to Lovejoy on 31 Dec. 1935: if everything becomes conventional, positivists lose the right to exclude metaphysics as meaningless.

Do not infer causal response from proximity. The manuscript existed before Baltimore; exact manuscript/publication comparison is required.

### 4.3 Carnap says `meaning = testability/verification` is only a rough first approximation

In the 1937 continuation Carnap says meaningfulness is closely connected with verification/confirmation/testing, but treating the questions as identical can be accepted only as a **rough first approximation**. The real task is to determine their **precise relation**.

This is almost exactly the bridge-reconstruction question now governing the Lovejoy repo.

### 4.4 Carnap explicitly brackets psychological meaning

Carnap says the essay concerns meaning in:

- methodology;
- epistemology;
- applied logic.

It does **not** concern the psychological question of what images or representations are associated with a sentence.

This creates a direct scope conflict with Lovejoy's first criterion, which asks whether there is any actual thought-content that introspection can find.

The opposition is therefore not simply:

`criterion A versus criterion B`.

It is partly:

`Lovejoy: general/introspectively entertainable thought-content`

versus

`Carnap: empiricist cognitive significance inside methodological language construction`.

### 4.5 Given language versus constructed language

Carnap separates two questions:

1. theoretical question about whether an expression satisfies the rules/criterion of an already given language;
2. practical question about what rules a language **should be constructed with**.

For the second there is no right/wrong in the ordinary factual sense; there are proposals/decisions whose convenience or suitability can be discussed.

He even recommends avoiding the vague words `meaningful` and `meaningless` and specifying the exact methodological property instead.

This is a major anti-flattening control against Lovejoy's general-meaning reconstruction.

### 4.6 Carnap explicitly revises earlier `meaningless` talk

Carnap gives the example `This stone is now thinking about Vienna.`

He says the Circle formerly preferred a scientific language in which no corresponding sentence appeared and said the sentence was `not false but meaningless`. Carnap's later preference is to include the sentence and call it false.

He identifies the earlier mistake as failing to recognize that the issue concerned a **decision about the form of language**, so the Circle had expressed a proposal in the form of an assertion.

This is unusually close to Lovejoy's own 1938 complaint that a verbal/definitional conclusion is being promoted into a factual conclusion.

Historical consequence:

> **by the published 1936–37 text Carnap himself has internalized a jurisdiction distinction between proposal/decision and assertion/truth that substantially narrows the target Lovejoy constructs from the 1928 book.**

Again: no causal arrow from Lovejoy is warranted without manuscript revision evidence.

### 4.7 Confirmability is more liberal than testability

Carnap explicitly distinguishes four requirements and recommends the most liberal:

- complete testability;
- complete confirmability;
- testability;
- **confirmability**.

He says confirmability admits predicates that are confirmable but not testable and suffices for the empiricist principle.

Carnap's later intellectual autobiography confirms that he deliberately chose the more liberal **confirmability** criterion; the stronger testability requirement corresponds approximately to Bridgman's operationism.

Therefore Lovejoy's 1938 phrase that empirical verifiability or `testability, as Carnap prefers to call it in more recent writings` is historically too compressed if taken as a description of Carnap's 1936–37 technical position.

Carnap does not simply prefer `testability`; he distinguishes it from confirmability and recommends confirmability.

## 5. Revised verdict on Lovejoy 1938

### What Lovejoy gets right

Lovejoy identifies a genuine normative question:

> why should an empiricist criterion for scientific/cognitive significance have authority over every intelligible philosophical question?

If the issue is framed as **scope of jurisdiction**, his objection is substantial. Carnap's empiricist restriction by itself does not prove that a question outside the chosen scientific language cannot be psychologically entertained or rhetorically discussed.

Lovejoy also correctly notices that a rule/definition about admissible scientific language should not silently be presented as an empirical discovery about what human beings can think.

### Where Lovejoy's reconstruction becomes stronger than Carnap's own architecture

1. He turns Carnap's `conceivable state of affairs` into general actual thought-content/introspective thinkability.
2. He treats Carnap's experiential support bridge as a second criterion whose inference from the first requires justification.
3. He explicitly refuses a merely restricted `scientific meaning` interpretation because it would make the doctrine philosophically too weak/tautological.
4. He therefore reconstructs the positivist claim at a broader level of **general meaningfulness** if it is to retain philosophical consequence.
5. That broader target is especially difficult to attribute to the 1936–37 Carnap, who explicitly limits the inquiry to methodological/cognitive significance and turns language construction into practical proposal.

### Best current formulation

> **Lovejoy 1938 is strongest as a challenge to the jurisdictional scope and philosophical authority of Carnap's empiricist criterion, not as a demonstration that the 1936–37 Carnap literally confused psychological thinkability with testability. Lovejoy obtains the sharper contradiction partly by widening `meaning` to general entertainability and by selecting the broader positivist reading because the restricted scientific-language reading would be philosophically less consequential.**

Warrant: direct-primary textual comparison.

## 6. Refined failure-mode vocabulary

The previous adversarial hypothesis was `overpartition`:

`A ≠ B`
→ sometimes Lovejoy may treat A and B as too independent.

The Carnap comparison adds two more precise mechanisms.

### A. Scope substitution / widening

Target technical scope:

`cognitive/scientific significance in an empiricist language`

is reconstructed as

`meaning / thinkability in general`.

### B. Bridge-status conversion

Target actor treats the connection as:

- stipulation;
- explication;
- methodological criterion;
- language-construction proposal;

while Lovejoy asks for it to be warranted as if it were:

- a general factual proposition about thought;
- an inference from one independently given criterion to another.

### C. Consequence-maximizing target selection

Lovejoy explicitly says the restrictive reading would be philosophically trivial, and therefore construes the thesis more broadly if it is to have philosophical importance.

This creates a testable longitudinal question:

> **Does Lovejoy elsewhere prefer the interpretation of a doctrine that maximizes its logical/philosophical consequences over a narrower historically local formulation?**

Do not generalize before further cases are checked.

## 10. Consequence for the unit-idea / Skinner problem

This branch now supplies a very sharp internal warning for mature Lovejoy historiography.

An historian can legitimately distinguish:

`idea/component A`

from

`actor motive M / linguistic function F / historical configuration C`.

But the historical issue is not settled by A≠M,F,C.

One must reconstruct the bridge:

- constitutive dependence?;
- pragmatic function?;
- inferential role?;
- actor-recognized implication?;
- historical association?;
- genealogy/transmission?;

The 1938 Carnap case shows why this matters: **changing the scope or status of the bridge can manufacture a stronger contradiction than the target actor actually asserts.**

Potential Skinner-facing formulation:

> **The central danger is not analytical individuation itself, but allowing an analytically isolated component to inherit a wider scope or stronger independence than its historically operative bridge-relations warrant.**

This is an analyst-level application. Do not claim Lovejoy's Carnap criticism caused or directly produced the mature unit-idea problem.
