# Batch 81 — The 1906 paper was received by disaggregation: Leuba, King, the James/Kallen text, and the Marett–Jevons debate

Date: 2026-08-18
Status: synced
Scope: immediate / near-immediate reception of Arthur O. Lovejoy, `The Fundamental Concept of the Primitive Philosophy`, *The Monist* 16.3 (1906), 357–382. This follows Batches 76–80, which reconstructed the paper's `vera causa`, claim-relative source criticism, asymmetric cross-regional triangulation, bibliographic function migration, and 005→1906 `reproof` architecture.

## Core result

The first reception pass falsifies a simple story in which later readers remembered Lovejoy's substantive theory (`primitive energetics`, `manitouism`, impersonal force) while immediately forgetting the evidentiary architecture that supported it.

The actual pattern is more differentiated:

1. **James H. Leuba (1912) is method-aware reception.** He cites Lovejoy's 1906 article directly, reproduces the crucial claim that the pre-animistic force works according to `quite regular and intelligible laws`, and then **re-audits whether the evidence warrants that proposition**. He accepts a non-personal power but rejects the inference to mechanical regularity because the reported efficacy is too unpredictable and the relation between ritual means and effects is insufficiently definite/stable.
2. **Irving King (1910) is a near-contemporary evidence-parallel and probably a direct Lovejoy reception case, but the exact direct citation remains publication-grade HOLD until p.163 is collated.** Leuba treats King's chapter `The Mysterious Power` and Lovejoy's article as alternative good summaries of the evidence; Nicolas Meylan independently reports that King directly referred to Lovejoy and concluded that impersonal force was not `in itself a religious concept` (King p.163).
3. **The posthumous James/Kallen text (1911) assimilates Lovejoy into an evidentiary packet.** *Some Problems of Philosophy* cites Lovejoy alongside Jastrow, Jevons, Frazer and Marett for `primitive sympathetic magic` / mana. The citation survives, but Lovejoy's distinctive `vera causa` and source-function architecture is not visible in that compressed use. Because Horace M. Kallen `largely prepared the book for the press`, the footnote cannot yet be assigned securely to James rather than Kallen without manuscript collation.
4. **The 1909 Marett–Jevons debate shows a parallel proof-threshold problem but no currently visible Lovejoy uptake.** Marett's collected *Threshold of Religion* and F. B. Jevons's *Folk-Lore* review debate whether a non-animistic/pre-animistic stage has actually been proved. In the searchable material checked so far, Lovejoy is not named. This is negative reception evidence only, not proof of ignorance or non-reading.

The best synthesis is therefore:

> **Reception did not erase Lovejoy's 1906 article; it disaggregated it. The substantive category, the causal thesis, the evidentiary dossier, and the proof architecture traveled at different rates.**

The component that appears most vulnerable to disappearance is not the phrase `primitive energetics` but the **architecture by which a stronger proposition had to acquire a stronger or independent warrant**. Leuba is the crucial exception: he preserves enough of the inferential structure to challenge the very warrant Lovejoy claims for mechanical regularity.

## 1. Leuba 1912 is the strongest early method-aware reader yet recovered

Primary source:

James H. Leuba, *A Psychological Study of Religion: Its Origin, Function, and Future* (New York: Macmillan, 1912), especially the discussion around printed pp.74–76.

Full-view controls:
- Google Books bibliographic/full-view record: `https://books.google.com/books/about/A_Psychological_Study_of_Religion.html?id=C9oHkL4aGQ4C`
- Open Library / IA record: `psychologicalstu00leubuoft`.

Leuba explicitly introduces Lovejoy by name and source:

- `In the Monist for 1906, Arthur O. Lovejoy offers a criticism of Marett which deserves attention`;
- he identifies the article as *The Monist* XVI, 357–382.

Leuba then reproduces the decisive Lovejoy proposition: Marett, Lovejoy argues, places the emphasis on the wrong side. The key feature is not the unpredictable/abnormal/portentous but a force conceived as operating according to **quite regular and intelligible laws**, therefore susceptible to study and control. Lovejoy's proposed name is `Primitive Energetics`.

### Leuba does not merely repeat the label

Leuba immediately asks whether the data actually license Lovejoy's adjective `regular` / the implied mechanical relation.

His counter-argument is evidentiary:

- Wakonda / Manitou / Mana behavior is in important respects unpredictable;
- the ritual means used to bring the mysterious power into play do **not** disclose a definite and stable quantitative relation between means and effects;
- therefore the operative power is better described as `magical`, not mechanical.

This is an unusually clean reception of the proof problem reconstructed in Batches 76–80.

Lovejoy's structure:

`independent lexical/native evidence`
→ establishes impersonal productive power as a vera causa
→ customs become applications
→ regular manipulability supports `primitive energetics`.

Leuba's response:

`impersonal power evidence accepted`
→ examine relation between ritual operation and outcome
→ regular quantitative/mechanical warrant judged insufficient
→ downgrade `mechanical power` to `magical force`.

Thus Leuba preserves the distinction between **category existence** and **stronger causal characterization**. He does not reproduce Lovejoy's entire citation-level source architecture, but he clearly treats `mechanical regularity` as an additional proposition requiring additional evidence.

### Reception code

- substantive impersonal-power category: **ACCEPT / REUSE**;
- `regular and intelligible laws`: **CONTESTED**;
- quasi-mechanical causality: **DOWNGRADED**;
- proof burden: **PARTIALLY PRESERVED**;
- proposition-relative source grading: **not visibly reproduced at Lovejoy's granularity**.

## 2. Leuba explicitly places King and Lovejoy together as evidence summaries

Immediately after criticizing Lovejoy's mechanical inference, Leuba turns to Irving King's chapter `The Mysterious Power` in *The Development of Religion* (1910).

He summarizes King's evidence as showing terms such as Manitou, Orenda, Wakonda and Mana denoting a **non-personal Power or Potency** at the basis of natural phenomena, with Australian parallels.

Most importantly for reception history, Leuba tells the reader that anyone interested in this point can find a **good summary of the evidence** either:

- in King's chapter VI;
- or in Lovejoy's shorter article cited immediately above.

This creates a direct contemporary reception relation even before King's own Lovejoy citation is fully collated:

> **By 1912 Lovejoy 1906 was being used as an evidentiary survey, not only as the source of a catchphrase.**

That matters because it shows that the 1906 paper's empirical apparatus remained legible to at least one early specialist reader.

## 4. James/Kallen 1911: Lovejoy becomes one authority inside a compressed primitive-philosophy packet

Primary source:

William James, *Some Problems of Philosophy: A Beginning of an Introduction to Philosophy* (New York: Longmans, Green, 1911), posthumous.

Full searchable text:
`https://persweb.wabash.edu/facstaff/helmang/phi288-1213S/smprblmsfphlsph/JamesSmPrblmsfPhlsph.html`

The text's account of the origins of human theorizing says that extraordinary events demanded explanation, that things acted through a mysterious energy or `mana`, that the central practical problem was to acquire mana oneself, and that `sympathetic magic` was a primitive philosophy.

The accompanying footnote directs readers, for primitive sympathetic magic, to:

- J. Jastrow;
- F. B. Jevons;
- J. G. Frazer;
- R. R. Marett;
- **A. O. Lovejoy, *The Monist*, xvi, 357.**

This is an important reception form. Lovejoy has become part of an authority packet that licenses a broad textbook-level proposition about earliest philosophy/magic.

### What disappears in the compression

The local text does not preserve:

- Lovejoy's `vera causa` requirement;
- his source-by-source separation of observed custom, lexical occurrence, translation and abstract semantics;
- his warning that different regional packets have unequal resolution;
- the 005→1906 `reproof` move.

Instead, Lovejoy helps support the compressed proposition:

`mana / mysterious energy + sympathetic magic = primitive philosophy`.

This is exactly the kind of theory/evidence-packet assimilation that can explain later historiographical flattening.

### Critical authorship caution

The book was unfinished at James's death in August 1910. Its own prefatory note states that two corrected typescripts survived and that, following James's memorandum, **Dr. H. M. Kallen compared the two versions and largely prepared the book for the press**; R. B. Perry also assisted with divisions/headings.

Therefore the current evidence licenses:

> **The 1911 posthumous James/Kallen text cites Lovejoy 1906.**

It does **not** yet license:

> `William James personally inserted/cited Lovejoy in this footnote.`

The manuscript/typescript would have to be checked to assign the annotation authorially.

This distinction is especially important in this project because Lovejoy's Harvard relation to James is otherwise biographically tempting.

## 5. Marett 1909: the collected response visibly answers Wundt, not Lovejoy

Primary object:

R. R. Marett, *The Threshold of Religion* (London: Methuen, 1909), collecting essays including `Pre-Animistic Religion` and `The Conception of Mana`.

Open Library / IA: `cu31924014376150` / `thresholdofrelig00mareuoft`.

The 1909 preface explicitly responds to **Wundt's** criticism of Marett's pre-animistic hypothesis. Marett clarifies that he is not positing a system of abstract ideas temporally prior to animism; rather he is concerned with religion as a whole — thought, emotion and behavior — and with movement from indistinct to distinct / undifferentiated to differentiated.

In the searchable 1909 text checked in this pass, **no Lovejoy reference has been recovered**.

This is useful but negative evidence only. It does not prove:

- Marett never read Lovejoy;
- Marett deliberately ignored him;
- no citation occurs in a page/OCR location missed by the present search.

The safe statement is narrower:

> **Lovejoy is not presently visible as a named interlocutor in the searchable 1909 Marett response, whereas Wundt is.**

## 6. Jevons 1909: the British debate independently makes proof sufficiency the problem

Primary source:

F. B. Jevons, review of R. R. Marett, *The Threshold of Religion*, *Folk-Lore* 20 (1909), beginning p.235.

Wikisource page image/text controls include scan pages corresponding to printed pp.235 onward.

Jevons is sympathetic to Marett's constructive, non-dogmatic intervention. But he forces a distinction highly relevant to the Lovejoy project:

- one may formulate a conception of powers lacking soul/spirit/personality/will;
- this does not yet prove the historical existence of a **pre-animistic stage**.

The review asks what would follow if `simple straightforward personification` were included within animism and says that a pre-animistic stage would then require proof that early humans actually imagined/stood in awe of non-personal powers.

Edward Clodd's published response later in the same volume makes the evidentiary limit explicit: direct evidence for a prehistoric pre-animistic religion is, by the nature of the case, non-producible; his argument therefore relies on a working hypothesis and the doctrine of continuity, using low extant religions as residual evidence.

### Why this matters

This is **not** evidence of Lovejoy reception. No Lovejoy citation has been recovered in the checked pages.

It is a period control for the same proof-threshold distinction:

`conceptual possibility / present analogy`

≠

`demonstrated historical stage`.

The immediate British discussion of Marett therefore contains a parallel version of the problem Lovejoy's notebooks and 1906 paper handle elsewhere: **what stronger historical proposition is a given kind of evidence licensed to prove?**

This makes the absence of visible Lovejoy uptake more historically interesting, not less. The issue was live, but he was not necessarily the named center of the British debate.

## 7. Reception should be coded by component, not as a binary `influence`

The evidence now supports a four-component model of the 1906 paper:

1. **substantive category** — impersonal / communicable power;
2. **causal thesis** — regular, intelligible, quasi-mechanical manipulation / `primitive energetics`;
3. **evidentiary dossier** — cross-regional lexical, grammatical and customary cases;
4. **proof architecture** — independent existence-control (`vera causa`), proposition-relative source competence, escalating warrants, asymmetric regional resolution.

Early readers can receive these independently.

### Leuba

- category: YES;
- causal thesis: YES, then contested;
- dossier: YES, explicitly recommends Lovejoy as evidence summary;
- proof architecture: PARTIAL — enough survives for warrant re-audit.

### King

- category: YES;
- dossier: YES;
- direct Lovejoy relation: strongly indicated but exact primary locus pending;
- proof architecture: not yet coded until p.163/ch.VI direct collation.

### James/Kallen

- category: YES at textbook-compression level;
- dossier: Lovejoy becomes one authority within a broader packet;
- causal thesis: not separately reconstructed;
- proof architecture: NO visible survival locally.

### Marett/Jevons British node

- named Lovejoy uptake: none yet found;
- same proof problem: YES independently;
- reception status: NEGATIVE/PARALLEL CONTROL.

## 8. Historiographic payoff: the article's later flattening can now be explained as selective transmission

The tempting historiographic story would be:

`Lovejoy had a complex method → later scholars reduced it to mana/energy`.

That is too simple.

Leuba shows that an early reader could see the inferential claim and challenge its evidence. At the same time, the James/Kallen citation shows how quickly the same paper could become one bibliographic item inside a generic `primitive sympathetic magic` packet. The British Marett debate, meanwhile, could pursue an adjacent proof-threshold problem without visibly centering Lovejoy.

The better model is **differential transmission**:

`1906 article`

→ **category** travels easily (`power`, `mana`, `primitive energetics`)

→ **causal thesis** travels but can be contested (Leuba)

→ **evidence packet** travels as a reference/survey (Leuba; James/Kallen)

→ **proof architecture** travels least explicitly and is easiest to detach from the named theory.

This creates a plausible mechanism by which Lovejoy could remain cited while the research practice reconstructed from 004/005 becomes historiographically invisible.

## 9. Relation back to Batches 76–80

### Batch 76: `vera causa`

Lovejoy insists that the causal conception must be independently evidenced rather than inferred only from the customs it explains.

### Batch 77: proposition-relative reliability

A source can prove external fact or lexical use while failing to prove translation, abstract semantics or mechanism.

### Batch 78: asymmetric resolution

North-American and some Bantu controls provide finer grammatical/native evidence than other regional packets; universal synthesis is triangulation under unequal resolution.

### Batch 79: source-function migration

Sources persist, disappear or replace one another according to the proposition the argument currently needs to prove.

### Batch 80: reproof

Mechanisms isolated in 005 are selected for publication only after acquiring an independent linguistic/native warrant.

### Batch 81: reception disaggregation

Readers do not receive this package whole. They can preserve the category while losing the warrant architecture, preserve the causal proposition only to reject its evidentiary license, or retain the paper merely as a source in a synthetic bibliography.

Thus the new longitudinal formula is:

> **research decomposes claims; publication re-proves selected claims; reception decomposes the publication again.**

## 10. Evidence cautions

1. **Leuba is direct primary reception.** His 1912 book names Lovejoy and identifies *Monist* XVI, 357–382.
2. Leuba's criticism is direct evidence of a warrant dispute over Lovejoy's `regular/intelligible laws` claim; do not reduce it to a terminological disagreement.
3. King's direct citation to Lovejoy is presently controlled through Meylan and Leuba's adjacent treatment, not yet direct facsimile p.163; keep publication-grade quotation HOLD.
4. The 1911 *Some Problems of Philosophy* footnote is direct in the published object, but its authorial provenance is unresolved because H. M. Kallen substantially prepared the unfinished manuscript for press.
5. No Lovejoy reference has yet been found in the searchable Marett 1909 preface/text or the checked Jevons review pages. This is a negative search result, not evidence of deliberate omission.
6. Jevons/Clodd are methodological parallels, not demonstrated Lovejoy readers.
7. `reception disaggregation`, `differential transmission`, and the four-component reception model are our analytical formulations.
