## Core result: Oldenberg priority is now secure for the basic anti-Senart upādāna argument

The main uncertainty from batch 32 can now be narrowed substantially.

Senart in 1896 argued that `upādāna`, as the ninth nidāna, is merely a shortened designation for `upādāna-skandhas` / `pañca upādānaskandhas`, so the five skandhas recur in the chain in both collective and distributed form. This duplication supported his larger claim that the twelvefold series is a comparatively late composite construction in which originally independent categories were amalgamated without strict logical order.

By 1898, Jacobi explicitly directs readers who want the rebuttal of Senart's equation `upādāna = upādānakkhandha` to **Oldenberg, _Buddha_, 3rd ed., p.273 note**. Jacobi also states at the opening of his article that Oldenberg's objections relevant to the Sāṁkhya question were in the **third edition** of _Buddha_. Oldenberg's own 1898 `Buddhistische Studien` then independently cross-refers the relevant `nandi/chandarāga` citations back to **his _Buddha_³ p.273 note**.

This creates a two-witness control for the same earlier printed locus:

1. **Jacobi 1898 → Oldenberg, _Buddha_³, p.273 note** specifically for Senart's `upādāna = upādānakkhandha` claim;
2. **Oldenberg 1898 → his own _Buddha_³, p.273 note** for the Pāli evidence connecting `upādāna` with `nandi/chandarāga` directed toward `rūpa`, `vedanā`, etc.

Since Senart's paper is 1896 and Oldenberg's third edition is 1897, the basic semantic/textual anti-Senart move predates Lovejoy's JAOS article of 1898. The remaining issue is therefore **dependence/circulation**, not priority.

## 2. Senart 1896: stronger and more precise than the shorthand “the formula is meaningless”

The primary text makes it important not to caricature Senart.

### p.284: the historiographical alternative

Senart asks whether the nidāna nomenclature directly represents Buddhism's original, reflective and conscious theory of human existence, or whether it is a later construction that amalgamates originally independent categories without strict logical order. He accepts the second alternative.

### pp.284–285: the upādāna/skandha duplication

His pivotal move is that `upādāna` is only a reduction of `upādāna-skandhas` or, more fully, `pañca upādānaskandhas`. Because individual skandha terms then recur elsewhere in the series, the skandhas effectively appear twice. On this basis he treats the chain as a composite scholastic series rather than as a single deductively generated theory.

### pp.286–288: stratification

Senart separates layers:

- `taṇhā` and `upādāna`, with the following three terms, form a nucleus associated with the Four Truths;
- `avidyā` and `saṃskāras` are tied to a Sāṁkhya-Yoga background;
- the remainder includes a detailed repetition of skandhas already, on his interpretation, implied collectively in `upādāna`.

The historical operation is therefore **stratigraphic**: identify borrowed/pre-existing categories, variant formulae, repetitions and layers, then reconstruct their filiation.

### methodological consequence

Senart's position is not simply “philology versus philosophy.” His own method is analytical and genealogical. The sharper contrast with Lovejoy is that Senart lets compositional history and duplication place a strong limit on synchronic system-reconstruction, whereas Lovejoy tries to recover functional relations even in material he also regards as composite and historically mixed.

## 3. Jacobi 1898: genealogy plus constructive system-reconstruction

Jacobi begins by clarifying his 1896 thesis. Buddhism is not an `Abklatsch` or simple copy of a finished Sāṁkhya system. He argues instead that Buddha grew up amid Sāṁkhya ideas and transformed them individually; Sāṁkhya is a `Grundlage`, not a ready-made model.

This matters because Jacobi combines two operations that Senart tends to separate:

1. **genealogy:** Sāṁkhya ideas were broad intellectual common property and supplied a conceptual background;
2. **system reconstruction:** the nidāna chain can still be made causally intelligible from the standpoint of Indian philosophy.

His own treatment of `upādāna` is revealing. He retreats from his earlier equation `upādāna = adṛṣṭa`, saying more cautiously that adṛṣṭa is somehow contained or co-posited in upādāna. He then argues that a simple “grasping of the sensory world” risks collapsing into `sparśa`, while pleasure in the sensory world risks collapsing into `tṛṣṇā`; he therefore construes upādāna as passions/sinful dispositions that permit karma to take effect and contribute to new karma.

Crucially, in the footnote to this discussion he writes, in substance: **regarding Senart's view that `upādāna = upādānakkhandha`, see Oldenberg's discussion, _Buddha_³, p.273 note**. This is the decisive priority control.

## 4. Oldenberg 1898: canonical semantics and anti-genealogical restraint

Oldenberg's 1898 section VII answers Jacobi directly. His method differs from Jacobi's in two ways.

First, he insists that the Buddhist `upādāna` must be established from Buddhist usage rather than reconstructed from the technical philosophical sense `upādāna = causa materialis`. He starts from Sanskrit `upādatte` / Pāli `upādiyati`: to seize, appropriate, take hold of. Upādāna is accordingly grasping/holding an object one wishes to possess or enjoy, or possibly the corresponding subjective condition.

Second, Oldenberg reconstructs the local sequence:

`phassa → vedanā → taṇhā → upādāna`

as contact with an external object → pleasant/painful feeling → desire → grasping/appropriation. He explicitly rejects Jacobi's claim that this would collapse upādāna into `phassa`; they are different stages. He concedes that if upādāna is rendered through the subjective `nandi/chandarāga`, its boundary with `taṇhā` becomes harder, but the texts distinguish them.

The important self-cross-reference occurs here: where `chandarāga` is directed toward `rūpa`, `vedanā`, etc., Oldenberg tells the reader to see the citations in **his _Buddha_³, p.273 note**. This independently corroborates Jacobi's footnote and fixes the 1897 priority.

Oldenberg then drives the argument away from Jacobi's `adṛṣṭa/karma` parallel: the attested Buddhist semantics place upādāna far from adṛṣṭa. He permits the possibility of some older Sāṁkhya influence on Buddhism, but broad “family resemblance” across Indian philosophies is insufficient evidence for the specific genealogy Jacobi wants.

A further nuance matters. Oldenberg's p.692 note suggests that the canonical/scholastic fourfold classification (`kāmūpādāna`, `diṭṭhūpādāna`, `sīlabbatūpādāna`, `attavādūpādāna`) may itself be a later scholastic intrusion into the interpretation of this nidāna. His reconstruction is therefore semantically conservative rather than a maximal harmonization of all Buddhist taxonomies.

## 5. Lovejoy 004 and 1898 after the priority correction

The archival notebook already shows Lovejoy working directly through the same disputed terrain: the relation of khandhas to dependent origination, Senart's `upādāna` claim, the four types of attachment, `upādāna/upādhi`, and the relation of karma to rebirth. The crucial notebook move is that **historical compositeness does not by itself make the series unintelligible**: Lovejoy accepts that terms may be inherited or heterogeneous while still seeking a technical ordering among them.

His published 1898 article then attacks Senart's `upādāna = upādānakkhandha` reading with Pāli textual distinctions between the aggregates and attachment to them. That basic anti-Senart semantic distinction is now known to have an Oldenberg precedent in 1897.

But the published paper contains a more specific constructive allocation that remains the strongest Lovejoy-specific candidate:

- **karma** explains the **particular condition/form** in which rebirth occurs;
- **upādāna** explains the **fact of rebirth as such**;
- `taṇhā` and `upādāna` are distinguished not merely lexically but by causal-functional role, with particular cravings feeding a more settled condition of attachment/will.

This is a stronger and narrower novelty claim than “Lovejoy restored coherence to a composite formula.” It should now be tested specifically against Oldenberg, not inferred from the mere fact that Lovejoy opposed Senart.

## 6. Citation asymmetry in Lovejoy 1898: priority secure, dependence unresolved

Lovejoy demonstrably knew and cited Oldenberg. In the second half of the 1898 JAOS paper, on `upādisesa`, he explicitly praises Oldenberg's appended discussion in _Buddha_ (English translation pagination) and repeatedly uses Oldenberg's textual argument.

In the first half, however, where Lovejoy directly refutes Senart on `upādāna`, the published presentation does **not** visibly cite Oldenberg's third-edition p.273 note. The asymmetry is real and should be preserved as evidence rather than resolved by speculation.

At present the admissible statements are:

- **priority:** Oldenberg 1897 is earlier for the basic anti-Senart `upādāna ≠ upādānakkhandha` argument;
- **availability:** the argument was in print before Lovejoy's 1898 paper;
- **dependence:** unestablished;
- **independent convergence:** possible but unproved;
- **unacknowledged dependence:** also possible but unproved.

A useful next source question is therefore whether Lovejoy had access to or read the 1897 third German edition, as opposed to the older English translation he explicitly cites elsewhere.

## 7. Four-way methodological map

### Senart
**Stratigraphic genealogy.** Variant formulae, repetitions, borrowed categories and lexical filiation expose successive historical layers. Historical compositeness limits claims for an originally autonomous coherent system.

### Jacobi
**Genealogical reconstruction through Indian philosophy.** Broad Sāṁkhya conceptual background plus causal reconstruction; apparent inconsistencies can result from inherited categories transferred into a new system.

### Oldenberg
**Canonical semantic control + restrained genealogy.** Establish word use from Pāli/Buddhist sources, reconstruct local sequence, resist speculative Sāṁkhya derivations not demanded by the texts.

### Lovejoy
**Functional reconstruction within composite inheritance.** Common provenance, lexical overlap, or repeated components do not establish conceptual identity. Ask what causal/semantic work a component performs in the configuration.

This four-way map is historically more useful than a simple Senart-versus-Lovejoy opposition.

## 8. Refined history-of-ideas payoff

The strongest continuity from 004 toward Lovejoy's later analytical practice should now be expressed as a rule of inference rather than as an early “unit-idea” doctrine:

> **Genealogical relation and synchronic function are separate questions. A concept can be borrowed, repeated, or historically composite while acquiring a distinct role inside a new configuration.**

In the 1898 Buddhist case, the operative tests are:

- lexical similarity does not prove identity;
- shared origin does not prove same function;
- repeated material does not by itself prove redundancy;
- historical layering does not by itself prove synchronic unintelligibility;
- a reconstruction succeeds only if it assigns different terms non-redundant explanatory work.

This is the cleanest bridge currently visible from the Orientalist notebook/article cluster toward the later Lovejoy problem of tracing inherited conceptual materials through altered configurations, without projecting mature “unit-idea” vocabulary backward.
