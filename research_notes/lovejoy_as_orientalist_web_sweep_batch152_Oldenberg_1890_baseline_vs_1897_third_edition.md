# Lovejoy as Orientalist / comparative religion — Batch 152

Date: 2026-08-19  
Status: synced  
Scope: use the uploaded **1890 second edition** of Oldenberg's _Buddha_ as a full-text baseline against the **1897 third-edition passages quoted or cited by Jacobi in 1898**. The immediate question is whether the third edition represents a conceptual reversal, or a controversy-specific expansion of an evidentiary position already present in the 1890 book.

## Core result

The 1890 second edition materially changes how the still-missing 1897 third-edition pages should be read.

Oldenberg was **already** doing two things before the Jacobi/Senart controversy:

1. acknowledging specific structural correspondences between Buddhist and Sāṁkhya/Yoga categories;
2. refusing to let those correspondences establish direct derivation or borrowing without stronger historical evidence.

The second edition therefore does not support a simple chronology in which Oldenberg only became anti-genealogical after Jacobi. At printed p.100 n.1 he says that real `Anklänge` between Sāṁkhya and Buddhist doctrine do exist, but seem to go no further than the familiar **family resemblance** among Indian philosophical systems. At p.226 n.2 he notes the striking fourfold Sāṁkhya/Yoga schema of liberation and explicitly says it cannot be established whether Buddhism borrowed this fourfold arrangement. At pp.262–266 he then develops detailed structural parallels around `avidyā/aviveka`, beginningless causal chains, and `saṃskāra/vāsanā`.

Most importantly, the second edition's printed p.268 already contains almost the same `saṅkhāra` paragraph that Jacobi attacks when citing the **third edition p.286f** in 1898. Oldenberg already describes saṅkhāras as the inner shaping of mind that determines rebirth and carries the being into a new existence. Thus at least one important Jacobi–Oldenberg disagreement in 1898 is **not generated by the new third-edition appendix**; Jacobi is attacking a pre-existing explanatory passage that has simply shifted forward in pagination in the enlarged edition.

By contrast, the controversy-specific 1897 appendix at pp.443–455 appears genuinely new. The 1890 second edition has no corresponding terminal Sāṁkhya appendix; its index locates Sāṁkhya discussion only in scattered main-text notes/pages (100 n.1; 226; 262 n.1; 264 n.2; 266 n.1). Jacobi 1898 quotes the 1897 appendix at pp.448, 450–452 as a direct reply to his own Sāṁkhya genealogy and source use.

The strongest current edition-history formulation is therefore:

> **1890: structural resemblance admitted, borrowing underdetermined → 1896 Jacobi presses a stronger Sāṁkhya genealogy → 1897 Oldenberg adds a dedicated polemical/source-critical appendix without abandoning the earlier evidentiary rule → 1898 Jacobi answers both the old main-text interpretation and the new appendix.**

This makes the third edition an **expansion and polemical consolidation**, not on present evidence a reversal of Oldenberg's basic inferential stance.

No new GLA/GAL/COV identifier is created in this batch.

## 1. Oldenberg 1890 p.100 n.1: explicit `family resemblance` baseline

The uploaded 1890 second edition gives a particularly clean baseline in the note attached to the discussion of `Kapilavatthu`.

Oldenberg rejects the strong claim that Buddhism derives from Sāṁkhya. After invoking Max Müller's failure to find definite similarities between Kapila's known system and Buddhist Abhidharma, he adds that real echoes/resemblances (`Anklänge`) do exist, but in his view they do not go farther than what is explained by the familiar:

> `Familienähnlichkeit aller philosophischen Systeme Indiens`.

He further says that using the Maitrāyaṇīya Upaniṣad as an older precursor of Sāṁkhya does not appear to change the result.

This is already the basic evidentiary rule later visible in the controversy:

> **resemblance is real evidence of a shared intellectual field, but resemblance alone does not warrant a direct genealogy.**

The 1897 appendix should therefore be read as an argument over the threshold for genealogical inference, not as the origin of that distinction in Oldenberg.

---

## 2. Oldenberg 1890 p.226 n.2: correspondence admitted, direction of borrowing left unresolved

Discussing the Four Noble Truths, Oldenberg notes that Sāṁkhya and Yoga likewise distinguish four sections in the theory of liberation:

- what one must be liberated from;
- liberation;
- the cause of bondage/suffering;
- the means of liberation.

He compares this with the medical fourfold structure disease / health / cause of disease / cure.

But his historical inference is deliberately limited. He says that whether Buddhism is the borrowing party in this fourfold articulation **cannot be established**, while the specifically Buddhist formulation of the Four Truths appears unmistakably its own.

Again, the structure is:

`formal/structural parallel`  
≠  
`demonstrated direction of transmission`.

This is a pre-1897 control directly relevant to the project's broader provenance/function distinction.

---

## 3. Oldenberg 1890 pp.262–266: detailed Sāṁkhya parallels already inside dependent origination

The second edition is not merely dismissive of Sāṁkhya parallels.

### p.262 n.1 — `avidyā / aviveka`

Oldenberg says the older Indian category of ignorance is adapted to each later philosophical system. In Sāṁkhya, ignorance appears as `aviveka`, the failure to distinguish soul and matter, causing their suffering-producing conjunction.

### p.264 n.2 — beginningless causal chain

He explicitly compares the Buddhist causal structure to a Sāṁkhya beginningless chain likened to seed and plant. Non-discrimination stamps the inner organ with a disposition (`vāsanā`), and that disposition generates the same non-discrimination in a later existence.

### p.266 n.1 — possible preservation of an older doctrinal configuration

This note is especially important. Oldenberg asks whether an authentic/older form of the doctrine, or something close to it, might have been **preserved in Sāṁkhya**. He then describes Sāṁkhya `saṃskāra/vāsanā` as impressions produced by ignorance/non-discrimination that survive cosmological dissolution and function as seeds of renewed bondage.

This is not a scholar refusing all historical relation. It is a scholar willing to consider preservation/common antecedents while refusing an easy one-way derivation.

## 4. Oldenberg 1890 p.268 already contains the `saṅkhāra` passage Jacobi attacks at Buddha³ p.286f

This is the most concrete edition-control result.

The 1890 second edition p.268 says, in substance and very nearly in the wording Jacobi later quotes, that the `saṅkhāras` which decide human rebirth are the **inner shaping of the mind**. This shaping can aim at earthly status, rise toward divine worlds, or, for the truly knowing person, turn toward the cessation of all shaping. Oldenberg ends the sequence with an image of fuel sustaining flame: the being's inner self-shaping holds dying existence to renewed life, after which the mind puts on a new `name-and-corporeality` garment.

Jacobi 1898 explicitly cites the **third edition p.286f** for essentially this same claim and objects that Oldenberg has smuggled in a mind that must precede `viññāṇa` in the nidāna chain. Jacobi's complaint is that the act of shaping presupposes a bearer/mental substrate which the Buddhist chain has supposedly omitted.

The pagination shift is exactly what an enlarged third edition would produce:

- 1890 second ed.: relevant paragraph **p.268**;
- 1897 third ed.: Jacobi cites **p.286f**.

The substantive continuity is strong enough that we should not attribute this explanatory construction to the 1897 controversy-specific addition.

## 5. What appears genuinely third-edition-specific: the terminal Sāṁkhya appendix

The 1890 second edition has no terminal appendix corresponding to third-edition **pp.443–455**. Its index entry `Sankhya-Philosophie` points only to:

- p.100 n.1;
- p.226;
- p.262 n.1;
- p.264 n.2;
- p.266 n.1.

Jacobi 1898, by contrast, begins by pointing readers to Oldenberg _Buddha_, **3rd ed. p.448ff**, and treats pp.448–452 as a direct counter-argument to his own 1896 position.

From Jacobi's quotations/summary, the new appendix contains at least these claims:

- p.448: Oldenberg does **not** deny that the Sāṁkhya concepts around `avidyā/saṃskāra` are closely related to Buddhist ones, but denies that Sāṁkhya constructs the same developmental sequence from ignorance to saṃskāras to further products;
- p.450: Oldenberg characterizes the stronger dependence thesis as making Buddhism an `Abklatsch` of Sāṁkhya, a characterization Jacobi rejects;
- pp.451–452: Oldenberg attacks Jacobi's use of Aśvaghoṣa's account of Arāḍa as evidence for an early Sāṁkhya state, emphasizing the absence of this doctrinal account from the Buddhist canon and the poetic/source-critical problem.

These are controversy-specific extensions of the earlier rule: the parallels can be admitted while the historical warrant for direct derivation is challenged at the level of source chronology, textual witness, and diagnostic specificity.

The exact German pages remain to be recovered before quoting Oldenberg directly.

## 6. Jacobi 1898 is answering two Oldenbergs at once

This edition control makes Jacobi's article structurally clearer.

### A. Jacobi answers a pre-existing main-text Oldenberg

His criticism of Oldenberg's explanation of `saṃskāra` (third ed. p.286f) is directed at wording already substantially present in the 1890 second edition p.268.

Likewise, his challenge to Oldenberg's broader semantic interpretation of the nidāna sequence belongs to a long-standing disagreement over what causal role these categories perform.

### B. Jacobi answers the new 1897 polemical appendix

His response to `Abklatsch`, to the precise relation of Sāṁkhya categories, and especially to the Aśvaghoṣa/Arāḍa source problem is an answer to the newly enlarged controversy state of Oldenberg's book.

This prevents us from treating all citations to `Buddha³` as equally new.

## 7. Consequence for Lovejoy 004 / 1898

The 004 problem can now be framed against a more accurate moving field.

Oldenberg's evidence regime was already visible by 1890:

- acknowledge structural resemblance;
- distinguish resemblance from proof of borrowing;
- allow possible common/older configurations;
- insist that direction of historical transmission may remain undecidable.

The 1897 third edition then appears to sharpen the **source-critical burden** in direct response to Jacobi's stronger genealogy.

Lovejoy's 004 and 1898 article should therefore not be positioned against a static `Oldenberg position`. They belong to a controversy in which Oldenberg himself was moving from dispersed comparative cautions in the main text toward a dedicated polemical appendix specifying why a proposed genealogy failed to meet the evidentiary threshold.

That yields a stronger comparative question:

> **What, if anything, does Lovejoy add to an inferential discipline already articulated by Oldenberg as `family resemblance ≠ demonstrated filiation`?**

The current answer remains downstream and configurational: Lovejoy recomposes this discipline across Sanskrit/Pāli lexical genealogy, causal role, textual distribution, and later conceptual-history problems, rather than inventing the distinction.
