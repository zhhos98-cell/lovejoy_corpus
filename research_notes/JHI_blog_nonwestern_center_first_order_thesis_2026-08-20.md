# JHI Blog — non-Western center + first-order thesis

Date: 2026-08-20
Status: pre-pitch conceptual reframing

## Problem

A Lovejoy-rehabilitation pitch is not enough. `Lovejoy needs reassessment`, `Skinner/Lovejoy deserves revisiting`, and `the history of ideas needs its own history` are all already familiar moves.

The sharper intervention is geographical and historiographical: **one early site in which Lovejoy confronted intellectual inheritance, borrowing, system-formation and philosophical originality was Indian philosophy, not the later Western canon with which the history of ideas became identified.**

The Blog therefore needs two distinct levels:

1. **entry into an ongoing general intellectual-history genealogy:** debates over the geography/genealogy of intellectual history, global or non-Western intellectual history, and the provincialization of methods later narrated as internal Western disciplinary developments;
2. **a first-order thesis defensible from the 1890s material:** Lovejoy's Buddhist intervention treated historical compositeness and philosophical originality as compatible.

## First-order thesis

Best version:

> **In his 1898 Buddhist intervention, Lovejoy argued that the historical distinctiveness of a philosophical tradition did not depend on doctrinal purity: its terms, `perhaps even the ideas`, could be borrowed while their `arrangement and application` remained `original and characteristic`.**

This is not a methodological abstraction. It is a proposition Lovejoy explicitly defended against Senart's interpretation of the twelve nidānas as a composite series in an `ordre plus ou moins accidentel`.

Notebook 004 makes the argumentative route visible:

- concede inherited/pre-existing materials;
- concede heterogeneity and repetition;
- reject the inference that the resulting Buddhist formula is therefore accidental or unintelligible;
- in print, formulate the positive claim that borrowed terms/ideas can acquire an original arrangement/application.

Warren is supporting evidence at the term level: `taṇhā` and `upādāna` need not be identical even when closely related. `upādisesa` is a coda showing the textual control by which Lovejoy tries to establish local Buddhist meaning.

## Non-Western payoff

The larger intervention should not be `Lovejoy was more contextual than we thought`.

It is:

> **The problem of intellectual inheritance that later became central to the history of ideas was, for the young Lovejoy, already being worked through Indian philosophical traditions. Buddhism was not a decorative non-Western example appended to a method formed elsewhere; it was one of the concrete historical fields in which questions of borrowing, recombination and philosophical individuality became unavoidable.**

This does **not** establish that India `caused` Lovejoy's mature method, nor that Indian scholars transmitted a method to him. It establishes a geography of early practice: a later founder of the history of ideas was doing technically serious historical-philosophical work on Buddhism/Sāṃkhya before the discipline hardened around a Western canonical self-description.

## Strongest general-intellectual-history entry

The ongoing conversation is not merely Lovejoy rehabilitation. It is the question of where the methods and objects of intellectual history are historically located.

Possible frame:

> Recent work has asked how far intellectual history's canonical methodological stories can be provincialized or globalized. Lovejoy's Buddhist notebook offers a different kind of evidence for that discussion: not an alternative theory of contextualism, but a concrete archive showing that one future architect of the history of ideas entered problems of intellectual inheritance through nineteenth-century debates over Indian philosophy.

Keep this framing short. Do not turn the piece into a review of global intellectual history.

## Indian scholars: Bankim and Mitra

Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay and Rajendralal Mitra should matter for field geography, not as alleged Lovejoy sources.

- Bankim 1871 separates similarity from identity and borrowing-direction in a Buddhism/Sāṃkhya discussion.
- Mitra 1883 distinguishes pre-existing materials from the completed Sāṃkhya system and rejects weak genealogical proofs.
- No evidence Lovejoy read either.

Their role is therefore:

> **The intellectual problem was not simply produced in Europe and applied to Indian material. Indian scholars were themselves arguing over similarity, inheritance, system-formation and historical dependence.**

Use one compact paragraph only. Avoid claiming direct transmission.

## Best two-sentence thesis architecture

Sentence 1 — first-order:

> **Against readings that made Buddhism's composite materials evidence of derivativeness, Lovejoy argued that borrowed terms — `perhaps even the ideas` — could be reorganized into an `original and characteristic` philosophical arrangement.**

Sentence 2 — historiographical payoff:

> **The significance of that argument is geographical as much as methodological: one of Lovejoy's earliest sustained encounters with intellectual inheritance took place in debates over Indian philosophy, complicating the later disciplinary story in which the history of ideas appears to emerge from an overwhelmingly Western canon.**

The first sentence is what the article proves. The second states why JHI Blog readers should care.

## Pitch language

Avoid leading with:

- `reassessing Lovejoy`;
- `rethinking Skinner`;
- `historical warrant`;
- `levels of inference`;
- `provenance/configuration`;
- `relation-type discrimination`.

Lead with:

- the notebook `Sankhya + Buddhism`;
- the actor-recognized nidāna / Sāṃkhya-Buddhism controversy;
- Lovejoy's explicit borrowed-material / original-arrangement claim;
- then the non-Western significance for the genealogy/geography of intellectual history.

2. **What Was Arthur Lovejoy Doing with Buddhism?**
3. **Before the History of Ideas: Lovejoy, Buddhism, and Intellectual Inheritance**

Title 1 makes the first-order thesis visible; title 2 remains the strongest Blog hook.

## Bottom line

The Blog should not defend the generic proposition that Lovejoy deserves reevaluation. It should defend a substantive historical claim about Buddhism and originality, then use that claim to relocate one early site of history-of-ideas practice outside the Western canon.

Compressed formulation:

> **Lovejoy's early Buddhism scholarship treated borrowed intellectual materials as compatible with philosophical originality; recovering that argument relocates one formative site of the later history-of-ideas problem of inheritance into nineteenth-century debates over Indian philosophy.**

Use `formative site` cautiously; if causality feels too strong in prose, replace with `early site`.
